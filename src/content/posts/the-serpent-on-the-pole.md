## What Happened

Near the end of the forty years, the people spoke against Elohim and against Moses and called the manna "this light bread" (Numbers 21:5). Yahuah sent fiery serpents among them, and many died. The people came to Moses and confessed, "We have sinned" (21:7). Yahuah did not take the serpents away. He gave a remedy. Moses made a serpent of bronze and set it on a pole, and anyone bitten who "beheld the serpent of brass" lived (21:9).

## The Burning Ones

The serpents of 21:6 are *nechashim seraphim*, "burning serpents," named for the fire of their bite. When Yahuah gives the remedy, He uses only the second word: "Make thee a *saraph*" (21:8). It is the same word Isaiah uses for the *seraphim* standing above the throne (Isaiah 6:2), and Isaiah also speaks of a "fiery flying serpent," a *saraph* (Isaiah 14:29; 30:6). The Scriptures share the word without saying more about how the two are linked, and the study goes no further than the text does. Moses then made what was commanded: a *nachash nechoshet*, a serpent of bronze (21:9).

## The Lifting, Not the Serpent

Yahushua drew the line Himself: "as Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, even so must the Son of man be lifted up" (John 3:14). The comparison is the verb. He does not say He is like the serpent; He says He must be lifted up as the serpent was. John explains what "lifted up" meant: "This he said, signifying what death he should die" (John 12:32–33). The pole in the wilderness and the tree outside Jerusalem are the point of contact, not the serpent and the Son.

## The Curse Judged

So what did the serpent picture? Not Messiah, but what was killing the people. The thing that bit them was lifted up, held still, and made harmless where all could see it. Paul gives the meaning: Yahuah "made him to be sin for us, who knew no sin" (2 Corinthians 5:21), and Messiah "redeemed us from the curse of the law, being made a curse for us: for it is written, Cursed is every one that hangeth on a tree" (Galatians 3:13). Sin and the curse were nailed up and judged in the one who carried them. The serpent stays a picture of evil the whole way through. On the pole it is defeated, not honored.

The giver of the remedy is named in the same passage: the Father "gave his only begotten Son" (John 3:16). In the wilderness Yahuah gave the remedy and Moses raised it. At the tree the Father gave the Son, and the Son was lifted up.

## One Look

No one was healed by touching the pole, carrying it, or making a copy. The bitten man looked and lived. Yahushua made the same point: "whosoever believeth in him should not perish" (John 3:15). The look was belief, and the life came from Yahuah.

## Nehushtan

Israel kept the bronze serpent for centuries, and in time "the children of Israel did burn incense to it." Hezekiah broke it in pieces and called it *Nehushtan*, a piece of bronze (2 Kings 18:4). The remedy Yahuah gave once had become an object of worship. A serpent image kept as something holy repeats the same mistake, whatever the reason given for it. The bronze serpent did its work on one day in the wilderness, and Yahuah never asked His people to keep it, display it, or honor it. Anything that takes His place, even something He once used, is a piece of bronze.

## Revelation

Revelation names the serpent behind every bite: "that old serpent, called the Devil," cast out (Revelation 12:9). His defeat comes the way the healing came in the wilderness, by what was lifted up: the saints "overcame him by the blood of the Lamb" (Revelation 12:11). In the end the serpent is bound and cast into the lake of fire (Revelation 20:2, 10), and in the city "there shall be no more curse" (Revelation 22:3).
