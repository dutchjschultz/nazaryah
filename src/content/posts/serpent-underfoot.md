The yellow Gadsden flag shows a coiled rattlesnake above the words "Don’t tread on me." Its history is told in [the American Idolatry article of the same name](/american-idolatry/dont-tread-on-me). This study sets the flag beside Scripture and asks one question: can a household that belongs to Yahuah hang the serpent’s words over its door?

## The Serpent in Scripture

From the first book to the last, the serpent is never a hero. In Eden it was "more subtil than any beast of the field," the deceiver who drew Eve away from the word of Yahuah (Genesis 3:1). Paul feared that the same serpent would beguile the believers as it beguiled Eve (2 Corinthians 11:3). At the end of the book John names it without disguise: "that old serpent, called the Devil, and Satan, which deceiveth the whole world" (Revelation 12:9).

Yahushua used the creature the same way. He called the scribes and Pharisees "ye serpents, ye generation of vipers" (Matthew 23:33), the same words John the Immerser had used (Matthew 3:7). In Scripture the serpent is the emblem of the adversary and of those who serve him.

## The Promise: The Serpent Will Be Trodden Down

The very first promise of deliverance is a promise about treading on the serpent. Yahuah told it that the Seed of the woman "shall bruise thy head" (Genesis 3:15). The Seed is Messiah (Galatians 3:16), and the bruising of the serpent’s head runs straight through to the assembly: "Yahuah of peace shall bruise Satan under your feet shortly" (Romans 16:20).

The same picture runs through both covenants. The psalmist said of the one who dwells in the secret place of the Most High, "the young lion and the dragon shalt thou trample under feet" (Psalm 91:13). Yahushua told the seventy, "I give unto you power to tread on serpents and scorpions" (Luke 10:19).

**Scripture says to the serpent, "You will be trodden down." The flag has the serpent answer, "Don’t tread on me."**

Those two sentences cannot hang on the same wall. One is Yahuah’s promise. The other is the serpent’s reply.

## The Brass Serpent That Became an Idol

Some will answer that Yahuah Himself once lifted up a serpent. He did. When fiery serpents struck Israel in the wilderness, Yahuah told Moses to make a serpent of brass and set it on a pole, and all who looked on it lived (Numbers 21:8-9). Yahushua named it as a picture of His own lifting up on the tree (John 3:14-15).

But the story does not end there. Israel kept that brass serpent for some seven hundred years and began to burn incense to it. Hezekiah broke it in pieces and called it *Nehushtan*, "a thing of brass" (2 Kings 18:4). A serpent Yahuah Himself commanded became an idol the moment the people honored the object instead of the One who healed them. Paul warned that these things were written as examples, so that believers would not become idolaters as some of them were (1 Corinthians 10:6-7).

If Yahuah’s own serpent had to be broken once it was revered, a serpent chosen by a Deist for a political cartoon has no better claim on a believer’s porch.

## The Spirit of the Motto

The flag’s rattlesnake, in Franklin’s words, never begins a fight but never surrenders. Its message is retaliation: touch me and I strike. The Torah already forbade that spirit: "Thou shalt not avenge, nor bear any grudge" (Leviticus 19:18). Yahuah keeps vengeance for Himself — "To me belongeth vengeance" (Deuteronomy 32:35) — and Paul repeats it to the assembly: "Vengeance is mine; I will repay" (Romans 12:19).

Yahushua lived the opposite of the coiled snake. He taught His disciples to turn the other cheek (Matthew 5:39). Isaiah saw Him "brought as a lamb to the slaughter" (Isaiah 53:7), and Philip read that very passage to the Ethiopian (Acts 8:32). When He was reviled, "he reviled not again; when he suffered, he threatened not" (1 Peter 2:23).

**Messiah went to the tree as a lamb, not as a rattlesnake.**

## The Believer’s Banner

Israel already had a banner, and it was not a creature. After the battle with Amalek, Moses built an altar and called it *Yahuah-nissi*, "Yahuah is my banner" (Exodus 17:15). Isaiah foretold a root of Jesse that would stand "for an ensign of the people" (Isaiah 11:10), and Paul applied that very verse to Messiah (Romans 15:12). The believer’s banner is the Name of Yahuah and the Messiah He lifted up, who draws all men to Himself (John 12:32).

A household under that banner has no need of the serpent’s. To fly the yellow flag is to fly the serpent’s own words; to belong to Messiah is to stand where Scripture puts the serpent — underfoot.

## The Serpent’s End

John saw how the serpent’s story ends. An angel came down from heaven and "laid hold on the dragon, that old serpent, which is the Devil, and Satan, and bound him" (Revelation 20:2), and at the last he is cast into the lake of fire (Revelation 20:10). The promise of Genesis 3:15 is kept in full. The serpent asked not to be trodden on. It will be.

*"And Yahuah of peace shall bruise Satan under your feet shortly" (Romans 16:20).*
