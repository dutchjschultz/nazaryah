## What Yahuah Is Doing

Yahuah means to be known. Not guessed at and not argued over, but known, by every nation on the earth.

His way of doing that has never changed. He puts somebody in place to carry it, and He sends men to say so out loud.

Isaiah is the most quoted prophet in the pulpit and one of the least read. Verses get pulled out one at a time, printed on cards and sung, and the chapter they came from never gets opened.

## How to Use This

Each entry gives the common teaching first and the verse second.

The point is not that these verses have nothing to do with the Messiah. Several of them are quoted by the apostles and by Yahushua Himself. The point is that a verse pulled out of its place loses the argument it was built to carry.

## Isaiah 1:18 — “Though your sins be as scarlet”

**Taught as:** A promise of forgiveness with nothing asked in return.

**What it says:** Verses 16 and 17 come first. Wash. Put away the evil. Stop doing wrong. Learn to do right. Help the oppressed and the fatherless. The offer in verse 18 comes attached to a turn, and the card prints the offer without it.

## Isaiah 2:4 — Swords into plowshares

**Taught as:** A slogan for world peace. It is carved on a wall across from the United Nations.

**What it says:** The whole passage runs on one condition, stated one verse earlier: the nations come up to be taught His ways and to walk in His paths. The weapons go down because the law goes out. Take the law out and the verse has no engine.

## Isaiah 6:3 — “Holy, holy, holy”

**Taught as:** Three persons, one holy each.

**What it says:** Hebrew has no word for very. It says a thing twice to make it strong and three times to say the most it can say. Jeremiah says O earth, earth, earth (22:29) about one planet. Ezekiel says I will overturn, overturn, overturn (21:27) about one act. If three times counted persons, Jeremiah just gave the world three earths.

## Isaiah 6:8 — “Who will go for us?”

**Taught as:** A conversation inside one being.

**What it says:** Isaiah is standing in a throne room he just described, and it is not empty. Seraphim are above the throne (6:2). Micaiah describes the same room: Yahuah on His throne with all the host of heaven standing on His right hand and His left (1 Kings 22:19). A king in a full court says who will go for us.

## Isaiah 7:14 — “A virgin shall conceive”

**Taught as:** A Christmas card verse with no setting.

**What it says:** It is a sign given to a frightened king named Ahaz while two armies march on Jerusalem, and it comes with a clock: before the child knows to refuse evil and choose good, both of those kings will be gone (7:16). Matthew quotes it, and rightly. But a reader handed half a verse has no idea a king, two armies, and a deadline are attached to it.

## Isaiah 9:6 — “The everlasting Father”

**Taught as:** Proof that the Son is one person of a triune God.

**What it says:** Read the whole list of names. One of them is Father. If a title turns the holder into the person named, this verse just erased the difference the doctrine depends on. The Hebrew is avi-ad, father of the age — an office, the way a king is called the father of his people.

## Isaiah 14:12 — “Lucifer”

**Taught as:** The devil's proper name, and his fall before creation.

**What it says:** Verse 4 says who is being spoken to: take up this proverb against the king of Babylon. The Hebrew word is heylel, a shining one, a morning star. It is not a name. Verse 16 has onlookers staring and asking, is this the man? It is a taunt song aimed at a human king.

## Isaiah 40:3 — “The voice crying in the wilderness”

**Taught as:** A prophecy about John and nothing else.

**What it says:** Verse 2 comes first: her warfare is accomplished, her iniquity is pardoned. The road being built is the road home from a finished captivity, and a few verses later the flock is led at the pace of the ewes with young (40:11). Malachi picked up the same words and aimed them at a coming messenger (3:1), and the Gospel writers followed him. Keep all three steps and the verse gets deeper. Keep the last one only and the chapter disappears.

## Isaiah 45:7 — “I create evil”

**Taught as:** Either a strike against the goodness of Yahuah, or a defense of it, depending on who is arguing.

**What it says:** The verse is built as a pair of opposites: light against darkness, peace against this word. The opposite of peace is trouble, not wickedness. He is claiming the disasters He sends, which is the whole point of these chapters — there is no second power running them.

## Isaiah 53:5 — “With his stripes we are healed”

**Taught as:** A guarantee of physical healing on demand.

**What it says:** The same sentence names what is being healed: wounded for our transgressions, bruised for our iniquities. Verse 6 spells out the sickness — all we like sheep have gone astray.

## Isaiah 55:8 — “My thoughts are not your thoughts”

**Taught as:** A conversation stopper, used to shut down a question nobody wants to answer.

**What it says:** Verse 7 is the sentence it finishes: let the wicked forsake his way, and the unrighteous man his thoughts, and let him return. The contrast is a wicked man's thinking against Yahuah's, and what is being offered is repentance. It is an invitation, not a wall.

## Isaiah 61:1–2 — “The acceptable year”

**Taught as:** One unified prophecy of the first coming.

**What it says:** Yahushua read it out loud at Nazareth and stopped in the middle of the sentence, closing the book before the day of vengeance (Luke 4:18–20). He divided the verse Himself. Half had come. Half had not.

## Isaiah 64:6 — “All our righteousnesses are as filthy rags”

**Taught as:** Proof that obedience is worthless and trying at it offends Yahuah.

**What it says:** It is a confession. A people in rebellion describing themselves — we are all as an unclean thing, we all fade as a leaf, there is none that calleth on thy name (64:6–7). It says what that generation was, not what the commandments are. Two chapters later the same book has all flesh keeping His days.

## Isaiah 65:17–25 — The new heavens and new earth

**Taught as:** A thousand-year kingdom on this present earth.

**What it says:** The passage opens with a new heavens and a new earth and the former ones not remembered. That is the end, not a stage before it. Chapter 66 repeats the same words and lands on all flesh worshipping from new moon to new moon and Sabbath to Sabbath (66:22–23).

## Isaiah 66:23 — New moons and Sabbaths in the new earth

**Taught as:** Not taught. Skipped.

**What it says:** This is the last picture of worship the book gives, and it runs on a calendar. Anyone teaching that the appointed times ended in the middle of the book has to explain why they are still being kept on the final page.

## Where It Lands

Isaiah does not trail off. The order to come out of Babylon is repeated word for word in Revelation 18:4. Death swallowed up in chapter 25 turns up again as every tear wiped away. The new heavens and new earth of chapters 65 and 66 are the new heaven and new earth of Revelation 21.

The book hands the reader forward, and the last book picks up the same sentence and finishes it.

## In One Sentence

Isaiah is only confusing to a reader who is mining it.
