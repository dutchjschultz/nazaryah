## What Came Into the Church

Many churches and Messianic congregations in America now light a nine-branch Hanukkah lamp in winter, and many men in those congregations wear the kippah, the small skullcap, during worship. Both are taken as signs of returning to the Hebrew roots of the faith. Neither comes from the Torah.

## The Nine-Branch Lamp

Hanukkah remembers the rededication of the Temple in 164 BC, after the Maccabees drove out the Greek king who had defiled it. The book of 1 Maccabees records an eight-day celebration. The story most people know — a single jar of oil that burned for eight days — first appears in rabbinic writing centuries later. The nine-branch lamp, the *hanukkiah*, holds eight lights plus a ninth "helper" light used to kindle the others.

The lamp Yahuah commanded for His sanctuary had seven lamps, not nine: "thou shalt make the seven lamps thereof" (Exodus 25:37). The nine-branch lamp is a rabbinic object, not a Torah one.

Some point to John 10:22 to show Yahushua kept Hanukkah. The verse says only that "it was the feast of the dedication, and it was winter. And Yahushua walked in the temple in Solomon's porch" (John 10:22-23). It gives the season and the place. It does not say He lit a lamp or kept a feast, and the chapter is about the Jews trying to stone Him.

## The Kippah

The Torah never commands a man to cover his head in worship. The only head-coverings in the Law were the mitres and bonnets of the priests, made for Aaron and his sons alone (Exodus 28:4, 40). The custom of the skullcap grew in rabbinic teaching and became common among Jewish men only in the Middle Ages. Paul wrote the opposite rule for the assembly: "Every man praying or prophesying, having his head covered, dishonoureth his head" (1 Corinthians 11:4).

The Torah did give Israel's men something to wear: fringes on the borders of their garments, so "that ye may look upon it, and remember all the commandments of Yahuah, and do them" (Numbers 15:38-39). A congregation that adopts the rabbinic skullcap while skipping the commanded fringes has traded Yahuah's sign for the rabbis'.

## The Verdict

The nine-branch lamp and the kippah are rabbinic traditions dressed as Hebrew roots. Returning to the roots means returning to the Torah, not to the rabbis who came after it. Both are strange fire.

Revelation shows the lamps that matter: the Son walks "in the midst of the seven golden candlesticks" (Revelation 2:1), and "the seven candlesticks ... are the seven churches" (Revelation 1:20). Seven, as the Law commanded — not nine.
