## Hebrew Bones in Greek Skin

Beyond single words, the sentences themselves run on Hebrew structure. A Greek reader of the first century would have found many of these patterns foreign. To a Hebrew ear they are native.

- **"And it came to pass"** — a direct rendering of the Hebrew vayehi (H1961) that opens story after story in Genesis, Judges, and Samuel. Luke uses it dozens of times.

- **"With desire I have desired"** (Luke 22:15) — the Hebrew doubling seen in "dying thou shalt die" (Genesis 2:17). Greek has no such pattern of its own.

- **"He stedfastly set his face to go to Jerusalem"** (Luke 9:51) — a Hebrew idiom of resolve (Jeremiah 21:10; Ezekiel 21:2).

- **"Son of peace," "children of the bridechamber," "sons of thunder"** (Luke 10:6; Matthew 9:15; Mark 3:17) — the Hebrew use of ben (H1121), "son," to mean one marked by a quality.

- **Parallel lines** — the Beatitudes and much of the Sermon on the Mount are built in the paired lines of Hebrew poetry, the structure of the Psalms.

These are not occasional slips. They are the grammar of a Hebrew mind running beneath Greek words — exactly the pattern a translation leaves behind.

## Wordplay That Only Works in Hebrew

The strongest evidence comes from passages whose meaning depends on a Hebrew word the Greek cannot carry. In each case the Greek reader is left with a statement that does not connect. The Hebrew reader sees it at once.

> **MATTHEW 1:21 — KJV**
>
> *And she shall bring forth a son, and thou shalt call his name Yahushua: for he shall save his people from their sins.*

The word "for" ties the name to the reason it was given. In Greek, Iēsous carries no meaning, and the "for" explains nothing. In Hebrew, Yahushua (H3091) is built from Yah and yasha (H3467), "to save." The name declares that Yahuah saves. The verse only makes sense in Hebrew.

> **MATTHEW 2:23 — KJV**
>
> *And he came and dwelt in a city called Nazareth: that it might be fulfilled which was spoken by the prophets, He shall be called a Nazarene.*

No verse in the Prophets contains the word "Nazarene." The Greek reader searches in vain. The Hebrew reader hears netzer (H5342), "Branch": "a Branch shall grow out of his roots" (Isaiah 11:1). The fulfilment rests entirely on a Hebrew root.

**Stones and children (Matthew 3:9).** "God is able of these stones to raise up children unto Abraham." In Greek, stones (lithōn) and children (tekna) share nothing. In Hebrew, stones are avanim (H68) and children are banim (H1121) — a play on sound that only lands in Hebrew.

**The jot and the tittle (Matthew 5:18).** The jot is the yod, the smallest Hebrew letter; the tittle is the small stroke that tells one Hebrew letter from another. Yahushua measured the permanence of the Torah by the strokes of Hebrew script — a saying that assumes a Hebrew Torah, a Hebrew-reading audience, and a Hebrew-speaking teacher.

## Matthew Follows the Hebrew Prophets

When Matthew quotes the Prophets, he often follows the Hebrew text rather than the Greek Septuagint — the Greek Bible a Greek author would be expected to use.

- **Matthew 2:15** — "Out of Egypt have I called my son" follows the Hebrew of Hosea 11:1. The Septuagint reads "his children," which would have destroyed Matthew’s point.

- **Matthew 8:17** — "Himself took our infirmities, and bare our sicknesses" follows the Hebrew of Isaiah 53:4. The Septuagint reads "he bears our sins," a different statement.

A writer who reaches past the Greek Bible to the Hebrew is a writer who reads, thinks, and works in Hebrew.

## The Objection: "The Apostles Quote the Septuagint"

Where the Greek New Testament matches the Septuagint, a translator rendering a Hebrew original would naturally use the Greek Scripture his readers already owned. Jerome says the Greek translator of Matthew is unknown; nothing prevents that translator from drawing on the Greek Bible of his day. Where the original writer’s own hand shows through — as in Matthew 2:15 and 8:17 — the quotation follows the Hebrew.
