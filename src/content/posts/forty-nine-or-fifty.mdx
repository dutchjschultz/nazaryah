The companion study, The Year of Liberty, makes its case without ever having to settle how long the Jubilee cycle runs: it shows the count was never lost, that Scripture marks two Jubilee years, and that the trumpet sounded in Nazareth. This study takes up the one question that study set aside — is the cycle forty-nine years, or fifty? It is the densest arithmetic in the whole subject, which is why it lives here on its own, where a reader who wants it can take it slowly.

## 1 · The Two Anchors

Everything here rests on two places where Scripture appears to mark a Jubilee year itself, so begin with a short recall of each. The full case for both is made in the main study.

The first is Ezekiel 40:1. Ezekiel dates his final vision “in the beginning of the year, in the tenth day of the month” — and “beginning of the year,” *rosh hashanah*, is the only occurrence of that phrase in all of Tanakh. A year that begins on the tenth day is a Jubilee year, because Leviticus 25:9 opens the Jubilee with the trumpet on the tenth day of the seventh month. Reckoned from Jehoiachin’s captivity, that vision falls at Yom Kippur of roughly 574 BC.

The second is Luke 4. Yahushua reads Isaiah 61 in the synagogue — the acceptable year, liberty to the captives, the Jubilee signature word for word — and says, “This day is this scripture fulfilled in your ears.” The trumpet that opens a Jubilee sounds again, in 27 AD.

A single anchor cannot establish a cycle. Project forward from one Jubilee at fifty-year steps and you have only assumed the fifty you were trying to find; the reasoning walks in a circle. But two anchors measure a distance, and a distance can be divided. Whichever cycle length divides the gap evenly is the cycle; the other is ruled out. Two anchors settle the phase and the interval in a single stroke.

## 2 · The Arithmetic

From Yom Kippur of 574 BC to Yom Kippur of 27 AD, remembering there is no year zero between them: 574 + 27 − 1 = 600 years.

**Six hundred years is twelve cycles of fifty, exactly.**

Now try forty-nine. Twelve cycles of forty-nine is 588 years, which lands in 15 AD. Thirteen cycles is 637, which lands in 64 AD. Neither one arrives at the scroll in Nazareth. Six hundred does not divide by forty-nine at all.

The fifty divides the interval. The forty-nine does not.

This is not proof that closes every mouth. Both anchors carry a year of tolerance, and the whole test rests on Luke 4 marking a Jubilee — which the main study argues from the text but does not prove the way a receipt proves a purchase. What it is, is a real test with a real result: the two places where Scripture appears to mark a Jubilee are separated by an interval that fifty divides cleanly and forty-nine cannot touch.

## 3 · What Torah Says About the Fiftieth

The debate is old, and the text genuinely leaves a gap.

Leviticus 25:8 says the count is forty-nine — “the space of the seven sabbaths of years shall be unto thee forty and nine years.” Then verse 10 hallows the fiftieth, and verse 11 calls it “that fiftieth year.” So Torah numbers the year. It is not floating free and uncounted; Scripture names it fiftieth three times over.

What Torah never says is which year begins the next count. That silence is the entire debate.

The forty-nine model closes the gap by making the hallowed year do double duty — the fiftieth of one cycle is simultaneously the first of the next, so the cycle is forty-nine. The fifty model reads it straight: count forty-nine, hallow the fiftieth, then begin the next forty-nine. The year of release is the year the count arrives at, not the year the next round of labor starts from. Torah does not enroll the Jubilee in the following count — that enrollment is supplied by the interpreter, not by the verse.

## 4 · A Card That Comes Off the Table

One passage gets played constantly in this argument and it should not be played by either side.

> Leviticus 25:20–22
>
> *And if ye shall say, What shall we eat the seventh year? behold, we shall not sow, nor gather in our increase: Then I will command my blessing upon you in the sixth year, and it shall bring forth fruit for three years. And ye shall sow the eighth year, and eat yet of old fruit until the ninth year...*

Look at the question being answered in verse 20: “What shall we eat the seventh year?” Seventh — not fiftieth. The answer runs six, seven, eight, nine: sow in the sixth, rest in the seventh, sow again in the eighth, eat the new crop in the ninth. Four consecutive years with nothing skipped. The Jubilee is never mentioned, and the sequence has no room for it — verse 22 says “sow the eighth year,” while verse 11 forbids sowing in the fiftieth.

So this passage is answering a question about the ordinary sabbath year. It is not evidence for forty-nine and it is not evidence for fifty. It belongs to neither side and should be surrendered by both.

And when it is set down, something else stands up. Torah promises provision for the seventh year and promises nothing at all for the Jubilee transition — where a sabbath year and a Jubilee year fall back to back and the land lies fallow twice running. Both models produce that double fallow; Leviticus 25:8–11 fixes the sabbath at forty-nine and the Jubilee at fifty no matter which year starts the next count. Torah simply never addresses it. For a nation that never once kept a Jubilee, that silence may be the loudest thing in the chapter.

## † A Note on the Book of Jubilees

The book of Jubilees states the cycle as forty-nine years, and it is often produced as though it settles the question. It does not, and the rule is the same one this ministry applies everywhere else: outside witness, never authority.

What it genuinely shows is that the ancients were arguing about this too — which is worth knowing, and worth nothing more than that. It cannot close a gap Scripture leaves open. Where Torah is silent about which year begins the next count, a second-century book asserting an answer is a man’s opinion with age on it. Let the Word govern, and let the silence stay silent until the Word fills it.
