## The Word Behind the Whole Subject

Leviticus 23:2 opens the chapter that governs everything that follows: “Speak unto the children of Israel, and say unto them, Concerning the feasts of Yahuah, which ye shall proclaim to be holy convocations, even these are my feasts.” The possessive is the point. They are not Israel's feasts, not the Jews' feasts, and not the church's optional heritage. They belong to Yahuah, and Israel was commanded to proclaim them, not to invent them.

The Hebrew word rendered “feast” is moed (H4150) — an appointed time, a fixed meeting, a set place on a calendar where two parties have agreed to be present. The English word “feast” suggests a party. The Hebrew word suggests an appointment kept. A man who misses an appointment does not merely miss a celebration; he misses the one he was to meet.

That same word appears in Genesis 1:14, before there was an Israelite alive to keep anything: “Let there be lights in the firmament of the heaven to divide the day from the night; and let them be for signs, and for seasons, and for days, and years.” The word for “seasons” is moadim. The appointments were hung in the sky on the fourth day of creation. The sun, moon, and stars are the timepiece, and the timepiece was built before the covenant, before Sinai, before Egypt, and before Abraham.

Colossians 2:16-17 is the verse most often used to sweep the whole subject away. It says the appointed times are “a shadow of things to come; but the body is of Messiah.” A shadow is proof that something solid is standing in the light. The verse does not abolish the shadow; it identifies what casts it. And the tense is not past. The shadow is of things still coming.

## The Calendar Underneath the Dates

None of the dates in this study can be located without the calendar Yahuah set in the heavens, so the backbone is worth stating plainly before the feasts are walked through.

The day is twelve hours, dawn to dusk. It begins when the light breaks and ends when the light goes. Sundown-to-sundown reckoning is Babylonian rabbinic tradition carried into the church, not Torah.

The month begins at the renewed moon — chodesh, from chadash, to renew. The renewal is the moment the moon passes the sun and turns back toward light, and the dawn that follows opens the month. Not the full moon, which Scripture names separately as kese (Psalm 81:3), and which falls on the fifteenth.

That moment is a single instant in the heavens, identical for every man on earth regardless of his longitude, his latitude, his season or his weather. It is the only reckoning under which the whole earth keeps one appointed time on one day. First visible light does not do this: the crescent appears on different evenings in different places, and a rule that hands two men two different days has not given them one appointed time. It also matches Hanok 78:7, which places accomplished light on the fifteenth day — full light falls roughly fourteen and three-quarter days after the passing, which lands on the fifteenth exactly. Reckoned from first visibility it would fall on the thirteenth or fourteenth.

The Sabbaths of each month fall on the 8th, 15th, 22nd, and 29th, resetting at each renewed moon. The continuous rotating week came from Rome after the resurrection.

The year begins with Aviv, and the marker is the sun itself. Watched from one fixed place, the sun rises far to the south through the winter and far to the north through the summer, walking out and back once each year. One morning in the spring it rises at the exact middle of that walk, and on that day the light and the dark are equal. That is the turn of the year.

The cost of reading it is two stones set on a line and a clear horizon. No instrument, no calculation, and no reckoning handed down from any authority. It works in Egypt, in a wilderness with no field in it, and on the far side of the earth on the same morning. At the turn the point of rising moves nearly a full sun-width every day, the fastest it moves all year, which is why the one moment the calendar needs is the one moment the sun is easiest to read. Hanok describes the same thing as the sun rising in the fourth portal, where the daytime is equal to the night.

The stars hold station rather than mark the turn. Scripture gives them rank and order — brought out by number, every one called by name, not one of them failing (Isaiah 40:26), Mazzaroth brought forth in his season and Ayish guided with her sons (Job 38:32). They are witnesses to a year already fixed, not the instrument that fixes it. The barley in the land is the same: a confirmation, and not a determiner, since Israel kept this appointment in a wilderness where no barley grew (Numbers 9:1-5).

With that in place, the appointed times fall into two groups: four in the first month and the weeks after it, and three in the seventh month. The first group has already landed, to the day and in several cases to the hour. The second group has not.

## Pesach — The Fourteenth Day of the First Month

### What was done

Exodus 12 gives the ordinance. A lamb without blemish was taken into the household on the tenth day and kept up until the fourteenth. On the fourteenth it was killed between the evenings — the declining afternoon, at about the ninth hour, three in the afternoon. Its blood was struck on the two side posts and the upper door post. The destroyer passed over the blood.

What is not in the passage deserves attention. There is no mention of the character, obedience, or worthiness of anyone inside the house. The blood was on the outside, where the destroyer could see it, and the household was spared on that basis alone. Nothing a man did inside the house that night added a thing to his protection.

### How it is portrayed in the Apostolic writings

Yahushua died at the ninth hour on the fourteenth of Aviv, in the same hour the lambs were being slaughtered in the courts (Matthew 27:46-50). The command that no bone of the lamb be broken (Exodus 12:46) is applied directly to Him in John 19:36, when the soldiers broke the legs of the two beside Him and found Him already dead. John announced Him as the Lamb of Yahuah in John 1:29. Paul states it without hedging in 1 Corinthians 5:7: Messiah our Pesach is sacrificed for us.

One correction belongs here, because nearly the whole of modern Christianity has the sequence a full day out of place. The meal in the upper room was not the Pesach meal. John 13:1 says it took place before the feast. John 13:29 records that when Judas left the table, the disciples assumed he had gone to buy what was needed for the feast — which means the feast was still ahead of them. And all four accounts of that meal use artos, ordinary leavened bread, not azymos, unleavened. The meal was a preparation meal on the thirteenth. Yahushua died on the fourteenth at three in the afternoon, and the Pesach meal was eaten that night with His body already in the tomb. Placing the upper room on Pesach pushes the death to the fifteenth and destroys the very pattern the feast was given to teach.

### What it accomplishes

Pesach is entry. It is the door, and the door is blood. Romans 5:9-10 sets the two stages side by side: justified by His blood, and saved by His life. The first half of that verse is Pesach.

The agency in that transaction is worth keeping straight. Yahuah is the redeemer and the only Savior — He says so Himself in Isaiah 43:11 and Hosea 13:4. The price He paid was His own Son. The Lamb was provided by the Father, exactly as Abraham told Isaac on the mountain in Genesis 22:8. The Son provided the way in; the Father is the one who saves.

## Hag HaMatzot — The Fifteenth Through the Twenty-First

### What was done

Seven days of unleavened bread, opening with a holy convocation on the fifteenth and closing with one on the twenty-first. Leaven was to be put out of the houses entirely, and whoever ate what was leavened during those days was cut off from the congregation (Exodus 12:15, 19).

Leaven works quietly. A small quantity is worked into the dough, disappears from sight, and given time changes the character of the entire lump. That is the reason it was chosen as the picture.

### How it is portrayed in the Apostolic writings

1 Corinthians 5:6-8 takes the ordinance and applies it directly to a mixed Greek assembly, in the present tense, as a thing they are to be doing: a little leaven leavens the whole lump; purge out therefore the old leaven; let us keep the feast, not with old leaven, neither with the leaven of malice and wickedness, but with the unleavened bread of sincerity and truth. Paul is not using a dead ceremony as a poetic figure. He tells them to keep it.

The tomb also sits inside these days, though not across all of them. Yahushua died on the fourteenth in the afternoon and rose on the sixteenth, the second day of the feast. Psalm 16:10 promised that His flesh would see no corruption, and Peter applies the verse to Him in Acts 2:31. The body lay through the opening days of unleavened bread and did not spoil.

### What it accomplishes

Where Pesach is entry, Hag HaMatzot is the walk. The blood brought the household out; the seven days deal with what the household brought with it. This is the second stage of deliverance — consummation through the bread, which is the body, which is the commandments of Yahuah lived out.

This is precisely the point where modern doctrine collapses. It keeps the blood and discards the seven days, teaching an entry with no walk behind it, and then has to argue that the Law was abolished in order to justify the gap. Scripture never separates them. The same night the blood went on the door, the unleavened bread went on the table.

## Bikkurim — The Sixteenth Day

### What was done

Leviticus 23:10-11 commands that when the harvest was come, a sheaf of the firstfruits be brought to the priest and waved before Yahuah on the morrow after the Sabbath, to be accepted. Verse 14 attaches the consequence: not a loaf, not a parched grain, not a green ear of the new harvest could be eaten by anyone until that one sheaf had been presented and received.

The date is fixed and not open to argument once the lunar reckoning is in place. The Sabbath that falls inside Hag HaMatzot is the fifteenth. The morrow after that Sabbath is the sixteenth, every year, without calculation. The long dispute over which Sabbath the verse means exists only because the continuous Roman week was laid over the top of Yahuah's lunar reckoning, leaving the wave sheaf floating loose.

### How it is portrayed in the Apostolic writings

1 Corinthians 15:20-23 names Him the firstfruits of them that slept, and orders the harvest: Messiah the firstfruits, afterward they that are His at His coming. The pattern of the ordinance is visible in the resurrection morning itself. In John 20:17 He tells Miryam not to touch Him, because He had not yet ascended to the Father. A few verses later He invites Thomas to reach out his hand and touch the wounds. Between those two moments the sheaf was presented and accepted, and the rest of the harvest could be handled.

### What it accomplishes

Bikkurim is a guarantee. The single sheaf is not the harvest; it is the pledge that the field behind it is coming in. His resurrection is not presented in Scripture as a wonder to be admired but as the first armful of a harvest already standing.

It is worth noticing what happened to this appointment. The resurrection is now observed on a spring Sunday fixed by an equinox rule and a full-moon calculation handed down from Rome, carrying a fertility name and fertility symbols, and landing wherever the arithmetic puts it. The actual date is the sixteenth of the first month, and it does not move.

### Three days and three nights

The sign given in Matthew 12:40 is the objection raised against every dating that is not a Friday burial and a Sunday resurrection. The fixed dates answer it without strain. Yahushua died on the fourteenth in the afternoon and rose on the sixteenth: the fourteenth, the fifteenth, and the sixteenth. Three days, counted the way Scripture counts days.

Scripture is explicit about how it counts. Joseph put his brothers in ward three days and then spoke to them on the third day (Genesis 42:17-18). The Egyptian servant found in the field had eaten no bread and drunk no water three days and three nights, and in the next breath said his master left him three days ago — the identical span, stated both ways (1 Samuel 30:12-13). Esther called a fast of three days, night or day, and went in to the king on the third day (Esther 4:16; 5:1). Rehoboam told Israel to return after three days, and they came on the third day (2 Chronicles 10:5, 12). Three days and three nights is a Hebrew expression for a span that touches three days. It was never a stopwatch.

The seventy-two-hour argument exists for one reason. Once the death was cut loose from the fourteenth of the first month and hung on a floating Friday, the arithmetic stopped working and had to be rescued by moving the crucifixion earlier in the week. Put the day back where the text fixes it and there is nothing left to rescue.

## Shavuot — Counted Out Into the Summer

### What was done

Leviticus 23:15-16 gives the count in two parts. Seven Sabbaths are to be counted complete from the day the sheaf was waved, and then a count of fifty days is numbered. Two loaves were then brought out of the habitations and waved — and those two loaves were commanded to be baked with leaven, the single exception in the entire sacrificial system. The reason is not obscure: those loaves are a picture of people, not of Him.

The count as written lands roughly a hundred days from the sixteenth of the first month, which puts Shavuot in the summer, when the wheat is actually standing and ready. The common Christian reckoning counts only fifty days and lands in late spring — about fifty days early, and in a season when the wheat harvest is not yet in. The Greek name attached to that early date should not be used at all; it is a manipulation, and the feast has a name of its own.

What happened at the mountain belongs in the same picture. Exodus 19 records fire, smoke, a voice, and the sound of a trumpet, and the Torah given to a people gathered at the foot of Sinai. Exodus 32:28 records that three thousand fell in the aftermath of the golden calf.

### How it is portrayed in the Apostolic writings

Acts 2 repeats the scene deliberately. There is a sound from heaven, there is fire resting on each of them, there is a gathering out of every nation under heaven, and three thousand are added. The mountain scene is run again with the numbers reversed — three thousand fell at the giving of the Law on stone, three thousand were added at the writing of the same Law on hearts.

That is what Jeremiah 31:33 promised and what Hebrews 8:10 quotes: I will put my law in their inward parts, and write it in their hearts. The content is not new. The location is. Anyone teaching that this event replaced the Law with something else has to explain why the promise being fulfilled is a promise about the Law itself.

### What it accomplishes

Shavuot is the covenant moved inward and the two loaves brought in together — the two houses, leavened because they are made of men, and accepted anyway because they are waved by the priest.

## Yom Teruah — The First Day of the Seventh Month

### What was done

Leviticus 23:24 calls it a memorial of blowing of trumpets, a holy convocation, with a commanded rest. Numbers 29:1 repeats it. It is the only appointed time in the year that falls on the renewed moon itself, on the first day of a month.

That detail carries the whole character of the day. The month does not open on a man’s report; it opens on the two lights themselves. The moon passes the sun at one instant, and that instant is handed to every man alive at once, whatever his sky looks like. A watchman in Judea and a watchman on the far side of the earth receive the same moment. It is an appointment the whole earth can keep together, on the same day, with no court standing in the middle to decide it.

### What has been laid on this day

Six things have been added to the first day of the seventh month, and not one of them is in the text. Taken singly each looks small. Taken together they replace the day.

First, the name. The day is now called the head of the year, a title Scripture never gives it. The first month of the year was named at the exodus, in the spring: “This month shall be unto you the beginning of months: it shall be the first month of the year to you” (Exodus 12:2). Scripture calls this day the first day of the seventh month and nothing else (Leviticus 23:24; Numbers 29:1). The Hebrew phrase rosh hashanah occurs exactly once in the whole of Scripture, at Ezekiel 40:1 — and there it lands on the tenth day of the month, not the first. The single occurrence of the phrase does not describe the day the name was later attached to.

Second, the length. The text names one day. Two are now kept, a second day added to cover the uncertainty introduced by a sighting rule that the text never gave in the first place — an addition built to patch an addition.

Third, the start. The day is now begun in the darkness of the night before, under the sundown reckoning carried back from Babylon. The Torah day is twelve hours of light, dawn to dusk. Yom Kippur and Pesach are the two exceptions named in the text, and Yom Teruah is not among them.

Fourth, the postponement. This is the one worth naming carefully, because the objection is often aimed at the wrong target. Calculation itself is not the fault; the passing of the moon by the sun is an event in the heavens and reckoning from it is reading the sign Yahuah hung there. The fault is that the reckoning was then bent. Days are pushed forward and held back by rule so that certain dates never fall where the calendar puts them, because falling there is inconvenient. Once a day can be moved for convenience it has stopped being an appointment and become a preference.

Fifth, the year. The day was made the beginning of a year that Yahuah began in the spring. A seventh month cannot be the head of a year. That is not a theological objection; it is counting. The one textual hook offered is the ingathering at the going out of the year (Exodus 23:16; 34:22) — the close of the agricultural season in the seventh month. But a year that ends in the seventh month began in the first. That verse assumes the spring beginning; it does not overturn it.

Sixth, the fast. A penitential season was built around the day, with fasting attached to it in custom, though the text commands no fast here at all. The only fast Yahuah commanded falls nine days later, on Yom Kippur. This day is commanded with a trumpet blast and a rest, not with affliction.

The month name itself belongs to the same accumulation. Tishri is not in the Torah; it belongs to the Babylonian month list carried back from the captivity, where Scripture simply numbers the months. That same imported list carries a month named for a foreign deity, and Ezekiel was shown women weeping for him at the gate of the house of Yahuah (Ezekiel 8:14).

The order of the additions is worth studying on its own, because it is not unique to this day. A thing is renamed. The new name is then treated as evidence. The day is extended, moved, and adjusted to suit, and each step is defended by the step before it, until the appointment on the page and the observance on the ground have nothing in common but a position in the month. The title became the argument, and recognizing that move is most of the answer.

### How it is portrayed in the Apostolic writings

1 Corinthians 15:52 and 1 Thessalonians 4:16 place the last trump at the resurrection of the dead, with the shout and the voice of the archangel. Matthew 24:36 is frequently attached to this day on the argument that a feast opening on a crescent sighting cannot be named in advance. That argument is set aside here. The date of the first day of the seventh month is reckoned from the passing of the moon by the sun and is knowable years ahead, so whatever Matthew 24:36 withholds, it is not the date of a moed. The verse speaks of the day of His coming, and nothing in this study rests on it.

Two errors deserve to be named. First, this is not a secret removal of believers ahead of a seven-year tribulation. Daniel's seventieth week is not sitting in a gap waiting to run; all four hundred and ninety years are complete, the sacrifice was caused to cease in the midst of the week at the tree in 31 AD, and the week closed within a few years afterward. Second, there is no thousand-year earthly kingdom on the far side of that trumpet. When He returns, death is ended and the judgment is held, and that is the whole of it.

### What it accomplishes

A trumpet in Scripture does three things: it assembles the camp, it warns of war, and it announces a king. Yom Teruah is the awakening — the appointment that gathers what has been scattered and calls it to attention before the day of covering that follows it.

## Yom Kippur — The Tenth Day of the Seventh Month

### What was done

Leviticus 16 and Leviticus 23:26-32 give the one day in the year when the high priest passed the inner veil into the Most Holy Place, and never without blood. Two goats stood at the door: one was killed and its blood carried in, and the other bore the iniquities of the people away into a land not inhabited. The people afflicted their souls and did no work at all, under penalty of being cut off.

This day and Pesach are the two stated exceptions reckoned from evening to evening (Leviticus 23:32). They are exceptions given by name, and an exception given by name does not overturn the rule it is an exception to. The ordinary day still runs dawn to dusk.

### The one commanded fast

The phrase rendered “afflict your souls” is anah nephesh, and it appears five times in connection with this day (Leviticus 16:29, 31; 23:27, 32; Numbers 29:7). Isaiah treats afflicting the soul and fasting as the same act, putting the two in parallel in a single complaint (Isaiah 58:3, 5). This is the only fast Yahuah ever commanded. Not a season of fasting, not a calendar of fast days — one day in the year, and this is it.

The weight attached to it is unusual. Leviticus 23:29 puts the man who will not afflict his soul on that day outside the people, and verse 31 forbids not merely servile work but any manner of work at all, a restriction heavier than the one placed on the other appointed times. Because this day is one of the two reckoned from evening to evening, the affliction runs that full span rather than the twelve hours of light.

The prophet also fixes what an accepted fast consists of. Isaiah 58:5-7 rejects the bowed head and the sackcloth taken by themselves and names the fast that is chosen: to loose the bands of wickedness, to undo the heavy burdens, to deal bread to the hungry, to bring the poor that are cast out into the house, to cover the naked. Abstaining from food while leaving those things undone is the fast the passage refuses.

Every other appointed time runs in the opposite direction. The commanded posture at the others is eating, drinking, and rejoicing (Deuteronomy 16:14; Nehemiah 8:10). One day of affliction stands in a calendar otherwise built on gladness, and the contrast is the point. The camp is cleansed once, and then it feasts.

The reckoning was still in ordinary use long after the resurrection. Luke dates a sailing decision by it and calls it simply “the fast,” with no explanation offered to his reader (Acts 27:9). The day needed no gloss.

### How it is portrayed in the Apostolic writings

Hebrews 9:11-12 is the direct commentary: Messiah being come an high priest of good things to come, by a greater and more perfect tabernacle, not made with hands, neither by the blood of goats and calves, but by His own blood He entered in once into the holy place, having obtained eternal redemption for us. Verses 24-26 press the same point — once, in the end of the world, to put away sin by the sacrifice of Himself.

The veil that tore at His death in Matthew 27:51 was the outer veil, the one closing the Holy Place. That tearing opened priestly access into the Holy Place for the believer-priest. The inner veil into the Most Holy is passed by the High Priest alone, by His own blood, and He passed it once.

This is where the doctrine of an investigative judgment beginning in 1844 falls apart. Hebrews 9 places His entry into the heavenly Most Holy at the tree, once, complete. It does not place it eighteen centuries later as an opening act still being worked through.

### What it accomplishes

Yom Kippur is covering and removal — the sanctuary cleansed, the iniquity carried out of the camp, the dwelling made fit for the one who dwells in it. It stands between the trumpet and the ingathering because that is the order: the camp is called, the camp is cleansed, and then the camp is gathered home.

## Sukkot — The Fifteenth Through the Twenty-First of the Seventh Month

### What was done

Leviticus 23:34-43 commands seven days of dwelling in booths, with the reason attached in verse 43: that the generations to come would know that Israel dwelt in booths when they were brought out of Egypt. Exodus 23:16 names it the feast of ingathering at the end of the year, when the labor of the field has been gathered in. It is the most joyful of the appointed times, and the one commanded with rejoicing.

The booth is deliberately temporary. A family left a house it had built and lived for a week in something that would not survive a season, in order to remember that the shelter that carried them through the wilderness was not one they constructed.

### How it is portrayed in the Apostolic writings

John 1:14 says the Word was made flesh and dwelt among us — the Greek is eskenosen, He tented, He tabernacled. He took up a temporary dwelling in the middle of the camp. Revelation 21:3 closes the matter at the other end of the book: the tabernacle of Yahuah is with men, and He will dwell with them, and they shall be His people. Zechariah 14:16 has the nations left after the great day going up year after year to keep the feast of tabernacles.

### What it accomplishes

Sukkot is the ingathering and the dwelling. It is the last of the appointed times, and what it pictures is the end of the matter — not a temporary earthly reign, but the new heavens and the new earth of Isaiah 65:17 and Revelation 21, where He dwells with His people, death is no more, and the Sabbath is kept from one renewed moon to another and from one Sabbath to another (Isaiah 66:22-23).

## Why the Dates Matter

The dates are the argument. Four appointments in the first group landed exactly where they were set: the Lamb died on the fourteenth at the ninth hour, the body lay unleavened through the seven days, the sheaf was accepted on the sixteenth, and the covenant was written on hearts at the counted-out feast. Nothing about that was approximate. He did not die near Pesach; He died on Pesach, in the hour the knives were being used.

A shadow that fell to the hour four times is not a shadow that can be swept out of the house before the rest of it has landed. The three appointments of the seventh month have not yet been kept in their fullness, and the precision of the first four is the only serious reason to expect the last three to arrive with the same precision.

That is also why the calendar cannot be treated as decoration. An appointment kept on the wrong day is an appointment missed. A calendar built on an equinox calculation, a rabbinic reckoning, or a Roman rotation will place a man in the right ceremony on the wrong date, and the moed is a meeting, not a mood.

The same principle answers the question of where the year begins. Yahuah named the first month in the spring, and He named it before Israel had left Egypt. Every scheme that opens the year in the seventh month has to reach past that verse to do it, and every one of them arrives by way of a name rather than by way of a text.

What the feasts accomplish, taken together, is a single line drawn from a door in Egypt to a dwelling in the new earth. Entry through the blood. The walk through the bread. The pledge of a harvest. The covenant written inward. The trumpet that wakes the camp. The covering that cleanses it. The gathering that brings it home. Seven appointments, one deliverance, and every one of them belongs to Yahuah.
