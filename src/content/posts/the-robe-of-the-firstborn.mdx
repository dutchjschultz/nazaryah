## What the Pulpit Taught

Most people met Joseph's coat in Sunday school. It was a bright coat with stripes of every color. Later a Broadway musical turned it into a "Technicolor Dreamcoat," and the picture was set for good. The lesson that came with it was just as simple: Jacob played favorites, his sons got jealous, and families should be careful about favoritism.

That lesson is not wrong as far as it goes. It just does not go very far. It turns one of the richest garments in the Bible into a piece of children's clothing, and it turns a story about rank, blood, and a sent-away son into a lesson about family manners. The translators made the coat colorful and the meaning gray. It is lipstick on a pig: something that looks better and says less.

## What the Hebrew Says

The Hebrew words in Genesis 37:3 are *ketonet passim*. *Ketonet* (H3801) is a tunic, a long inner garment. *Pas* (H6446) is the palm of the hand or the sole of the foot. The same root shows up in Daniel 5:5, where the *"part of the hand"* that wrote on the wall is the *pas*, the palm.

Put together, a ketonet passim is a tunic reaching to the palms and the soles: long sleeves to the wrists, a hem to the ankles. That is not a work garment. A man who works the flocks wears something short that he can move in. A man wears a full-length tunic when he is not expected to labor. It marks a position.

The colors came from translation, not from the Hebrew. The Greek Old Testament rendered the phrase as a *chiton poikilos*, a varied or many-colored tunic. The Latin went with a tunic of many threads. The English Bibles followed the Greek, and "coat of many colours" stuck. Even Strong's dictionary, after giving the meaning palm and sole, adds a note that the garment was "perhaps" variegated. That "perhaps" is the translators' tradition talking. The Hebrew word itself points to length and rank.

## Where Scripture Uses the Ketonet

The best way to learn what a word means is to watch where Scripture uses it. The ketonet appears at three moments that settle the matter.

First, the ketonet is the first garment in the Bible. After the fall, Yahuah clothed the man and the woman Himself:

> *Unto Adam also and to his wife did Yahuah Elohim make coats of skins, and clothed them.*  
> — Genesis 3:21

Those coats are *kotnot*, the plural of ketonet. Skins mean an animal died. The first ketonet was a covering given by Yahuah, made from a death that was not their own.

Second, the ketonet is the garment of the priesthood. Yahuah commanded it for Aaron and his sons, and on the day of atonement the high priest wore it into the Most Holy Place:

> *He shall put on the holy linen coat, and he shall have the linen breeches upon his flesh.*  
> — Leviticus 16:4

Third, the only other ketonet passim in the entire Bible belonged to Tamar, the daughter of King David. The text stops to explain what the garment meant:

> *And she had a garment of divers colours upon her: for with such robes were the king's daughters that were virgins apparelled.*  
> — 2 Samuel 13:18

Scripture defines its own term. A ketonet passim was the robe of a king's house. It told everyone who saw it who the wearer was and what place they held. When Tamar was shamed, she *"rent her garment of divers colours"* (2 Samuel 13:19). The torn robe was a torn position.

## The Robe of the Firstborn

So what was Jacob doing when he put this robe on Joseph? He was not handing out a gift. He was naming an heir.

Reuben was Jacob's firstborn, but he defiled his father's bed (Genesis 35:22), and Jacob later said plainly that he would *"not excel"* (Genesis 49:4). The right of the firstborn had to go somewhere. Scripture tells exactly where it went:

> *Now the sons of Reuben the firstborn of Israel, (for he was the firstborn; but, forasmuch as he defiled his father's bed, his birthright was given unto the sons of Joseph the son of Israel.*  
> — 1 Chronicles 5:1

Joseph was the firstborn of Rachel, the wife Jacob loved. When Jacob dressed him in the ketonet passim, every brother understood the message. That is why the very next verse says they *"hated him, and could not speak peaceably unto him"* (Genesis 37:4). Then came the dreams of sheaves and stars bowing down (Genesis 37:7-9), and the brothers asked the real question: *"Shalt thou indeed reign over us?"* (Genesis 37:8).

Grown men do not plot murder over a colorful coat. They plot it over who will rule. The robe was the father's appointment, and the brothers rejected it.

## Stripped in the Wilderness

Jacob sent Joseph to check on his brothers, and they saw him coming from far off. The robe would have been visible at a distance. Their first move tells everything:

> *And it came to pass, when Joseph was come unto his brethren, that they stript Joseph out of his coat, his coat of many colours that was on him.*  
> — Genesis 37:23

They went for the robe first. Then they cast him into *"this pit that is in the wilderness"* (Genesis 37:22), a pit with no water in it (Genesis 37:24). The firstborn was stripped of his position and put away in the wilderness by his own brothers.

Judah then asked, *"What profit is it if we slay our brother, and conceal his blood?"* (Genesis 37:26). So they did not shed his blood. They sold him alive for twenty pieces of silver to traders going down to Egypt (Genesis 37:28). But they still needed blood to bring home.

## The Two Goats

Here is where the story opens up. The brothers needed the robe to come back bloody, so they took an animal:

> *And they took Joseph's coat, and killed a kid of the goats, and dipped the coat in the blood.*  
> — Genesis 37:31

The Hebrew for *"kid of the goats"* here is *se'ir izzim*. *Se'ir* (H8163) is the word for the goat of the sin offering. It is the exact phrase Yahuah uses for the day of atonement:

> *And he shall take of the congregation of the children of Israel two kids of the goats for a sin offering.*  
> — Leviticus 16:5

On Yom Kippur there were two goats, and only one of them bled. The goat for Yahuah was slain, and its blood was carried in before the mercy seat:

> *Then shall he kill the goat of the sin offering, that is for the people, and bring his blood within the vail, and do with that blood as he did with the blood of the bullock, and sprinkle it upon the mercy seat, and before the mercy seat.*  
> — Leviticus 16:15

That blood finished the atonement. Genesis 37 holds that goat. A goat died, and its blood went on the firstborn's robe in place of the firstborn's own. Then the robe carrying that blood was brought to the father, the way the blood of the goat for Yahuah was carried in before Him.

The brothers meant it as a cover story. They were carrying out a picture they did not understand: the firstborn's garment, marked with sin-offering blood, presented to the father. The pattern of atonement was written into Israel's family history long before Sinai.

And the guilt did not go away with the lie. The brothers carried it for more than twenty years, until it came out in Egypt: *"We are verily guilty concerning our brother"* (Genesis 42:21). Blood covers sin. Hiding it covers nothing.

## A House of Goats and Garments

The goat and the garment did not appear out of nowhere in Genesis 37. Jacob had used the same two things years before, on his own father.

When Jacob took Esau's blessing, Rebekah dressed him in Esau's garments and covered his hands and neck with *"the skins of the kids of the goats"* (Genesis 27:15-16). Isaac was deceived by a goat and a garment. Now Jacob was deceived by his sons with a goat and a garment. What Jacob sowed with his father, he reaped with his sons.

And the very next chapter does it again. Judah promised Tamar *"a kid from the flock"* as payment and left his signet, bracelets, and staff with her as a pledge (Genesis 38:17-18). When the truth came out, Tamar sent the pledge to Judah with these words: *"Discern, I pray thee, whose are these"* (Genesis 38:25).

That is the same Hebrew phrase the brothers used when they handed Jacob the bloody robe: *"Know now whether it be thy son's coat or no"* (Genesis 37:32). Both are *haker na*, "recognize, please." Judah, who suggested selling his brother, was made to recognize his own items the way his father had been made to recognize Joseph's robe. Scripture ties the two chapters together with a goat, a garment, and the same two words.

## Re-Robed in Linen

Joseph's garments keep changing, and every change marks his position.

In Potiphar's house, his master's wife caught him *"by his garment"* and he fled, leaving it in her hand (Genesis 39:12). For the second time, Joseph's garment was taken and used as false witness against him, and he went down into a prison pit.

When Pharaoh called for him, Joseph *"shaved himself, and changed his raiment"* (Genesis 41:14). Then, after he gave the interpretation, Pharaoh dressed him in a new robe:

> *And Pharaoh took off his ring from his hand, and put it upon Joseph's hand, and arrayed him in vestures of fine linen, and put a gold chain about his neck.*  
> — Genesis 41:42

*Fine linen* is *shesh* (H8336). It is the same material Yahuah required for the priestly coats: *"coats of fine linen of woven work for Aaron, and for his sons"* (Exodus 39:27). The firstborn stripped of his robe in the wilderness is clothed again in priestly linen. Joseph was *"thirty years old when he stood before Pharaoh"* (Genesis 41:46), the same age at which the priests entered their service (Numbers 4:3).

The day of atonement itself turns on a change of garments. After the work in the Most Holy Place, the high priest *"shall put off the linen garments"* and *"put on his garments"* and come out (Leviticus 16:23-24). Joseph's life follows that same line: robed, stripped, put away, and then robed again in glory.

## Second Only to the Throne

Joseph's exaltation also settles a question the modern church gets wrong. Pharaoh gave Joseph authority over all the land, and every knee bowed before him (Genesis 41:43). But Pharaoh set one boundary in the same breath:

> *Thou shalt be over my house, and according unto thy word shall all my people be ruled: only in the throne will I be greater than thou.*  
> — Genesis 41:40

Joseph ruled over everything, yet he was not equal to Pharaoh. He held the ring, the authority, and the people's bowed knees, but the throne stayed Pharaoh's. Every honor given to Joseph came from Pharaoh and pointed back to him.

That is exactly how the apostles describe the Son. All things are put under Him, *"it is manifest that he is excepted, which did put all things under him"*, and in the end *"shall the Son also himself be subject unto him that put all things under him"* (1 Corinthians 15:27-28). Every knee bows to Yahushua, *"to the glory of God the Father"* (Philippians 2:10-11). The Joseph picture has a father, a firstborn, and a throne that belongs to one. It leaves no room for a Trinity of co-equal persons. The firstborn is exalted by the father, not equal to him.

## The Messiah in the Robe

This is not reading too much into the story. Stephen, standing before the council, used Joseph as the pattern for what the leaders had done to Yahushua: *"the patriarchs, moved with envy, sold Joseph into Egypt: but God was with him"* (Acts 7:9). The brothers who rejected the firstborn were the picture of Israel's leaders rejecting the Messiah.

At the cross, the soldiers did what Joseph's brothers did. They stripped Him. They had already put a scarlet robe on Him in mockery and taken it off again (Matthew 27:28, 31). Then they divided His garments:

> *Then the soldiers, when they had crucified Yahushua, took his garments, and made four parts, to every soldier a part; and also his coat: now the coat was without seam, woven from the top throughout.*  
> — John 19:23

The Greek word for that coat is *chiton* (G5509), the same word the Greek Old Testament uses for Joseph's ketonet in Genesis 37:3. It was woven in one piece, like the priestly coats of *"woven work"* in Exodus 39:27. They did not tear it. They cast lots for it, fulfilling *"They part my garments among them, and cast lots upon my vesture"* (Psalm 22:18).

The day of atonement pattern carries through as well. The sin-offering animals whose blood went into the sanctuary were burned *"without the camp"*, and so Yahushua *"that he might sanctify the people with his own blood, suffered without the gate"* (Hebrews 13:11-12). Joseph's robe was dipped in the blood of a sin-offering goat. Yahushua entered the Most Holy Place *"by his own blood"*, once for all (Hebrews 9:12). Joseph needed a goat to stand in for him. Yahushua was the one Yahuah provided.

The last picture of the robe comes from Revelation. John sees one *"like unto the Son of man, clothed with a garment down to the foot"* (Revelation 1:13). That is the ketonet passim again: a robe to the soles of the feet. The Greek word, *poderes* (G4158), is the word the Greek Old Testament uses for the high priest's robe (Exodus 28:4). And when He rides out at the end, *"he was clothed with a vesture dipped in blood"* (Revelation 19:13). The robe Joseph's brothers dipped in blood to deny his position is the robe the Firstborn wears to claim His.

## Taking the Lipstick Off

"Coat of many colours" did not translate Genesis 37:3. It decorated it. And once the coat became a pretty piece of clothing, everything else went gray: the firstborn's appointment, the brothers' rejection, the wilderness pit, the sin-offering goat, the robe returned to the father in blood, and the son sent away alive and later robed in linen and set at the right hand of the throne.

Read the garment the way Scripture defines it, and Joseph stands where the text puts him. He is the appointed firstborn, hated by his brothers, stripped of his robe, put away in the wilderness, and exalted by the father to rule over all, second only to the throne. That is not a children's story about favoritism. It is the gospel of the Firstborn, written into the life of Joseph centuries before the Messiah walked out of the tomb.

Revelation finishes the picture. Joseph's brothers finally stood before the one they had stripped and sold, and *"they were troubled at his presence"* (Genesis 45:3). So it will be when the Firstborn returns in the robe dipped in blood: *"every eye shall see him, and they also which pierced him"* (Revelation 1:7). The robe the brothers took from Joseph, the Father has given back to His Son, and every eye will see Him wearing it.
