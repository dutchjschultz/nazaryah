## Courtroom or Wedding

Most believers picture the final judgment as a courtroom: a ledger of deeds, a judge weighing each offense, and a verdict read out like a criminal sentence. Modern teaching offers two ways out of that courtroom. One says the commandments no longer apply to the believer. The other says that once a person believes, nothing can ever be lost. Both rest on the courtroom picture.

Scripture gives another picture, and it changes the question. The final examination is a wedding. The question is not how many offenses are on the record. It is whether the betrothed bride kept her vows. This study completes the sequence of Given in Love and Written on the Heart: the ten were given in love at Sinai, written on the heart in the new covenant, and at the end they are the vows the Bridegroom examines.

## Betrothal in Scripture

The Hebrew word is *aras*, "to betroth." Betrothal was not a casual engagement. It was a binding covenant made before the wedding feast and before the couple lived together. A betrothed young woman was already called "his neighbour's wife" (Deuteronomy 22:24), and unfaithfulness during betrothal was treated as adultery.

The New Testament carries the same pattern. Mary was "espoused to Joseph, before they came together" (Matthew 1:18), and Joseph is called "her husband" in the next verse. Paul tells the assembly at Corinth, "I have espoused you to one husband, that I may present you as a chaste virgin to Messiah" (2 Corinthians 11:2). The assembly is not yet at the wedding. It is betrothed, and it is waiting.

## Sinai Was the Betrothal

Yahuah describes Sinai in marriage terms. He carried Israel "on eagles' wings, and brought you unto myself," then set the terms: "if ye will obey my voice indeed, and keep my covenant" (Exodus 19:4-5). The people answered, "All that Yahuah hath spoken we will do" (Exodus 19:8). That was the bride's yes. Yahuah later remembers "the love of thine espousals" (Jeremiah 2:2) and calls Himself the one who "was an husband unto them" (Jeremiah 31:32). The ten, laid up in the ark, were the vows.

Hosea shows what a betrothal holds. Yahuah will betroth His bride "in righteousness, and in judgment, and in lovingkindness" and "in faithfulness" (Hosea 2:19-20). Judgment sits inside the betrothal itself. It is not a separate courtroom outside the marriage.

## The Father Gives the Bride to the Son

The parables show a Father arranging a wedding for His Son: "a certain king, which made a marriage for his son" (Matthew 22:2). It is the pattern of Abraham sending his servant to find a bride for Isaac (Genesis 24). The Father chooses and gives the bride; the Son receives her. These are two, not two halves of one Elohim. The same order governs the examination: "the Father judgeth no man, but hath committed all judgment unto the Son" (John 5:22). The Son examines by His Father's appointment, by His Father's words.

## The Wilderness Between

Israel was betrothed at Sinai but waited forty years to enter the land. The wilderness was where the bride was tested: Yahuah led them "to prove thee, to know what was in thine heart, whether thou wouldest keep his commandments, or no" (Deuteronomy 8:2). Revelation places the woman in the wilderness through the whole age before the return (Revelation 12:6, 14).

The assembly lives in that wilderness now, betrothed through the blood but not yet at the feast. Paul feared that "as the serpent beguiled Eve," the Corinthians would be led astray (2 Corinthians 11:3). The teaching that nothing can be lost after first belief skips the wilderness and treats the betrothal as the wedding. Yahushua said the one who "shall endure unto the end" shall be saved (Matthew 24:13), and Paul warned, "otherwise thou also shalt be cut off" (Romans 11:22).

## The Examination

Torah set out what happened when a husband questioned whether his bride had kept herself during the betrothal. The matter went to the elders at the gate and the evidence was examined (Deuteronomy 22:13-21). The question was not a tally of small faults. It was one question: had she kept herself for him?

Joseph shows a betrothal could end before the wedding. When Mary was found with child, Joseph, "being a just man," planned "to put her away privily" (Matthew 1:19), the ordinary word for divorce, and Scripture calls him just for it. Yahuah did the same with the northern kingdom: "I had put her away, and given her a bill of divorce" (Jeremiah 3:8).

The same examination stands at the end. "Fear Elohim, and keep his commandments," for Elohim "shall bring every work into judgment" (Ecclesiastes 12:13-14). James names the standard: "so speak ye, and so do, as they that shall be judged by the law of liberty" (James 2:12), after quoting two of the ten by name. The Bridegroom examines, and He examines by the vows.

## Free from the Curse, Still Bound by the Vows

The believer is not under the curse of the law. Messiah "hath redeemed us from the curse of the law, being made a curse for us" (Galatians 3:13). The law as the "schoolmaster" (Galatians 3:24-25) no longer stands over the delivered with its penalties. But being freed from the penalty of broken vows is not being freed from the vows. A bride whose debt has been paid is still a bride, and the Bridegroom still looks for faithfulness.

Paul warns of those who perish "because they received not the love of the truth" (2 Thessalonians 2:10). The Greek Old Testament uses the same word for truth in Psalm 119:142: "thy law is the truth." The lost did not lack information. They did not love the vows.

## Not Sentenced but Sent Away

Yahushua's pictures of the end are wedding pictures, and in each one the rejected are not read a sentence. They are refused entry. In the parable of the ten virgins, all ten carry lamps and all ten fall asleep. The foolish run out of oil, and when they return the door is shut. They cry, "Master, Master, open to us," and hear, "I know you not" (Matthew 25:11-12). Solomon had already named the lamp: "the commandment is a lamp; and the law is light" (Proverbs 6:23).

The same words close the Sermon on the Mount: "I never knew you: depart from me, ye that work iniquity" (Matthew 7:23). The Greek for iniquity is *anomia*, life without the law. "Know" is the marriage word of Scripture, "Adam knew Eve his wife" (Genesis 4:1), and Hosea's betrothal ends with "thou shalt know Yahuah" (Hosea 2:20). The promise of the betrothal is to know Him. The word to the rejected is that He never knew them.

The guest without a wedding garment is not questioned over a list of crimes either. He is asked how he came in without the garment, and he is cast out (Matthew 22:11-13). Being shut out is no light thing; outside is "outer darkness." But the deciding question is not how many offenses are on the record. It is whether the bride is His.

## The Bride Made Ready

Revelation shows the bride who passed the examination. "The marriage of the Lamb is come, and his wife hath made herself ready," clothed in fine linen, which "is the righteousness of saints" (Revelation 19:7-8). The Greek is *dikaiōmata*, righteous acts, the same word Paul uses for "the righteousness of the law" fulfilled in those who walk by the Spirit (Romans 8:4). The wedding dress is woven from the vows kept. Even so, "to her was granted" to wear it (Revelation 19:8): given by favor, worn by walking.

The book closes at the door. The blessing goes to those "that do his commandments, that they may have right to the tree of life," who "enter in through the gates into the city" (Revelation 22:14). And while the door stands open, the invitation still goes out: "the Spirit and the bride say, Come" (Revelation 22:17).
