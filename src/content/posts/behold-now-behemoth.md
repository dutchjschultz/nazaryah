Yahuah shows Job two great creatures, one right after the other: Behemoth, then Leviathan (Job 40–41). Behemoth is named in only one verse of the Bible, and Scripture never says what he stands for. What follows is the conclusion this study has reached, and each reader is invited to check it against the Word and dig in for himself. The conclusion is simple: Leviathan is the unseen power behind pride, and Behemoth is pride out in the open, the part a man can see.

## What Job Says

“Behold now behemoth, which I made with thee; he eateth grass as an ox” (Job 40:15). Yahuah made him, right along with man. His strength is in his loins and belly, and his bones are like brass and iron (Job 40:16, 18). He is “the chief of the ways of God,” yet his Maker can still bring a sword to him (Job 40:19). He is so sure of himself that “he trusteth that he can draw up Jordan into his mouth” (Job 40:23).

His name comes from *behemah*, the everyday Hebrew word for a beast or cattle, from a root that probably means mute. The picture is clear: a huge, fleshly, self-assured beast that cannot reason.

## Four Marks of Pride

Job names him in one verse, but he gives him several marks, and four of them can be followed through the rest of Scripture. All four lead to the same place. That should be no surprise, since Yahuah told Job just before this, “behold every one that is proud, and abase him” (Job 40:11).

**A tail like a cedar.** He “moveth his tail like a cedar” (Job 40:17). The cedar is Scripture’s picture of proud height. The day of Yahuah comes on “every one that is proud” and on “all the cedars of Lebanon” (Isaiah 2:12-13). The dragon’s tail drags down the stars (Revelation 12:4). This mark is height: pride stands tall and looks down.

**Bones of brass and iron.** His bones are brass and iron (Job 40:18). In the Law, Yahuah says, “I will break the pride of your power,” and He makes heaven “as iron” and earth “as brass” (Leviticus 26:19). Isaiah calls the neck of stubborn Israel “an iron sinew” and her brow “brass” (Isaiah 48:4). Daniel’s fourth beast has teeth “of iron” and nails “of brass” (Daniel 7:19). This mark is hardness: pride will not bend and will not be told.

**Grass like an ox.** He eats grass as an ox (Job 40:15). At the golden calf, Israel “changed their glory into the similitude of an ox that eateth grass” (Psalm 106:20). Nebuchadnezzar boasted of “great Babylon, that I have built,” was given “a beast’s heart,” and “did eat grass as oxen” until he learned that “those that walk in pride he is able to abase” (Daniel 4:16, 30, 33, 37). This mark is the trade: pride gives up the glory of Yahuah for something it can see and feed on.

**A beast in the reeds.** He lies in “the covert of the reed” (Job 40:21). In Psalm 68:30, the words the King James gives as “company of spearmen” are, as its own margin shows, “the beasts of the reeds,” set beside a multitude of bulls. Many commentators read this as a picture of proud Egypt. Pharaoh is “the great dragon” who said, “My river is mine own, and I have made it for myself” (Ezekiel 29:3), and Behemoth is the one who “trusteth that he can draw up Jordan into his mouth” (Job 40:23). This mark is self-trust: pride says it made itself.

Put the four marks together. He stands tall. He will not bend. He trades the glory of Yahuah for what he can see. He says he made it all himself. That is no strange animal. That is the proud man, drawn from the outside. Yahuah says “behold every one that is proud,” and four verses later He says “Behold now behemoth” (Job 40:11, 15). All four marks point the same way: Behemoth is the pride of man, out where anyone can look at it.

## Mute and Coiled

A mute beast cannot reason, and neither can pride. Scripture says it outright. “Man that is in honour, and understandeth not, is like the beasts that perish” (Psalm 49:20). The psalmist who watched the proud wear pride “as a chain” confessed, “So foolish was I, and ignorant: I was as a beast before thee” (Psalm 73:6, 22). Nebuchadnezzar shows the cure. When his pride was broken, he said, “my reason returned unto me” (Daniel 4:36).

Job says Behemoth’s force is “in the navel of his belly” (Job 40:16). Picture a man out in the sun with his shirt off, flexing for the crowd and posing for pictures. He cannot see how he looks or what any of it gains him. The only thing he can see is himself.

Leviathan’s name means something like the coiled one, and Behemoth’s name points to the mute beast. Together they describe pride exactly: coiled up in itself, and unable to reason past itself.

## Seen and Unseen

Yahuah says “behold” Behemoth, who lies out in the reeds (Job 40:21). Leviathan is different. He stays down in the deep, and Job is asked if he can “draw out leviathan” (Job 41:1). One can be looked at. The other has to be hauled up from where no one can see.

Behemoth is described by his flesh: loins, belly, and bones. Paul says believers wrestle “not against flesh and blood, but against principalities” (Ephesians 6:12). Leviathan fits the principality. Behemoth fits what that power looks like walking around in the world: the proud man, the proud nation, the proud life.

Scripture shows that split between a seen enemy and an unseen one again and again, and the book of Job is the clearest case. Job watched the Sabeans take his oxen and the Chaldeans take his camels (Job 1:15, 17). He never saw what the reader sees: Satan standing before Yahuah before any of it happened (Job 1:6-12). Job lost everything to men he could see because of a power he could not.

Daniel was shown the same thing. Cyrus sat on the throne of Persia in plain sight (Daniel 10:1), yet a heavenly messenger told Daniel that “the prince of the kingdom of Persia” had withstood him for twenty-one days, and that “the prince of Grecia” would come next (Daniel 10:13, 20). The seen king had an unseen prince behind him. Judas shows it in one man. He walked to the chief priests on his own two feet, yet Luke says, “Then entered Satan into Judas” (Luke 22:3).

Paul puts it in one line. There is a “prince of the power of the air,” and he is “the spirit that now worketh in the children of disobedience” (Ephesians 2:2). The prince is unseen. His children are not. The book of Job says the same thing in other words when it calls Leviathan king over “the children of pride” (Job 41:34).

This matters for how a person lives. Anyone who looks only at Behemoth blames the man. He argues with the proud boss, the proud neighbor, or the proud ruler, and when that one is gone another stands in his place. Anyone who looks only at Leviathan blames the devil, and he never deals with the pride in his own mirror. Both have to be seen. Job finally saw clearly at the end, and it was not by catching either creature. He said, “now mine eye seeth thee. Wherefore I abhor myself” (Job 42:5-6). Seeing Yahuah was what let him see himself.

## Two Beasts, One End

Job gives one great creature of the land and one of the sea. Revelation gives a beast “out of the sea” and a beast “out of the earth” (Revelation 13:1, 11). The sea hides what is in it. The earth shows what is on it. Enoch 60:7-9 also sets Leviathan in the sea and Behemoth in the wilderness. Revelation says the beast’s number is “the number of a man” (Revelation 13:18). The beast has a human face.

Job could not tame either creature, and neither can anyone else. Only their Maker can. Deliverance through Messiah pulls a person out from under the unseen king, and a humbled life takes the beast out of the man. The two will not last. The devil is cast into the lake of fire, “where the beast and the false prophet are” (Revelation 20:10). The power no one could see and the pride everyone could see end in the same place.
