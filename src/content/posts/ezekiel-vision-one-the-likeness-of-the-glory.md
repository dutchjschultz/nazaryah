The fifth day of the fourth month, in the fifth year of King Jehoiachin’s captivity (1:2), about 593 BC. Ezekiel is a priest among the captives by the river Chebar in Babylon. Jerusalem is still standing, and its temple is still in use. Back home, people were repeating a slogan: “The temple of Yahuah, The temple of Yahuah, The temple of Yahuah, are these” (Jeremiah 7:4). As long as the house stood, they said, Yahuah was in it, and the city could not fall. In Babylon, prophets were promising the captives they would be home within two years (Jeremiah 28:3-4). The first vision is Yahuah’s answer to all of it.

## The Vision

A storm rolls in from the north: a great cloud, fire folding in on itself, and brightness all around. Inside are four living creatures, each with four faces and four wings, and beside each one a wheel within a wheel, its rims full of eyes. Wherever the spirit goes, the creatures and the wheels go. Over their heads stretches a platform like crystal, and on it a throne like sapphire. On the throne is a likeness with the appearance of a man, wrapped in fire, with a rainbow around Him. Ezekiel falls on his face. A voice sends him to a rebellious house, has him eat a scroll full of mourning, and sets him as a watchman.

## The Thirtieth Year

The book opens “in the thirtieth year” (1:1), and most read it as Ezekiel’s age. Thirty was the age a priest began serving in the house of Yahuah (Numbers 4:3). Ezekiel had trained his whole life for the temple, and the year he came of age he was sitting by a canal in Babylon with no temple to serve in. So Yahuah came to him instead. “The heavens were opened, and I saw visions of Elohim” (1:1). Centuries later, Yahushua was “about thirty years of age” when He came up out of the river and “the heaven was opened” (Luke 3:21-23).

## Out of the North

“A whirlwind came out of the north” (1:4). In Scripture the north carries two meanings, and both are in this storm. It is the direction of Yahuah’s seat: “mount Zion, on the sides of the north, the city of the great King” (Psalm 48:2). And it is the direction judgment came from. When Jeremiah was called, he was shown a boiling pot tipped from the north and told, “Out of the north an evil shall break forth upon all the inhabitants of the land” (Jeremiah 1:13-14). Babylon’s armies always came down from the north. The throne arrives from Yahuah’s own seat, and it arrives the way judgment arrives, as a storm.

## The Living Creatures

In chapter 10 Ezekiel sees these creatures again and names them: “I knew that they were the cherubims” (10:20). To understand them, follow the cherubim through Scripture.

The first time cherubim appear, they stand at the east of Eden “to keep the way of the tree of life” (Genesis 3:24). They guard the way into Yahuah’s presence. Then Moses is told to make two cherubim of gold on the cover of the ark, and Yahuah says, “there I will meet with thee, and I will commune with thee from above the mercy seat, from between the two cherubims” (Exodus 25:22). Cherubim were woven into the veil of the tabernacle (Exodus 26:31), and Solomon set two great cherubim of olive wood over the ark in the temple (1 Kings 6:23-28). Every time, the cherubim mark the place where Yahuah meets His people. He is the One who “dwellest between the cherubims” (Psalm 80:1).

In Jerusalem, the cherubim stood still, carved and fixed in a room. Here they are alive, and they are carrying the throne. The place where Yahuah meets His people has come to Babylon.

## Wheels Within Wheels

Beside each creature is a wheel, “as it were a wheel in the middle of a wheel” (1:16), and the creatures “turned not when they went” (1:17). The throne can go in any direction without turning around. Daniel saw the same thing in his vision of the Ancient of days: “his throne was like the fiery flame, and his wheels as burning fire” (Daniel 7:9). Yahuah’s throne has wheels.

Compare that to the ark. The ark was carried on poles by Levites, and once it was set in the most holy place of Solomon’s temple, it stayed there (1 Kings 8:6-8). The people of Jerusalem had come to think of Yahuah the same way, set in place and staying put. The throne in Ezekiel’s vision says the opposite. It goes where He sends it.

The rims of the wheels are “full of eyes round about” (1:18). These are the eyes of Yahuah, which “run to and fro throughout the whole earth, to shew himself strong in the behalf of them whose heart is perfect toward him” (2 Chronicles 16:9). Nothing the captives were going through was out of His sight. And the wheels do not steer themselves: “the spirit of the living creature was in the wheels” (1:20). Babylon looked like it was running the world, but Yahuah called its king “Nebuchadrezzar … my servant” (Jeremiah 25:9). Babylon was one of the wheels, going where it was sent.

## The Sapphire Throne

Above the creatures is a platform “as the colour of the terrible crystal,” and above it “the likeness of a throne, as the appearance of a sapphire stone” (1:22, 26). Israel had seen that stone before. When the covenant was made at Sinai, Moses and the elders went up the mountain “and they saw the Elohim of Israel: and there was under his feet as it were a paved work of a sapphire stone” (Exodus 24:10). The throne by the river is the throne of Sinai, the throne of the covenant. The people who had broken that covenant were about to be judged by the One who made it. The Nazaryah study [*The Finger of Yahuah*](/blog/the-finger-of-yahuah) follows the sapphire stone further.

## The Rainbow

Around the One on the throne is brightness “as the appearance of the bow that is in the cloud in the day of rain” (1:28). The bow first appears after the flood. Yahuah set it in the cloud as a sign that “the waters shall no more become a flood to destroy all flesh” (Genesis 9:13-15). It is the sign that judgment will not be the end.

That is exactly what the captives needed to see. The throne was coming in judgment, and Jerusalem would fall. But the throne was circled by a promise. Ezekiel’s book does not end with the city burned. It ends with the glory coming home (43:4) and a city named “Yahuah is there” (48:35). The rainbow is around the throne from the very first chapter.

## The Likeness of the Glory

Ezekiel is careful how he names what he saw: “This was the appearance of the likeness of the glory of Yahuah” (1:28). Not Yahuah Himself. The appearance of the likeness of His glory, three steps back, because Yahuah had told Moses, “there shall no man see me, and live” (Exodus 33:20). Some teach that the man-like figure on the throne (1:26) is the Son before His birth. Ezekiel’s own words leave one figure on one throne: the glory of Yahuah, shown in a form a man could look at and survive.

## Why This Vision, and Why Now

Put the pieces together and the message is plain. The captives thought Yahuah was in the building in Jerusalem, and that the building guaranteed the city. The vision shows them a throne that is not in any building. It is carried by the cherubim who mark His presence, it rolls on wheels in any direction, it sees everything, and it has come to them in Babylon. Yahuah says it in words a few chapters later: “Although I have cast them far off among the heathen … yet will I be to them as a little sanctuary in the countries where they shall come” (11:16).

And this vision comes first for a reason. In chapters 8 to 11, Ezekiel is carried to Jerusalem and sees this same throne again, the same creatures and the same wheels: “This is the living creature that I saw by the river of Chebar” (10:15). He watches it lift off the temple, stop at the east gate, and leave the city for the mountain to the east (10:18-19; 11:23). Vision One shows Ezekiel the throne so that when it drives away from Jerusalem, he will know exactly what he is watching. The building the people trusted was about to be empty.

## The Scroll and the Watchman

Having seen the throne, Ezekiel is sent. He is told to speak to a rebellious house “whether they will hear, or whether they will forbear” (2:5), and not to be afraid of them, though they are “briers and thorns” and “scorpions” (2:6). He is handed a scroll “written within and without,” full of “lamentations, and mourning, and woe” (2:10), and told to eat it. “It was in my mouth as honey for sweetness” (3:3). A message of judgment, and it tasted sweet, because it was Yahuah’s word.

Then Ezekiel sits among the captives “astonished among them seven days” (3:15), and at the end of seven days the word comes: “I have made thee a watchman unto the house of Israel” (3:16-17). Seven days was the length of a priest’s ordination: “ye shall not go out of the door of the tabernacle of the congregation in seven days … for seven days shall he consecrate you” (Leviticus 8:33). The priest who turned thirty with no temple was ordained anyway, by a river in Babylon, not to serve at an altar but to stand watch over the people.

## Bringing It Home

People still trust buildings. A church, a denomination, a place where Yahuah is supposed to live, as if nothing could go wrong while it stands. The first vision says His throne has wheels. He is not held in a building, and He can drive away from one that has filled itself with idols. “Judgment must begin at the house of Elohim” (1 Peter 4:17).

But the same vision is comfort. A believer far from any temple, cut off from the places he was raised to trust, is not cut off from Yahuah. He came to the captives by the river. He is a little sanctuary to His people wherever they have been carried.

And He still makes watchmen the same way: first the throne, then the word eaten, then the message spoken whether anyone listens or not.

## Where It Lands

John saw the same throne. A rainbow around it, a sea of glass like crystal before it, and four living creatures like a lion, a calf, a man, and a flying eagle (Revelation 4:3, 6-7). John was handed a book and told to eat it, sweet as honey in his mouth (Revelation 10:9-10). And at the end the throne stops moving, because it no longer has to come to anyone: “Behold, the tabernacle of Elohim is with men, and he will dwell with them” (Revelation 21:3).
