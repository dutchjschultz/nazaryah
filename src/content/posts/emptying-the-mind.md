## What Came West

Mindfulness is now taught in hospitals, public schools, corporations, the military, and many churches. It is presented as a simple stress tool: sit still, breathe, notice your thoughts, and let them pass without judging them.

The practice has a known birthplace. Mindfulness is the English word for the Pali term *sati*, the seventh step of the Buddha's Noble Eightfold Path, called "right mindfulness." It is the core of *Vipassana*, or insight meditation. In 1979 Jon Kabat-Zinn, a student of Zen and Buddhist teachers, opened a stress clinic at the University of Massachusetts Medical School and taught Buddhist meditation there under a medical name: Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction. He removed the Buddhist vocabulary so it could enter hospitals and schools. The method stayed the same.

## What the Method Teaches

The goal of Buddhist meditation is to watch the mind until the self is seen as empty — no lasting soul, no Creator, only passing thoughts and sensations. Mindfulness trains a person to observe thoughts without weighing them as right or wrong. Even in its clinical form, the instruction is to let every thought come and go with "nonjudgmental awareness."

## What Scripture Calls Meditation

Scripture also commands meditation, but it runs in the opposite direction. The blessed man delights in the law of Yahuah, "and in his law doth he meditate day and night" (Psalm 1:2). Joshua was told the book of the Law "shall not depart out of thy mouth; but thou shalt meditate therein day and night" (Joshua 1:8).

The Hebrew word is *hagah*, to mutter or speak under the breath. It is the low sound of a man going over the words of Yahuah again and again. Biblical meditation is not an empty mind; it is a full one. Its content is the Law.

Nor does Scripture ask the believer to stop judging his thoughts. It asks him to put them on trial: "bringing into captivity every thought to the obedience of Messiah" (2 Corinthians 10:5). Paul gave a list of what to fill the mind with and said, "think on these things" (Philippians 4:8).

## The Verdict

Mindfulness empties the mind and refuses to judge what passes through it. Scripture fills the mind with the Law and judges every thought by it. The two practices share only the act of sitting still. A believer who wants a quiet mind has a better practice already written: speaking the commandments of Yahuah under his breath, day and night.

Revelation opens with the same pattern: "Blessed is he that readeth, and they that hear the words of this prophecy, and keep those things which are written therein" (Revelation 1:3) — reading, hearing, keeping. Nothing is emptied.
