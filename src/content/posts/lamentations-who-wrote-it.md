## What Yahuah Is Doing

Lamentations records the fall of Jerusalem to Babylon in 586 BC and Yahuah keeping the word He gave through Moses: the curse for refusing His commandments (Deuteronomy 28:15) and the way home for those who turn back (Deuteronomy 30:1-3). The message of the book does not depend on who held the pen. Who held the pen is still a fair question, because most English Bibles answer it in the title, and the Hebrew text does not.

## What the Hebrew Text Says

Nothing. The Hebrew book of Lamentations names no writer. It has no opening line saying who spoke it, no closing line signing it, and no verse inside it where the speaker gives a name. The man of chapter 3 says only, “I am the man that hath seen affliction” (3:1).

Its Hebrew title is simply its first word, *Eikhah*, “How!” (1:1). In the Hebrew arrangement of Scripture, it stands among the Writings, with books like Ruth, Ecclesiastes, and Esther, and not among the Prophets next to Jeremiah.

## Where the Name Came From

**The Greek translation.** When the Hebrew Scriptures were put into Greek, the translation known as the Septuagint added an opening line to Lamentations that the Hebrew does not have. In plain words it says that after Israel was carried captive and Jerusalem laid waste, Jeremiah sat weeping and spoke this lament over Jerusalem. That line is the source of the name.

**The new placement.** The Greek Bible also moved the book. It set Lamentations after Jeremiah, with the book of Baruch, Jeremiah’s scribe, between them, so that it read as part of a Jeremiah collection.

**The Latin and English.** The Latin Bible carried the added line forward, and English Bibles took the arrangement from there. The King James Version titles it “The Lamentations of Jeremiah.” The KJV does not print the added line itself, but the title it does print says the same thing.

## The Case for Jeremiah

The tradition did not come from nowhere. There are honest reasons it took hold.

- **He was there.** Jeremiah lived through the siege and the fall, and he was released from prison when the city was taken (Jeremiah 39:11-14).
- **He was a weeper.** “Oh that my head were waters, and mine eyes a fountain of tears, that I might weep day and night for the slain of the daughter of my people” (Jeremiah 9:1).
- **Shared phrases.** Both books speak of eyes running down with tears (Jeremiah 14:17; Lamentations 1:16; 3:48), of the virgin daughter of Zion, and of terror on every side (Jeremiah 6:25; Lamentations 2:22).
- **He wrote laments.** “Jeremiah lamented for Josiah,” and those laments were “written in the lamentations” (2 Chronicles 35:25).

## The Case Against

**The Chronicles verse points elsewhere.** The laments of 2 Chronicles 35:25 were for King Josiah, who died in battle in 609 BC, more than twenty years before Jerusalem fell. The book of Lamentations does not mention Josiah. Whatever collection Chronicles names, it is not this book.

**The book hopes in what Jeremiah warned against.** Several lines speak for a mindset Jeremiah opposed:

- “Our eyes as yet failed for our vain help: in our watching we have watched for a nation that could not save us” (4:17). That nation was Egypt. Jeremiah had told the king that Egypt’s army would turn back home and the Chaldeans would return and burn the city (Jeremiah 37:7-8).
- “The anointed of Yahuah… of whom we said, Under his shadow we shall live among the heathen” (4:20). That was King Zedekiah. Jeremiah told Zedekiah to surrender to Babylon (Jeremiah 38:17-18), and named him among the bad figs (Jeremiah 24:8).
- “Her prophets also find no vision from Yahuah” (2:9). Jeremiah was still receiving the word of Yahuah after the city fell (Jeremiah 42:7).

**The style is different.** Jeremiah’s book is sermons, oracles, and long stretches of history told in prose. Nowhere in its fifty-two chapters does it use an alphabet poem. Lamentations is nothing but tight, crafted poetry, four of its five chapters built letter by letter on the Hebrew alphabet.

**The New Testament is silent.** The apostles name Jeremiah as the prophet behind several quotations (Matthew 2:17; 27:9). None of them ever names the writer of Lamentations.

## What the Record Shows

The Hebrew book is anonymous. The name Jeremiah enters through a line added in the Greek translation, and through a later arrangement that set the book next to his. Some evidence fits him: he was there, he wept, and he shares some phrases with the book. Some evidence does not: the hope in Egypt and in Zedekiah, the prophets with no vision, and a style unlike anything in his own book.

Scripture does not settle the question, and the reader can weigh the record. What Scripture does settle is the message. Whoever held the pen, the book says Yahuah “hath fulfilled his word that he had commanded in the days of old” (2:17).

## Bringing It Home

A title on a page is not the same as a word in the text. Much of what is taught as Bible fact came from a heading, a footnote, or an order of books set by translators, not from the inspired words themselves. The habit worth keeping is the Berean one: search the Scriptures to see whether these things are so (Acts 17:11), and hold the line between what the text says and what was added around it.

## Where It Lands

When Yahuah wanted a writer named, He named him. Revelation opens with the name of the servant who received it: “his servant John” (Revelation 1:1). John names himself again and again, “I John” (Revelation 1:9; 22:8).

Revelation also closes with a warning about the words of the book itself, that no man should add unto them nor take away from them (Revelation 22:18-19). The text is what carries authority, and the text of Lamentations leaves its writer unnamed.
