## A Wall of Thou-Shalt-Nots

Most believers learn the Ten Commandments as a list of rules: things to do, things not to do, and a judge waiting behind each one. Read that way, they feel like a weight to be carried and a test to be failed. Modern teaching then offers relief from that weight by declaring the commandments finished. Both the burden and the relief rest on the same misreading. The Hebrew does not present these words as a threat hanging over the people. It presents them as the shape of a life already rescued and already loved.

Scripture itself never calls them "commandments" in the Hebrew. Moses calls them *aseret ha-devarim*, "the ten words" (Exodus 34:28; Deuteronomy 4:13), from *davar*, a spoken word. They were spoken first, from the mouth of Yahuah to a people He had just carried out of bondage.

## Rescue Before Rule

The first sentence Yahuah speaks at Sinai is not a demand. It is a declaration of what He has already done: "I am Yahuah thy Elohim, which have brought thee out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of bondage" (Exodus 20:2). Deliverance comes first; the words follow. Israel did not keep the ten in order to be brought out of Egypt. They were brought out, and then were told what life with their Deliverer looks like. The order has never changed: deliverance through the blood first, then the walk in the commandments.

The speaker is one. The Hebrew reads *Anokhi*, the singular "I," and the verb "brought thee out" is singular. Whatever is made of the plural form *Elohim*, the One speaking at Sinai speaks as one Person, and the first word that follows forbids any other before His face.

## The Little Word "Not"

Biblical Hebrew has two ways to say "do not." The first is *al* with a short command form, used for an immediate, in-the-moment "stop" or "don't." The second is *lo* with the ordinary imperfect verb, the same form Hebrew uses to state plain future fact: "you will not." Eight of the ten words use *lo*. None of them uses *al*.

That choice changes how the words sound. The *lo* form carries no scolding tone of the moment; it is permanent and settled, and it reads equally well as a promise: "you will not murder," "you will not steal." Isaiah shows both forms side by side in a single verse addressed to Israel as a wife. He opens with *al*, "Fear not," a gentle command for the moment, and then turns to *lo*: "for thou shalt not be ashamed" (Isaiah 54:4). The second phrase is not an order. It is a promise, and the very next verse gives the reason: "thy Maker is thine husband" (Isaiah 54:5). The Ten Words are written in that promise form. On the surface they read as prohibitions; underneath, they describe what the redeemed will not do because of who now holds them.

The covenant at Sinai as a marriage, with the ten as its vows, is taken up in Examined at the Wedding, the third study in this sequence (see Jeremiah 2:2). This study stays with the grammar and with love.

## Two Tables, One Love

When a lawyer asked Yahushua which commandment was greatest, He answered with two, both quoted straight out of the Torah: love Yahuah with all the heart, soul, and might (Deuteronomy 6:5), and love the neighbor as oneself (Leviticus 19:18). On those two, He said, "hang all the law and the prophets" (Matthew 22:36-40). The ten fall into exactly those two parts. The first four describe love toward Yahuah; the last six describe love toward the neighbor. Paul draws the same conclusion: "love is the fulfilling of the law" (Romans 13:10). The ten were never a separate code standing beside love. They are love, spelled out.

Even the warning in the second word is set inside love. Yahuah shows "mercy unto thousands of them that love me, and keep my commandments" (Exodus 20:6). The Hebrew is *ohavai*, "those loving Me", and the mercy is *chesed*, covenant loyalty. Loving and keeping are placed side by side as one thing.

## The Ten Words, One by One

Each word below starts where the ten start, with love. Read this way, every line is not first a rule laid on the people but what love does, or will never do, toward Yahuah and toward a neighbor.

### 1. No Other Gods (Exodus 20:3)

Love wants to be the only one. *Lo yihyeh lekha elohim acherim al-panai*, "there will not be for you other gods before My face," is spoken by a Husband who wants His bride face to face with Him, and a bride who is loved like that has no reason to look for another. Yahushua named it the first and great commandment, to love Yahuah with all the heart (Matthew 22:37-38).

### 2. No Graven Image (Exodus 20:4-6)

Love wants the real Person, not a picture of Him. Yahuah calls Himself *qanna*, jealous, the jealousy of a husband who loves too much to share his bride, and the passage closes on *chesed* to thousands "that love me." A wife whose husband is home does not bow to his photograph, and John writes with the same tenderness: "Little children, keep yourselves from idols" (1 John 5:21).

### 3. The Name Not Carried Empty (Exodus 20:7)

Love gives its name. Yahuah puts His Name upon His people (Numbers 6:27) the way a husband gives his name to his bride, and *lo tissa* uses *nasa*, to carry, with *shav*, emptiness: the bride who treasures that name will not carry it in vain. Yahuah went out to take "a people for his name" (Acts 15:14), and a loved people wear it with honor.

### 4. Remember the Sabbath (Exodus 20:8)

Love sets aside time to be together. *Zakhor*, "remember," marks the seventh day as a standing appointment the Husband keeps open for His bride, and Deuteronomy gives the reason: "remember that thou wast a servant in the land of Egypt" (Deuteronomy 5:15). A people who never had a day off as slaves were handed a day of rest by the One who freed them, and Yahushua says it "was made for man" (Mark 2:27), a gift, not a weight.

### 5. Honor Father and Mother (Exodus 20:12)

Love begins at home. *Kabbed*, from *kaved*, "heavy," means giving real weight to the two people who gave life, and it is the first love a child ever learns. Yahuah answers that love with a promise, long days in the land, which Paul calls "the first commandment with promise" (Ephesians 6:2), a Father rewarding the child who loves his parents.

### 6. No Murder (Exodus 20:13)

Love guards a neighbor's life. *Lo tirtsach* uses *ratsach*, murder, and the one who loves his neighbor will not take what Yahuah made in His own image (Genesis 9:6). Yahushua carries the word down to the anger of the heart (Matthew 5:21-22), and John shows the root on both sides: "Whosoever hateth his brother is a murderer" (1 John 3:15), while love keeps the brother alive.

### 7. No Adultery (Exodus 20:14)

Love keeps its promise. *Lo tin'af* uses *na'af*, the same word Yahuah uses when Israel went after other gods (Jeremiah 3:9), because the marriage at home is a picture of the marriage with Him. The one held by a faithful Husband stays faithful to a spouse, and the marriage bed is kept "undefiled" as something held in honor (Hebrews 13:4).

### 8. No Stealing (Exodus 20:15)

Love gives instead of taking. *Lo tignov* uses *ganav*, taking by stealth, used for stealing hearts as well as goods (Genesis 31:20; 2 Samuel 15:6), and a heart that has been given everything does not reach in secret for what belongs to another. Paul shows where love carries this word: "Let him that stole steal no more: but rather let him labour... that he may have to give to him that needeth" (Ephesians 4:28). Love turns the thief into a giver.

### 9. No False Witness (Exodus 20:16)

Love protects a neighbor's name. *Lo ta'aneh* uses *anah*, to answer, with *ed sheqer*, a witness of falsehood, courtroom language, and the one who loves will not stand up against a neighbor with a lie. Love "rejoiceth not in iniquity, but rejoiceth in the truth" (1 Corinthians 13:6), and it guards a neighbor's good name as carefully as his life.

### 10. No Coveting (Exodus 20:17)

Love is content in the one it loves. *Lo tachmod* uses *chamad*, to desire or delight in, a word that is not evil in itself, since Yahuah Himself "desireth" Zion (Psalm 68:16), and a heart delighted in its Husband does not settle its delight on what belongs to a neighbor. Hebrews ties contentment straight to His love: "be content with such things as ye have: for he hath said, I will never leave thee, nor forsake thee" (Hebrews 13:5). It is the only one of the ten that lives entirely inside the heart, which shows the whole ten were aimed at the heart from the start.

## Not Grievous

The two words without *lo*, remember and honor, stand at the seam between the tables: one closes the love toward Yahuah, the other opens the love toward the neighbor. Around them the eight *lo* words describe a people who will not need other gods, will not murder, will not steal, will not lie, and will not covet, because they have been brought out and are now loved.

John writes it plainly: "this is the love of God, that we keep his commandments: and his commandments are not grievous" (1 John 5:3). The Greek *barus* means heavy, burdensome. The ten were never the weight. They were the description of a freed people walking with the One who freed them.

Between Sinai and the end of the story stands the heart. The tenth word already points there, and the next study in this sequence, Written on the Heart, follows the ten from the tablets of stone to the tablets of flesh.

Revelation ends the story with the same people. The saints are those who keep the commandments of Elohim and hold the belief of Yahushua (Revelation 14:12), and the blessing at the close of the book goes to "they that do his commandments, that they may have right to the tree of life" (Revelation 22:14). What was spoken at Sinai as a promise is found fulfilled at the gates of the city.
