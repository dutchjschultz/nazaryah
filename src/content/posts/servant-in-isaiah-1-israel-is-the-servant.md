## What Yahuah Is Doing

Yahuah means to be known. Not guessed at and not argued over, but known, by every nation on the earth.

His way of doing that has never changed. He puts somebody in place to carry it — somebody who lives it out in the open where the rest of the world can watch.

Isaiah is the record of who was holding that assignment. It changes hands twice before the book is finished, and the whole book is readable once a person knows which hand it is in.

## When This Is Happening

Isaiah covers four stretches of time, in order, front to back.

Chapters 41 and 42 fall in the second stretch, chapters 40 through 48. That is the Babylon captivity and the road home — roughly 605 to 538 BC. The nation has already been carried off. These chapters are spoken to people living in somebody else's country, being told the sentence has been served and they are going home.

## Why People Get Lost Here

The word servant shows up again and again in Isaiah. Most readers assume it means the same person every time. It does not, and that is why these chapters feel slippery.

Isaiah uses the word the way anybody would. A servant works for an owner, carries his name, and does his business out in front of other people. When the servant shows up, the owner is being represented.

So the question in these chapters is never what a servant is. It is always who is holding the assignment.

## Chapter 41 Is a Courtroom

Before Yahuah names anybody, He puts the nations on notice. He calls them in, tells them to bring their case, and dares their gods to say one true thing about the future.

Nobody answers. In the middle of that silence He turns and speaks to one people.

## He Names the Servant Out Loud

> “But thou, Israel, art my servant, Jacob whom I have chosen, the seed of Abraham my friend … Thou art my servant; I have chosen thee, and not cast thee away.”
>
> — Isaiah 41:8–9

A whole nation is handed the assignment, by name. Nothing is hidden in it.

He adds one more thing at the end of it. I have not cast thee away. He says that before anything in the story has gone wrong.

## Who He Is Talking To

It would be easy to picture a strong people here. Isaiah does not allow it.

> “Fear not, thou worm Jacob … I will help thee, saith Yahuah, and thy redeemer, the Holy One of Israel.”
>
> — Isaiah 41:14

A worm. Something small enough to step on without noticing.

Twice in this one chapter He tells them not to be afraid (41:10, 41:14). People who need to be told that twice are not feeling strong.

## What the Assignment Was

The assignment was to show the rest of the world who Yahuah is.

Every other nation had its own gods, its own idols, its own stories. Israel was meant to be the one people on earth living a different way, keeping His commandments, so anybody watching could see what the real Elohim is like.

They were not picked because they were big. They were small, and He says so. They were picked to be the display.

## What Chapter 42 Does With It

The next chapter does two things to what was just handed over.

Somebody else appears — another servant, described in a way that sounds nothing like a worm.

Then, before that same chapter is finished, Yahuah looks at the nation He just chose and calls it blind.

Both are in chapter 42. That is Part Two.

## A Second Line of Evidence

The four stretches of time in Isaiah were not worked out from the servant. They came from somewhere else: one sentence repeated at the close of two of them, and a change of tense between chapters 39 and 40.

The servant is a separate thread, traced a separate way. Where the two threads land on the same seams, the book is witnessing to its own order. By Part Four they do. At the mouth of two witnesses a matter is established (Deuteronomy 19:15).

## In One Sentence

Yahuah handed a small, frightened nation the assignment of showing the world who He is, and told them up front that He had not thrown them away.
