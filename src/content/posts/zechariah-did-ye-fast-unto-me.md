This is not a vision. It is a dated event: the fourth day of the ninth month, Chisleu, in the fourth year of Darius (7:1), late in 518 BC. Nearly two years have passed since the night of the eight visions. The temple is going up, and a practical question comes to the priests and prophets.

## The Account

Men are sent to the house of Yahuah to ask the priests and prophets whether they should keep weeping and fasting in the fifth month, as they have done for seventy years (7:3). Yahuah answers through Zechariah with a question of His own: when you fasted, was it for Me? He reminds them of what the earlier prophets said, judgment, mercy, and care for the weak, and of how their fathers refused to hear it and were scattered. Then He turns to promises. He is coming back to Zion. Old people will sit in the streets again, and children will play there. The fasts will become feasts. And men from every nation will take hold of a Jew and ask to go with him.

## The Fast Nobody Commanded

The fast of the fifth month mourned the day Babylon burned the temple (2 Kings 25:8-9). Chapter 8 names three more: the fourth month, when the city wall was broken (Jeremiah 52:6-7); the seventh, when Gedaliah the governor was murdered (2 Kings 25:25); and the tenth, when the siege began (2 Kings 25:1). The people had kept all four for seventy years.

None of them is in the Law. The only day Yahuah commanded His people to afflict their souls is Yom Kippur (Leviticus 23:27). These four were days men made to remember their own disasters. Now that the temple was being rebuilt, they wanted to know if they could stop.

## Did Ye Fast Unto Me?

Yahuah does not answer yes or no. He asks: “When ye fasted and mourned in the fifth and seventh month, even those seventy years, did ye at all fast unto me, even to me?” And when they ate and drank, “did not ye eat for yourselves” (7:5-6)? The fasting was about their loss, not about Him.

Then He points them back to what He had actually said through “the former prophets” (7:7): “Execute true judgment, and shew mercy and compassions every man to his brother: And oppress not the widow, nor the fatherless, the stranger, nor the poor” (7:9-10). That is straight from the Law (Exodus 22:21-22; Deuteronomy 24:17). Isaiah had said the same thing about fasting a long time before: “Is not this the fast that I have chosen? to loose the bands of wickedness” (Isaiah 58:6).

## A Heart Like Adamant

Zechariah then explains why the captivity came. Their fathers “refused to hearken, and pulled away the shoulder, and stopped their ears” (7:11). Pulling away the shoulder is the picture of an ox that will not take the yoke. They “made their hearts as an adamant stone, lest they should hear the law” (7:12). Adamant was the hardest stone known. The fathers hardened themselves on purpose so the Law could not get in. So Yahuah “scattered them with a whirlwind” (7:14).

## Streets Full Again

Chapter 8 turns to promise. “I am returned unto Zion” (8:3). Jerusalem will be called “a city of truth.” Old men and old women will sit in the streets, each with a staff in his hand for very age, and the streets will be “full of boys and girls playing” (8:4-5). A city that had been empty for seventy years will be full of the very old and the very young again.

Then comes a charge to the builders: “Let your hands be strong” (8:9, 13). And the same Law again: “Speak ye every man the truth to his neighbour; execute the judgment of truth and peace in your gates” (8:16).

## Fasts Into Feasts

Then the answer to the original question: “The fast of the fourth month, and the fast of the fifth, and the fast of the seventh, and the fast of the tenth, shall be to the house of Judah joy and gladness, and cheerful feasts; therefore love the truth and peace” (8:19). Yahuah did not order the fasts kept, and He did not condemn the people for their grief. He promised to turn the mourning into joy, and He tied that joy to loving the truth.

## Ten Men and a Corner

The chapter ends with the nations. “Ten men … out of all languages of the nations, even shall take hold of the skirt of him that is a Jew, saying, We will go with you: for we have heard that Elohim is with you” (8:23).

The word for skirt is *kanaph*, the corner of a garment. It is the word Moses used for the corners where Israel was to wear fringes, so that “ye may look upon it, and remember all the commandments of Yahuah, and do them” (Numbers 15:38-39). The nations take hold of the corner where the commandments hang. A sick woman once did the same thing, reaching for the border of Yahushua’s garment (Matthew 9:20).

## Bringing It Home

Religious calendars are still full of days men made: seasons of giving things up, special fasts, forty-day challenges. Zechariah does not say grief is wrong or that a man may never fast. He asks the question that cuts through all of it: did you do it for Me, or for yourself? And he points away from the days men invented to what Yahuah actually asked for. Justice, mercy, truth, care for the widow and the stranger. Yahushua named the same things when He rebuked the men who tithed their spices carefully and “omitted the weightier matters of the law, judgment, mercy, and faith” (Matthew 23:23).

There is also the matter of the corner. The nations grabbed the man of Judah because they could see that Elohim was with him. A believer who walks in the commandments wears that corner where people can see it. Someone may reach for it.

## Where It Lands

Yahushua said His disciples could not mourn while the Bridegroom was with them, but they would fast when He was taken away (Matthew 9:15). That fast has an end. In Revelation the marriage supper of the Lamb is spread (Revelation 19:9), a multitude from every nation stands before the throne (Revelation 7:9), and “there shall be no more death, neither sorrow, nor crying” (Revelation 21:4). Every fast of mourning becomes a feast.
