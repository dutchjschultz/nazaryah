## The Claim

Most pulpits teach that Ezekiel 28 tells the secret history of Satan. Before the world began, he was a perfect angel, “the anointed cherub that covereth,” who walked in Eden, led heaven’s worship with “tabrets and pipes,” and fell when pride was found in him. Isaiah 14 is laid beside it, and the two chapters together become the life story of Lucifer.

The question is not whether the language is grand. It is. The question is who Ezekiel says he is talking to, and when.

## When and Where

Ezekiel dates this whole section. “In the eleventh year, in the first day of the month” (26:1). That is the eleventh year of the captivity, 586 BC, the year Jerusalem fell to Babylon. Ezekiel is a captive priest living by the river Chebar. The temple has just burned.

The word is against Tyre, the great sea-trading city on the coast north of Israel, built on an island just off the shore. And it begins with Tyre’s reaction to Jerusalem’s fall: “Aha, she is broken that was the gates of the people … I shall be replenished, now she is laid waste” (26:2). Tyre saw a competitor gone and a market opened. That is the setting of chapter 28. It is not before the world began. It is the week Jerusalem burned, and a rich neighbor was counting the profit.

## The Chapters Around It

Chapter 28 is the end of a three-chapter word against one city, and each part is plain about its target.

- **Chapter 26:** Tyre’s fall. Nebuchadnezzar will come against it, and it will become “a place to spread nets upon” (26:5, 7).
- **Chapter 27:** a lament for Tyre pictured as a great merchant ship, loaded with the trade of every nation, sinking in the sea (27:3-36). The chapter lists Tyre’s customers by name, including the merchants who brought “emeralds, purple, and broidered work, and fine linen, and coral, and agate” (27:16).
- **Chapter 28:1-10:** a word to the *prince* of Tyre.
- **Chapter 28:11-19:** a lament for the *king* of Tyre.
- **Chapter 28:20-26:** a word against Sidon, Tyre’s sister city.

Nobody reads chapters 26 or 27 as being about the devil. The ship is Tyre, the merchants are real nations, and the city is real. Chapter 28 is the last movement of the same song.

## The Prince: “Thou Art a Man”

The first half of chapter 28 settles the question before the lament even starts. The prince of Tyre says, “I am El, I sit in the seat of Elohim, in the midst of the seas” (28:2). Tyre was an island city. Its ruler sat “in the midst of the seas” and thought he was untouchable.

Yahuah answers him directly: “yet thou art a man, and not El” (28:2). He is “wiser than Daniel” (28:3), a living man Ezekiel’s hearers knew (14:14). His wisdom made him rich: “by thy great wisdom and by thy traffick hast thou increased thy riches, and thine heart is lifted up because of thy riches” (28:5). And his end will prove it: “thou shalt be a man, and no God, in the hand of him that slayeth thee” (28:9), dying “the deaths of the uncircumcised by the hand of strangers” (28:10).

Teachers who see Satan in this chapter usually agree that verses 1-10 are a man. They say the lament in verses 11-19 switches to the spirit behind him. But Ezekiel uses both titles, prince and king, for Tyre’s ruler, and the second half names the very same sin as the first.

## A Funeral Song, Not a History

Before reading the second half of the chapter, notice what Ezekiel calls it: “take up a lamentation upon the king of Tyrus” (28:12). A lamentation, *qinah*, is a funeral song. Ezekiel sings four of them in his book, and every one is full of pictures. The princes of Judah are a lioness and her cubs (19:1-9). Tyre is a great ship sinking in the sea (27:2-36). The king of Tyre is a jeweled cherub in Eden (28:12-19). Pharaoh is a young lion and a monster in the seas (32:2).

Nobody reads Pharaoh as a real sea monster, or Tyre as a real ship, or Judah’s princes as real lions. David’s funeral song over Saul and Jonathan calls them “swifter than eagles” and “stronger than lions” (2 Samuel 1:17, 23), and nobody thinks they had wings. The cherub and Eden belong to the same kind of song. The king of Tyre was real, and his fall was real. The pictures are how a funeral song honors and mourns a fallen king. This is the key most Christians are never handed: the pulpit reads the pictures of a funeral song as a history of the devil, and loses the man it was sung over.

## The Lament for the King

With that in mind, read each picture of the lament in turn.

## “Thou hast been in Eden”

Eden is Ezekiel’s picture for the height of royal glory, and he uses it for more than one king. Three chapters later he paints Pharaoh as a cedar so great that “all the trees of Eden, that were in the garden of Elohim, envied him” (31:9), and he closes with, “This is Pharaoh and all his multitude” (31:18). Nobody makes Pharaoh the devil. Eden is the language of a king at the top of the world.

## “Every precious stone was thy covering”

The list of stones in 28:13 — sardius, topaz, diamond, beryl, onyx, jasper, sapphire, emerald, carbuncle — matches the stones set in the high priest’s breastplate (Exodus 28:17-20). And Tyre was the trading house for exactly these goods; chapter 27 lists its dealers in precious stones (27:16, 22). The king was clothed in jewels his city bought and sold.

“The workmanship of thy tabrets and of thy pipes was prepared in thee in the day that thou wast created” (28:13). Tabrets and pipes are hand drums and flutes, the music of a coronation and a palace, and Tyre was known for “the noise of thy songs” and “the sound of thy harps” (26:13). The word “created” is used of peoples and kings, not only of angels. Ezekiel tells the Ammonites, “I will judge thee in the place where thou wast created, in the land of thy nativity” (21:30).

## “The anointed cherub that covereth”

“Thou art the anointed cherub that covereth; and I have set thee so: thou wast upon the holy mountain of Elohim” (28:14). Anointed is a king’s word. The covering cherubs stretched their wings over the ark in the holy place (Exodus 25:20). Here is the history the picture fits: Tyre once stood guard over Yahuah’s holy mountain. Hiram king of Tyre sent the cedar and the craftsmen that built Solomon’s temple on Mount Zion (1 Kings 5:1-10), and Tyre and Israel had a covenant of brothers. Amos condemned Tyre because it “remembered not the brotherly covenant” (Amos 1:9). The king who once covered the holy mountain turned on it.

## “Perfect in thy ways … till iniquity was found in thee”

“Perfect,” *tamim*, means blameless, upright. It is the word used of Noah (Genesis 6:9). It does not mean a sinless angel. It means a ruler who started well.

## The sin is named

Then Ezekiel says exactly what went wrong: “By the multitude of thy merchandise they have filled the midst of thee with violence, and thou hast sinned” (28:16). His heart was lifted up because of his beauty (28:17), and he “defiled thy sanctuaries by the multitude of thine iniquities, by the iniquity of thy traffick” (28:18). Merchandise. Traffic. Trade. The sin of the covering cherub is the same sin as the prince in verse 5: getting rich and getting proud. The devil does not run a shipping business.

## The end is named

“I will cast thee to the ground, I will lay thee before kings … I will bring thee to ashes upon the earth in the sight of all them that behold thee … never shalt thou be any more” (28:17-19). A king brought down in front of other kings and burned to ashes on earth while people watch. That is the fall of a man and a city. Satan’s end in Scripture is different: he is cast into the lake of fire (Revelation 20:10).

History followed the word. Nebuchadnezzar besieged Tyre for years after 586 BC, and Ezekiel himself records that his army worked so hard against it that “every head was made bald, and every shoulder was peeled” (29:18). The island city was finally broken by Alexander in 332 BC.

## Two Kings, One Story

Ezekiel’s king of Tyre and Isaiah’s king of Babylon are told the same way: a proud king, a boast that reaches to heaven, a fall to the ground in front of other kings. ## Bringing It Home

This is where the pulpit’s reading costs the most. Make the chapter about a fallen angel before time, and it becomes a curiosity with nothing to say to anyone in the pew. Read it as Ezekiel wrote it, and it is a warning aimed straight at us.

Tyre’s king was blessed, gifted, set beside the holy mountain, and once helped build Yahuah’s house. Then money did its work. The trade grew, the pride grew, and he defiled the sanctuaries “by the iniquity of thy traffick.” That is not an ancient problem. Religion that becomes a business, preachers who say wealth proves Yahuah’s favor, and believers whose hearts are lifted up by what they own are walking the king of Tyre’s road. Yahushua drove the traders out of His Father’s house for the same reason: “make not my Father’s house an house of merchandise” (John 2:16).

And the sentence on the man who says “I sit in the seat of Elohim” still stands: “thou art a man, and not El.”

## Where It Lands

Revelation takes Ezekiel’s lament for Tyre and sings it over Babylon the great. The merchants of the earth weep because no one buys their goods anymore, and the list of cargo runs from gold and precious stones to “the souls of men” (Revelation 18:11-13). The shipmasters stand far off, cast dust on their heads, and cry, “What city is like unto this great city!” (Revelation 18:17-19), the same words and the same dust as Tyre’s sailors in Ezekiel 27:29-32. The king of Tyre was never the devil’s autobiography. He was a first picture of the merchant city that falls at the end.
