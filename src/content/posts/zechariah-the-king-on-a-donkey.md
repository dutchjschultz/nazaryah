The visions are over and the dates stop. Chapter 9 opens with a new heading: “The burden of the word of Yahuah” (9:1). A burden, *massa*, is a heavy message handed to the prophet to carry. This first burden runs from the Greek conquest of the coast, about 332 BC, to the Shepherd sold for silver in 31 AD. It is long and full of pictures, so it is taken in two parts. Part One covers chapters 9 and 10: the conqueror, the King, and the gathering of the flock.

## The Burden, Part One

Judgment sweeps down the coast from Syria through Tyre and the Philistine cities, while Yahuah camps around His own house. Then the King comes to Jerusalem, righteous and lowly, riding a donkey, and speaks peace to the nations. Prisoners are sent up out of a dry pit by the blood of the covenant and called to the stronghold. Judah and Ephraim are bent like a bow against the sons of Greece. The people wander like sheep with no shepherd, so Yahuah promises to whistle for them and gather them, and out of Judah will come the corner, the nail, and the battle bow.

## The Conqueror Comes Down the Coast

The burden names its targets in order: Hadrach and Damascus in Syria, then Hamath, Tyre and Sidon, then Ashkelon, Gaza, Ekron, and Ashdod (9:1-6). Tyre “did build herself a strong hold, and heaped up silver as the dust,” and yet “she shall be devoured with fire” (9:3-4).

History walked that same road. In 332 BC Alexander the Great came down the coast from Syria. Tyre sat on an island and thought it could not be taken, so he built a causeway out to it, broke the walls, and burned the city. Gaza fell to him next. History does not prove the prophecy, but it lines up with it town by town.

Two things in the middle of the judgment matter. First, the Philistine who is left “shall be for our Elohim, and he shall be as a governor in Judah, and Ekron as a Jebusite” (9:7). The Jebusites were the old owners of Jerusalem who ended up living among Israel. Even the Philistine remnant is brought in. Second, Yahuah says, “I will encamp about mine house because of the army” (9:8). Tyre and Gaza burned. Jerusalem and its new temple were not destroyed.

## The King on a Donkey

Right after the conqueror comes the King.

> Rejoice greatly, O daughter of Zion … behold, thy King cometh unto thee: he is just, and having salvation; lowly, and riding upon an ass.
>
> — Zechariah 9:9

Conquerors rode warhorses. This King rides a donkey, the animal of peace. When David made Solomon king, he set him on David’s own mule (1 Kings 1:33). Jacob had foretold that Judah would bind “his ass’s colt unto the choice vine” (Genesis 49:11). Yahushua rode into Jerusalem on exactly this verse, and Matthew and John both quote it (Matthew 21:5; John 12:15).

Notice what the King does when He arrives. He cuts off the chariot and the war horse and the battle bow, and “he shall speak peace unto the heathen,” with a dominion “from sea even to sea” (9:10). His kingdom spreads by peace spoken to the nations, not by the sword.

## Prisoners of Hope

“As for thee also, by the blood of thy covenant I have sent forth thy prisoners out of the pit wherein is no water” (9:11). A pit with no water was a dry cistern used as a prison. Joseph was thrown into one: “the pit was empty, there was no water in it” (Genesis 37:24). Jeremiah was lowered into another (Jeremiah 38:6).

The way out is “the blood of thy covenant.” Moses sprinkled the people with “the blood of the covenant” at Sinai (Exodus 24:8). Yahushua lifted the cup and said, “this is my blood of the new testament” (Matthew 26:28), and Hebrews speaks of “the blood of the everlasting covenant” (Hebrews 13:20). Then the call: “Turn you to the strong hold, ye prisoners of hope” (9:12). They are still called prisoners, but they are prisoners with hope, and the stronghold is open to them.

## Judah the Bow, Ephraim the Arrow

“When I have bent Judah for me, filled the bow with Ephraim, and raised up thy sons, O Zion, against thy sons, O Greece” (9:13). After Alexander died, Greek kings ruled the land, and in the second century BC Judah fought them for its life. Zechariah saw it centuries ahead. Notice that both houses are in Yahuah’s hand together: Judah is the bow and Ephraim is the arrow. Yahuah is the archer, His arrow goes out “as the lightning,” and He blows the trumpet Himself (9:14). Afterward His people “shall be as the stones of a crown, lifted up as an ensign upon his land” (9:16).

## Sheep Without a Shepherd

Chapter 10 opens with the Law’s promise of rain: “Ask ye of Yahuah rain in the time of the latter rain” (10:1; Deuteronomy 11:14). Then the problem: “the idols have spoken vanity, and the diviners have seen a lie … therefore they went their way as a flock, they were troubled, because there was no shepherd” (10:2). Yahuah is angry at the shepherds who let it happen (10:3).

Out of Judah comes the answer: “Out of him came forth the corner, out of him the nail, out of him the battle bow” (10:4). The corner is the cornerstone the builders rejected (Psalm 118:22). The nail is a tent peg driven into a sure place. Isaiah used it for the steward who held the key of the house of David: “I will fasten him as a nail in a sure place” (Isaiah 22:22-23). Revelation gives that key to the One who is holy and true (Revelation 3:7).

Then Yahuah promises to gather the scattered: “I will hiss for them, and gather them; for I have redeemed them” (10:8). To hiss is to whistle, the call Isaiah uses for summoning peoples from far away (Isaiah 5:26), the way a shepherd calls his flock home. And the scattering has a purpose: “I will sow them among the people: and they shall remember me in far countries” (10:9). Scattered seed is not lost seed. The chapter ends with both houses brought home, Judah strengthened and Joseph saved (10:6), and “they shall walk up and down in his name” (10:12).

## Bringing It Home

Part One puts two questions in front of every believer.

Which king do you expect? The world wanted a conqueror on a warhorse, and many in Jerusalem wanted one too. The King came lowly, on a donkey, speaking peace. He still builds His kingdom that way, by peace spoken to the nations and not by the sword. A believer looking for a political strongman to rescue him is looking for Alexander, not for the King of Zion.

Where were you when He found you? In a pit with no water. The way out was not climbing. It was the blood of the covenant. But the call does not end there: “Turn you to the strong hold, ye prisoners of hope.” The prisoner who is set free still has to walk to the stronghold, and the gathered flock “shall walk up and down in his name” (10:12). Entry by the blood, then the walk.

## Where It Lands

The King came the first time on a donkey. Revelation shows Him coming the second time on a white horse, called Faithful and True, with “KING OF KINGS” written on His robe (Revelation 19:11-16). And the scattered seed of 10:9 comes home as “a great multitude, which no man could number, of all nations, and kindreds, and people, and tongues,” standing before the throne (Revelation 7:9).

Part Two picks up in chapter 11 with the Shepherd and the thirty pieces of silver.
