## What the Pulpit Does With This Parable

The parable is four verses long.

> So is the kingdom of Yahuah, as if a man should cast seed into the ground; And should sleep, and rise night and day, and the seed should spring and grow up, he knoweth not how. For the earth bringeth forth fruit of herself; first the blade, then the ear, after that the full corn in the ear. But when the fruit is brought forth, immediately he putteth in the sickle, because the harvest is come. — Mark 4:26–29

Modern preaching reads it as patience in ministry. A man hands the message to someone, goes back to his own life, Yahuah does the rest, and one day there is a harvest. Read fast, that sounds like the text. It comes apart line by line.

Verse 26. The seed goes into the ground. In modern teaching the entrance is a moment — a man accepts the Messiah, is saved, and the matter is closed. A closed transaction has no stages. This parable has three.

Verse 27. The sower sleeps and rises and does not know how. The whole trade built on this parable is measurement: decisions counted, converts tracked, discipleship graded in levels, growth reported to a board. The verse says no man can see the field while it is working. The teaching that leans hardest on this parable contradicts its middle clause.

Verse 28. Blade, ear, full grain. This is where the reading dies. Something is growing, in stages, over time, toward a standing crop. Ask a modern pulpit what that something is, and its own system has no answer. The law has been declared set aside, so the word that went in has no instruction left in it to grow into a way of living. What gets put in the blade instead? Feelings. Closeness. Attendance. Giving. "Maturity," which is never defined. None of it is in the text, and none of it can be cut and carried off a field.

The same verse supplies the cover. "Of herself" is preached to mean the word works automatically and nothing is asked of the man it went into. That phrase is used to hide the hole, not to explain it.

Verse 29. If the fruit is converts, then the harvest is evangelistic success and the sickle is a happy ingathering. Yahushua took that sentence out of Joel 3:13, where the sickle goes in because wickedness has come to full size. No man puts a blade to his own converts.

So the trouble is not that the modern reading gets one point wrong. It is that there is nothing to put in the middle of it. That is why this parable is almost never preached, and why a person can sit through all four verses and come away with nothing. The parable is about what grows after the entrance. A system that has only an entrance has nothing to say here.

## The Scene That Produced It

The parable is an answer, and the thing it answers happened in the chapter before.

Yahushua healed a man on the Sabbath. The Pharisees went out and took counsel with the Herodians on how to destroy Him (Mark 3:6) — over a healing, on a day they were policing. Scribes came down from Jerusalem and said the work in Him belonged to Beelzebub (Mark 3:22). Then He defined His own household: whoever does the will of Yahuah is His brother and sister and mother (Mark 3:35). Doing.

The next verse puts Him in a boat, teaching the crowd in parables (Mark 4:1–2). The order is the argument. The parables begin the moment the men who held the law have declared against Him.

## Why He Began Speaking in Parables

He gives the reason and does not soften it. To those outside, everything comes in parables, so that seeing they may see and not perceive, and hearing they may hear and not understand (Mark 4:11–12). That is Isaiah 6:9–10, spoken over a nation that held the word of Yahuah and would not do it.

Parables were not a device for making hard things easy. They were a sentence handed down on men who had the light and refused it. So this parable is spoken into a rejection already under way, and its images are aimed at the men doing the rejecting.

## What the Rejection Actually Was

They did not deny the law. They handled it all day. What they had done was move it — out of a man and onto the outside of him, where it could be inspected, enforced, and added to.

Isaiah had named that failure exactly: the people draw near with the mouth and honour with the lips, but the heart is far off, and their fear of Him is taught by the rules of men (Isaiah 29:13). Yahushua threw that verse at these same men and told them what they were doing with it — laying aside the commandment of Yahuah to keep their own handed-down tradition (Mark 7:6–9).

A law kept on the outside of a man can be graded by another man. That was the business, and this parable ends it.

## What the Hebrew Scriptures Said the Word Was For

Moses closed the covenant with the opposite claim. The commandment is not hidden, not in heaven, not across the sea, not something a man must go and fetch:

> But the word is very nigh unto thee, in thy mouth, and in thy heart, that thou mayest do it. — Deuteronomy 30:14

In the mouth and in the heart, and stated for one purpose — so that it gets done. The same instruction runs through the Shema: these words which I command thee this day shall be in thine heart, and taught to the children in the house and on the road (Deuteronomy 6:6–7). The Writings say it the same way: the law of his God is in his heart, and none of his steps slide (Psalm 37:31); the word is hidden in the heart so the man will not sin (Psalm 119:11). Jeremiah promised the finish of it — Yahuah puts His law in their inward parts and writes it on their hearts (Jeremiah 31:33).

The law was always meant to be planted in a man. It was never meant to be posted over him.

That is the seed.

## The Seed Is Already Named

No hunting is required. Yahushua gave the meaning fifteen verses earlier, in the same sitting, to the same men.

> The sower soweth the word. — Mark 4:14

The word of Yahuah is His instruction. The commandment is a lamp and the law is light (Proverbs 6:23), and a teaching with no law in it has no light in it (Isaiah 8:20). So the thing going into the ground is not a feeling and not a decision. It is what Yahuah said, planted in a man to be lived — which is what Moses said the word was for.

The crowd on that shore already had the picture. Isaiah had put rain, earth, seed, and word into one image, and said the word does not return void but accomplishes what Yahuah pleases (Isaiah 55:10–11). Note what Isaiah does not say. He does not say the word accomplishes what the sower pleases.

## The Man Who Sows Cannot Watch It

The man sleeps. He rises. Night and day pass, the seed grows, and he knows not how (Mark 4:27). Yahuah neither slumbers nor sleeps (Psalm 121:4), and nothing about Him or His Son is unknowing. The man is a servant carrying the word to someone else.

That one line cancels the trade of the men standing in front of Him. If the word goes into the ground and grows out of sight, no overseer can stand at the edge of another man's field and grade the crop. The servant can put the seed in. He cannot watch it work, cannot force it, cannot hurry it, and cannot measure it.

Solomon said it to a farmer. A man does not know the way of the wind or how bones are formed in the womb, and no more does he know the works of Yahuah who makes all — so sow in the morning and do not hold back the hand in the evening, because a man does not know which sowing will prosper (Ecclesiastes 11:5–6).

## What Is Actually Growing

The three stages are stages of one thing — fruit. In the parable told minutes earlier, the good ground hears the word, receives it, and brings forth fruit (Mark 4:20). Fruit is not the hearing. Fruit is the doing that comes out of the hearing.

So the stages can be named plainly. The blade is a man in whom Yahuah's instruction has taken hold — he has heard it and it is alive in him. The ear is that instruction forming into a way of living: what he eats, which day he stops, how he treats the man next to him, what he will no longer do. The full grain is a life that does what Yahuah said all the way through, which is what Moses put the word in the heart for.

That is why it comes in stages. Obedience does not arrive whole. A man does not hear the Sabbath, the appointed times, the clean and the unclean, and the whole walk in one afternoon and have it standing full by evening. It comes up out of him over time, and the growing is Yahuah's working, not the sower's.

This is also the exact point a lawless system cannot reach. Declare the instruction void and there is nothing in the seed that could grow into a way of living, so the blade and the ear have to be filled with something invented.

One more thing the verse does not say. "Of herself" means the sower's hand is off it — not that the ground owes nothing. Yahuah tied the yield of a field to a condition and stated it plainly:

> If ye walk in my statutes, and keep my commandments, and do them; Then I will give you rain in due season, and the land shall yield her increase, and the trees of the field shall yield their fruit. — Leviticus 26:3–4

The terms are walking in what He laid down and doing it. Deuteronomy repeats them — listen to the commandments and do them and He sends the early and the latter rain and the grain is gathered in; turn aside and He shuts the sky and there is no harvest at all (Deuteronomy 11:13–17).

The men watching Him wanted a blade they could count. Yahuah waits for grain.

## The Sickle Is Joel's Sickle

Then the last verse turns. The fruit is brought forth, and immediately the sickle goes in, because the harvest is come (Mark 4:29). That is not general farm talk. It is a prophet's line, and the hearers knew whose:

> Put ye in the sickle, for the harvest is ripe: come, get you down; for the press is full, the fats overflow; for their wickedness is great. — Joel 3:13

Joel is not describing a gathering of the faithful. He is describing a field of people whose wickedness has come to full size, and the next words are multitudes in the valley of decision, because the day of Yahuah is near (Joel 3:14).

Joel gets quoted constantly in the very churches that skip this verse. Joel's army — an end-time company of overcoming believers who bring in the kingdom — came out of the 1948 revival movements and is built on the strong people of Joel 2. Joel names that army himself: it is Yahuah's own force, sent against a covenant-breaking people, and the question standing in the middle of it is who is able to abide the day (Joel 2:11). The one verse of Joel that Yahushua actually quoted is the one they never read.

Ripeness is not the reward. Ripeness is the signal. And the timing belongs to no servant — the text gives the reason for the cutting, because the harvest is come.

## What Happened When a Field Was Measured Before

Isaiah sang of a vineyard given every advantage, and when the owner came looking for good grapes it brought forth wild ones. He looked for judgment and found bloodshed, for righteousness and found a cry (Isaiah 5:1–7). The field had everything except the crop.

Amos was shown a basket of summer fruit, ripe and at its best, and the meaning he was given was not blessing:

> And behold a basket of summer fruit … Then said Yahuah unto me, The end is come upon my people of Israel; I will not again pass by them any more. — Amos 8:1–2

Ripe meant finished. Hosea put it in the language of a failed crop — they sowed wind and reaped storm, the stalk has no head on it, and it will yield no meal (Hosea 8:7). Jeremiah gave the words of a people who worked out too late what the season had been:

> The harvest is past, the summer is ended, and we are not saved. — Jeremiah 8:20

Every one of those fields had the word. What none of them had was the doing.

## The Objection

The push-back is that Mark 4:29 is ordinary farming language, and that Joel is being read into a picture meant to teach patience and quiet growth.

It does not hold. Joel 3:13 is the one place in the Hebrew Scriptures where the sickle is commanded in because a harvest has ripened, and Mark 4:29 carries that command almost word for word. A crowd raised on the prophets did not hear a farming proverb at the end of that sentence. They heard the valley.

## The Verdict

The parable was spoken to men who had taken the law off the heart and hung it on the outside of the nation, where they could measure it and charge for the reading. Yahushua answered them with a field.

The word goes into a man. What grows is that word turning into his walk — blade, ear, and full grain, obedience coming up over time. It grows by Yahuah's own working, out of sight, on a schedule no one is shown, and the servant who carried the seed can neither force it nor grade it. And when the crop stands to its full height the sickle goes in at once, because the harvest has come.

Modern preaching keeps the first half as reassurance, has nothing to name in the middle, and quotes Joel's army while stepping over Joel's sickle. The comfort in this parable belongs to the sower, who was never carrying the weight of the growing. The warning belongs to the ground.

The question left standing is not how the field is coming along. It is what will be found standing in it on the day the blade goes in.
