## What This Parable Says It Is About

A man scatters seed across a field and goes home. He sleeps that night, gets up the next morning, and does the same the night after that. The seed comes up out of the ground, and the parable says in plain words that the man has no idea how it happened. Four verses long, and it ends with a blade going into that field.

The opening line says what the whole thing is about, and it is the line almost nobody reads out loud: *So is the kingdom of Yahuah.*

The parable told immediately after it opens the same way — *Whereunto shall we liken the kingdom of Yahuah?* (Mark 4:30). Two parables in a row, each naming its subject in its first words. Where a parable states its own subject, that statement caps what the parable can be made to say. The subject is the kingdom.

## How a Parable Is Read

Asaph said the method out loud: *I will open my mouth in a parable; I will utter dark sayings of old* (Psalm 78:2). Matthew quotes that line and says it was fulfilled in the teaching of Yahushua (Matthew 13:35). A dark saying is *chidah*, a riddle. Of old means the answer was already written.

So the parable is the riddle and the older Scriptures are the answer key. Every image in these parables was defined before Yahushua ever used it, in the books the crowd standing in front of Him already owned. And no prophecy of the Scripture is of any private interpretation (2 Peter 1:20). A parable is not open ground. It is not grounds for a new doctrine, and it is not a prop under an old one that cannot stand on the plain word.

That rule does the deciding in what follows. Where the Hebrew Scriptures define an image, that settles it. Where they say nothing, nothing is said here.

## The Scene on the Shore

The crowd pressing in was so large that Yahushua got into a boat and pushed out onto the water to teach from it, with the people standing at the water's edge (Mark 4:1).

What the record says about the hours before that is not softened anywhere. He had healed a man on the Sabbath, and the Pharisees took counsel with the Herodians how they might destroy Him (Mark 3:6). Scribes came down from Jerusalem and said He had an unclean spirit (Mark 3:22, 30). His own kin went out to lay hold on Him, saying He was beside Himself (Mark 3:21). The parables begin on the next page.

## The Question That Set the Terms

Those around Him with the twelve asked about the parable He had just told, and His answer sets the terms for everything taught in that sitting. To those outside, all things come in parables; to those given it, the mystery of the kingdom of Yahuah is given (Mark 4:10–11). Then He asks them one back:

> Know ye not this parable? and how then will ye know all parables? — Mark 4:13

That sentence licenses carrying the sower's definitions across, and only those. And in the sower He says what the seed is — *the sower soweth the word* (Mark 4:14) — and what the four grounds are, and every one of them is a man who hears.

Between that parable and this one He says two more things to the same crowd. A candle is not lit to be put under a bushel or a bed; it is lit to be set up where it can be seen. And there is nothing hid which shall not be manifested, neither was any thing kept secret but that it should come abroad (Mark 4:21–22). The same speaker, in the same sitting, to the same people, has already said that hiding is a season with an end on it.

## The Parable

> So is the kingdom of Yahuah, as if a man should cast seed into the ground; And should sleep, and rise night and day, and the seed should spring and grow up, he knoweth not how. For the earth bringeth forth fruit of herself; first the blade, then the ear, after that the full corn in the ear. But when the fruit is brought forth, immediately he putteth in the sickle, because the harvest is come. — Mark 4:26–29

In plain words. A man puts seed in the ground and goes about his life. The seed grows without him, by a process he cannot see and does not understand, coming up in stages until the grain stands full. And the moment it is ready, the blade goes in, because the harvest has arrived.

Each piece is taken below in the order the parable gives it.

## The Kingdom

The Hebrew Scriptures do not leave that term open. Yahuah has prepared His throne in the heavens, and His kingdom rules over all (Psalm 103:19). His kingdom is an everlasting kingdom (Psalm 145:13).

And Daniel was shown it directly. A stone was cut out of the mountain without hands — no quarry crew, no tools, no man's arm in it. That stone struck the image of the kingdoms of men and broke them, and became a great mountain that filled the whole earth (Daniel 2:34–35). The meaning was given with it:

> And in the days of these kings shall the God of heaven set up a kingdom, which shall never be destroyed: and the kingdom shall not be left to other people, but it shall break in pieces and consume all these kingdoms, and it shall stand for ever. — Daniel 2:44

Cut out without hands. That is the kingdom those hearers already knew about. Something Yahuah sets up Himself, that no man quarries and no man stops, that starts the size of a stone and ends the size of the earth. Every image in this parable fits that and nothing else.

## The Man Who Casts the Seed

The parable does not name him. It says what he does and nothing else, and the Hebrew Scriptures supply no name for him either. So no name is supplied here.

What is known is who sows in the Hebrew Scriptures. *I will sow her unto me in the earth*, Yahuah says through Hosea (Hosea 2:23). *I will sow the house of Israel and the house of Judah with the seed of man*, He says through Jeremiah (Jeremiah 31:27). Where the Scriptures name the one who sows a people into a land, it is Yahuah.

## The Seed

This is the one term defined on the spot: the sower sows the word. And Isaiah had already put the word and the seed into one picture:

> For as the rain cometh down, and the snow from heaven, and returneth not thither, but watereth the earth, and maketh it bring forth and bud, that it may give seed to the sower, and bread to the eater: So shall my word be that goeth forth out of my mouth: it shall not return unto me void, but it shall accomplish that which I please, and it shall prosper in the thing whereto I sent it. — Isaiah 55:10–11

That line names whose purpose is served. It accomplishes what Yahuah pleases. It does not say it accomplishes what the sower pleases.

## The Ground, the Sleeping, and the Not Knowing

The earth bringing forth is the language of the creation account. Yahuah said let the earth bring forth grass and the herb yielding seed, and the earth brought it forth (Genesis 1:11–12). The ground produced because He had spoken into it, and there was no man in the field when it happened.

The sleeping and the rising, night and day, carries no figure anywhere in the older Scriptures. A farmer sleeps and gets up because that is what a farmer does between planting and harvest. It marks time passing, and the sower being somewhere else while it passes. Nothing more can be drawn out of it, so nothing more is.

Then the line that he knows not how. Solomon had already said it, and he said it to a farmer. A man does not know the way of the wind, nor how the bones grow in the womb, and no more does he know the works of Yahuah who makes all of them — so sow in the morning, and in the evening do not hold back your hand, because a man does not know which sowing will prosper (Ecclesiastes 11:5–6). The not knowing is not a fault in the man. It is the condition of the work, because the works of Yahuah are not open to a man's inspection.

## Of Herself

The Greek word is *automatē* — of its own accord, with no hand on it. The Greek Scriptures of the Hebrew text use it only a handful of times, and almost always of something happening with no human hand in it at all.

Two of those sit in the law of Moses, where the word carries the Hebrew *saphiach* — the growth that comes up on its own from last year's dropped grain. In the sabbath year, and again in the jubilee, that crop is named and the owner of the field is forbidden to reap it:

> That which groweth of its own accord of thy harvest thou shalt not reap, neither gather the grapes of thy vine undressed: for it is a year of rest unto the land. — Leviticus 25:5

It stands in his own field and he may not put a blade to it.

Another is the sign given to Hezekiah. Eat this year what grows of itself, and the second year what springs of the same, and in the third year sow and reap. And the words that follow name what the sign meant: the remnant that escapes shall take root downward and bear fruit upward, and the zeal of Yahuah of hosts shall do this (2 Kings 19:29–31; Isaiah 37:30–32).

So of herself does not mean that nothing is asked of anyone. It means the growing is Yahuah's own doing and no man's hand is on it — and in the law it marks a crop that belongs to Him rather than to the man who owns the ground.

## Blade, Ear, Full Corn

These are the Torah's own harvest words. The green ear is *aviv*, the stage the barley had reached when it was struck in Egypt (Exodus 9:31) and the stage named for the firstfruits offering. The full ear is *carmel*, and the two stand side by side in the law of the meal offering (Leviticus 2:14). The sequence from green to full is how the Scriptures describe a grain crop ripening toward its harvest.

What the Hebrew Scriptures never do is turn those three into three stages of a man's walk. That search comes back empty. The stages mark a crop getting ready, and a harvest that cannot be brought in until it is.

## The Sickle Is a Quotation

The last line is not farming language. It is a quotation, and the crowd knew whose.

> Put ye in the sickle, for the harvest is ripe: come, get you down; for the press is full, the fats overflow; for their wickedness is great. — Joel 3:13

Joel reads, send out the sickle, for the harvest stands ready. The parable reads, he sends the sickle, for the harvest stands ready. The verb is the same, the sickle is the same, and the words for standing ready are the same. Only the name of the crop is changed.

In Joel that sickle goes in for a stated reason — the press is full and the vats overflow, because the wickedness is great — and the next words out of him are multitudes in the valley of decision, because the day of Yahuah is near (Joel 3:14).

The Hebrew word for that sickle is *maggal*, and it appears twice in the whole of the Hebrew Scriptures. Once in Joel. Once in Jeremiah, of the man who handles the sickle in the harvest of Babylon (Jeremiah 50:16). Both are harvests of judgment. There is no third place to go. The Torah's ordinary harvest sickle is a different word altogether, *hermesh* (Deuteronomy 16:9; 23:25), and the two should not be run together.

Joel also gets quoted constantly in the churches that skip this verse. Joel's army — an end-time company of overcoming believers who bring in the kingdom — came out of the revival movements of 1948 and is built on the strong people of Joel 2. Joel names that army himself: it is Yahuah's own force, sent against a covenant-breaking people, and the question standing in the middle of it is who is able to abide the day (Joel 2:11). The one verse of Joel that Yahushua actually quoted is the one they never read.

## The Parable Spoken Right After

*Whereunto shall we liken the kingdom of Yahuah?* A grain of mustard seed, less than all the seeds in the earth when it is sown, which grows up and becomes greater than all herbs and puts out great branches, so that the fowls of the air lodge under the shadow of it (Mark 4:30–32).

That picture is Ezekiel's. Yahuah takes a tender twig, plants it on a high mountain, and makes it a goodly cedar, and under it dwells all fowl of every wing. And that passage closes with the only answer the Hebrew Scriptures give to a man who does not know how it happened:

> I Yahuah have spoken and have done it. — Ezekiel 17:24

Two parables in a row, the same subject named in both, and the same shape in both.

## Where the Preached Reading Comes Apart

The parable is taught as advice to a worker about being patient with slow results. Four things are wrong with that before a single image is argued.

It answers the wrong question. The question actually on the table that day was why He teaches in parables at all, and this parable answers it by naming the kingdom in its first line.

It is aimed at the wrong audience. It is preached to ministry workers. The people in front of Him were a crowd on a shore, and men who had just said His works came from an unclean spirit.

It splits one sitting into separate occasions. These parables get taught as separate lessons with separate subjects, gathered as though from different days. One boat, one crowd, one sitting — and He states that the first of them is the key to the rest.

And it argues in a circle. The fruit is said to be converts because the harvest is evangelism, and the harvest is said to be evangelism because the fruit is converts. Neither is established from anywhere except the other.

## What the Parable Says

Put the pieces back together and it says one thing, in the order it was given.

The kingdom of Yahuah is His own planting, the stone no hand cut out of the mountain. The word sown is His word, and it performs His purpose and not the purpose of whoever carried it. The ground produces because He spoke into it, the way the earth brought forth at the beginning. The man who sowed sleeps and rises and is not present for the work, and his not knowing how it happens is the condition of the whole business. What comes up, comes up of itself — which in the law of Moses is the crop a man is forbidden to claim. It ripens in its own stages toward a harvest that cannot be hurried. And the instant it stands ready, the blade goes in, and the blade is the one Joel named.

Which answers the men standing on that shore, measuring Him by what they could see and finding nothing they recognised as a kingdom. It was already sown, already growing where they could not watch it or stop it, and the day it would be seen by everyone was a day set by Yahuah and not by them.

## It Is Not the Last Time the Sentence Is Spoken

The line Yahushua quoted does not end in this parable. It is spoken once more at the close of the Revelation, where one like the Son of man sits on a white cloud with a sharp sickle, and the word goes out:

> Thrust in thy sickle, and reap: for the time is come for thee to reap; for the harvest of the earth is ripe. — Revelation 14:15
