Many Bible readers meet Leviathan in the book of Job and move on, as if he were a strange sea monster with no meaning. He is not a loose end. Scripture shows who he is, what he rules, and how he ends. The answer is simple: Leviathan is the old serpent from the garden, reigning as king over the proud.

## Who He Is

The story starts in the garden. The serpent offered Eve pride in one short line: “ye shall be as gods” (Genesis 3:5). The Hebrew word for that serpent is *nachash*.

Isaiah uses the same word for Leviathan. He calls him “the piercing serpent,” “that crooked serpent,” and “the dragon that is in the sea” (Isaiah 27:1). In one verse, Leviathan is both serpent and dragon.

Even his name fits. *Leviathan* comes from the Hebrew root *lavah*, which means to join or twine. The name means something like “the coiled one,” and Isaiah calls him “crooked.” Levi’s name comes from the same root. Leah named him hoping her husband would “be joined” to her (Genesis 29:34), and the tribe of Levi was “joined” to Aaron to serve at the tabernacle (Numbers 18:2, 4). Scripture does not draw this contrast itself, but the words are worth noticing: Levi is joined in service to Yahuah, while Leviathan is coiled up in himself.

Revelation uses the same two names: “the great dragon” is “that old serpent, called the Devil, and Satan” (Revelation 12:9). Revelation does not quote Isaiah here, but the old Greek translation of Isaiah 27:1 uses the same two Greek words for dragon and serpent, and both books end with this enemy destroyed. The garden serpent, Isaiah’s Leviathan, and Revelation’s dragon are one enemy.

## What He Rules

Job says it plainly: Leviathan is “a king over all the children of pride” (Job 41:34). He is not pride itself. He is the king, and the proud are his people. Yahuah points Job to “every one that is proud” (Job 40:11-12), and the speech ends with the king of the proud.

That explains the heads. Psalm 74 speaks of “the heads of leviathan” (Psalm 74:14), and Revelation’s dragon has seven (Revelation 12:3). Scripture says what a head means: the seven heads are also “seven kings” (Revelation 17:9-10). The beast has seven heads too, and the dragon gives him his power (Revelation 13:1-2).

So Pharaoh could be called “the great dragon” (Ezekiel 29:3), and Nebuchadnezzar could swallow Israel “like a dragon” (Jeremiah 51:34). Proud kings are his heads. Kings die and empires fall, but the serpent behind them keeps raising up another.

## How He Ends

Leviathan is no rival god. Yahuah made him (Psalm 104:26). Psalm 74 says “God is my King of old” and says He broke “the heads of leviathan in pieces” (Psalm 74:12, 14). There are two kings in this story, and only one is King of old.

The first promise was that the serpent’s head would be bruised (Genesis 3:15). Isaiah saw Yahuah’s “sore and great and strong sword” punish Leviathan (Isaiah 27:1).

The choice today is still the one from the garden: be as gods, or bow to Yahuah. Pride keeps a person in the serpent’s kingdom. Deliverance through Messiah brings him out, and the commandments of Yahuah are how he walks once he is free. That is why the dragon makes war on those who “keep the commandments of God, and have the testimony of Yahushua Messiah” (Revelation 12:17). But his end is already written: “the devil that deceived them was cast into the lake of fire” (Revelation 20:10).
