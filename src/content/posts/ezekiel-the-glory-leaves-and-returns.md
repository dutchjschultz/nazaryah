## The Claim

Ezekiel is not a box of strange pictures thrown together. It is the most carefully dated book among the prophets, and it runs one direction.

Five blocks carry it from Ezekiel’s own day, through the coming of the Shepherd and the age now running, to the end of the age and what comes after it. The whole book hangs on one frame. The glory of Yahuah leaves the temple in chapter 11 and comes back in chapter 43. Everything in between is the road from the one to the other.

A reader who knows which block he is standing in knows who is being spoken to and what time it is. That one fact keeps every verse where Ezekiel put it.

## Why It Looks Scattered

Most people know Ezekiel by its parts. The wheels in chapter 1. The king of Tyre in chapter 28, preached as the devil’s life story. The dry bones in chapter 37, preached as the founding of the modern State of Israel. Gog and Magog in chapters 38 and 39, preached as a Russian invasion. The temple in chapters 40 to 48, preached as a stone building for a thousand-year kingdom on the present earth. Even the shut east gate of chapter 44 was used by early church writers to argue that Mary never had other children.

Each of those readings lifts a chapter out of its block and sets it down in a different century. Read front to back, the book will not allow it. Ezekiel dates his messages thirteen times, and apart from one late word filed with the others against Egypt (29:17), the dates move forward.

## The Five Blocks

### Block One — Ezekiel 1 to 24

*Jerusalem warned, and the glory leaves. About 593 to 588 BC.*

**The years.** Ezekiel is a priest already carried to Babylon with King Jehoiachin, living among the captives by the river Chebar (1:1-3). His call comes in the fifth year of that captivity. Jerusalem is still standing, the temple is still in use, and the people left behind believe the worst is over.

**The throne.** Yahuah shows him the throne moving on its wheels, and the text names what sits on it: “the likeness of the glory of Yahuah” (1:28). One glory, one name, no second figure introduced.

**What happens.** Ezekiel is sent to act out the siege with a clay tile, lie on his side for days on end, and shave his head with a sword (4-5). In chapters 8 to 11 he is carried in vision into the temple and shown what goes on inside it: images on the walls, women weeping for Tammuz, and twenty-five men with their backs to the temple, bowing to the sun toward the east (8:16). Then the glory lifts off the cherubim, moves to the threshold, to the east gate, and finally leaves the city for the mountain on the east side (10:18-19; 11:23).

**The charge.** Chapter 20 names it. Yahuah gave His Sabbaths “to be a sign between me and them” (20:12), and they polluted them along with their idols (20:13, 16, 24). Chapter 22 adds that the priests hid their eyes from His Sabbaths and put no difference between holy and profane (22:26).

**How it ends.** The king of Babylon sets himself against Jerusalem on a dated day (24:1-2). That evening Ezekiel’s wife dies, and he is told not to mourn, because the people will not be allowed to mourn their temple either (24:16-24). His mouth is to stay shut until a survivor arrives with the news (24:26-27).

### Block Two — Ezekiel 25 to 32

*The nations around her. About 587 to 571 BC.*

**The years.** The siege is under way, and Jerusalem falls in 586 BC. While Ezekiel waits for the news, the words turn outward.

**What happens.** Seven nations hear their sentence: Ammon, Moab, Edom, and Philistia in one chapter (25), then Tyre (26-28), Sidon (28:20-23), and Egypt (29-32). Tyre gets three chapters because it was the trading capital of the age. Egypt gets four because Judah kept running to it for help. Pharaoh is pictured as a great cedar of Lebanon, cut down (31).

**The king of Tyre.** Chapter 28 is the one most pulpits lift out. The text names the man and speaks to him directly: “thou art a man, and not El” (28:2). His sin is trade and pride, “by the multitude of thy merchandise” (28:16). The garden and the cherub are the picture Yahuah paints over a king who thought his rich island was paradise. They are not a hidden biography of Satan. The full chapter is read in its setting in [*Thou Art a Man, and Not El*](/blog/thou-art-a-man-and-not-el).

**How it ends.** Egypt goes down into the pit and finds the other nations already lying there (32:18-32).

### The Hinge — Ezekiel 33

*The news arrives. About 585 BC.*

Chapter 33 is not a block of its own. It is the door between the two halves of the book. Ezekiel is set again as a watchman (33:7), the same charge given at his call (3:17). Then a man who escaped Jerusalem walks in and says it plainly.

> The city is smitten.
>
> — Ezekiel 33:21

The evening before, Ezekiel’s mouth is opened (33:22), the mouth that was shut in chapter 3 and promised back in chapter 24. From here on the book stops warning about the fall. The fall has happened. Every message after this looks forward.

### Block Three — Ezekiel 34 to 37

*The Shepherd and the gathering. From the road home, through the Messiah, across the age now running.*

**The years.** The gathering out of the nations begins with the road home from Babylon, but the block is carried by the Shepherd and the new heart, and those arrive with the Messiah. This is the block the reader is standing in.

**The Shepherd.** Chapter 34 fires the shepherds of Israel, who fed themselves instead of the flock (34:2-10), and promises one Shepherd in their place, “my servant David” (34:23). David had been dead four hundred years when this was written. The Shepherd is David’s Son. Yahushua took the title in John 10 and added that He had other sheep to bring in, so there would be “one fold, and one shepherd” (John 10:16).

**The new heart.** Chapter 35 sentences Edom, the brother who claimed the land for himself (35:10). Chapter 36 promises the land back, and then the change inside the people.

> A new heart also will I give you … and cause you to walk in my statutes.
>
> — Ezekiel 36:26-27

Most pulpits stop at the new heart. Ezekiel does not. The heart is made new so that the statutes can be kept. Same law, new place: the heart instead of the stone.

**The bones and the sticks.** Chapter 37 gives the two pictures of the age. The valley of dry bones stands up when the breath enters them, and the Hebrew word for that breath is *ruach*, the same word as spirit (37:9-10, 14). Then two sticks, one for Judah and one for Joseph and Ephraim, become one stick in Ezekiel’s hand (37:16-19). One nation, one king, one Shepherd, walking in Yahuah’s judgments and keeping His statutes (37:22-24). The full vision is read in [*Can These Bones Live?*](/blog/ezekiel-vision-three-can-these-bones-live).

**How it ends.** The chapter reaches past the age to its close: the graves opened and the people brought up out of them (37:12-13), and the sanctuary set in their midst forever (37:26-28).

### Block Four — Ezekiel 38 and 39

*The last gathering. The close of the age.*

**The years.** “In the latter years” (38:8), when the people gathered in Block Three are living at rest in villages with no walls, bars, or gates (38:11).

**What happens.** Gog, the chief prince of Meshech and Tubal, gathers nations from the far edges of the known world against the people of Yahuah. No army of Israel fights. Yahuah answers with pestilence, hail, fire, and brimstone (38:22), and the birds are called to the slain (39:17-20).

**Why it is written.** The two chapters give the reason six times over: “they shall know that I am Yahuah” (38:23). One name is honored by one act, and no one stands beside Him in it.

**How it ends.** The dead are buried for seven months and the weapons burned for seven years (39:9, 12). Then Yahuah says He will hide His face from them no more (39:29). The full case against the Russian-invasion reading is laid out in the Nazaryah investigation [*Gog and Magog*](/blog/gog-and-magog).

### Block Five — Ezekiel 40 to 48

*The glory comes home. What follows the end.*

**The years.** The vision is dated to the twenty-fifth year of the captivity, about 573 BC (40:1), but what it shows lies past the last battle. It is the book’s ending in every sense.

**What happens.** A man with a measuring reed walks Ezekiel through a house, wall by wall and gate by gate (40-42). Then the glory comes back the same way it left.

> The glory of Yahuah came into the house by the way of the gate whose prospect is toward the east.
>
> — Ezekiel 43:4

Yahuah names it the place of His throne, where He will dwell in the midst of Israel forever (43:7).

**The law in the new house.** The priests are charged to teach the difference between holy and profane, clean and unclean, and to hallow His Sabbaths (44:23-24). Those are the exact things the priests of chapter 22 had hidden their eyes from. The Sabbaths and the new moons are kept at the east gate (46:1-3).

**The river.** Water comes out from under the threshold, rises from the ankles to the knees to the loins to a river no man can cross, and heals the Dead Sea (47:1-9). Trees grow on both banks, bearing fruit every month, “and the leaf thereof for medicine” (47:12).

**The stranger.** The land is divided among the tribes, and the stranger who lives among them receives an inheritance “with you among the tribes of Israel” (47:22-23). No second people. One house, enlarged.

**How it ends.** The last words of the book are the name of the city: “Yahuah is there” (48:35), in Hebrew *Yahuah shammah*.

## How the Breaks Are Marked

**The dates.** Ezekiel stamps his messages with the year, month, and day of the captivity. Block One runs from the fifth year to the ninth. Block Two sits in the tenth through the twelfth, with one late word on Egypt from the twenty-seventh. The news arrives in the twelfth. The last vision carries the twenty-fifth.

**The prophet’s mouth.** It is shut in chapter 3 (3:26), promised open in chapter 24 (24:27), and opened the night before the runner arrives (33:22). That marks the turn from warning to hope.

**The glory.** It leaves by the east (10:19; 11:23) and returns by the east (43:2-4). That is the frame of the whole book. Everything inside it is the time the glory was gone from the house.

## Nine Runs for Fast Placement

Five blocks place a chapter roughly. Nine runs place it exactly.

**1 – 3** — The throne by the river, and the watchman’s call.

**4 – 7** — The siege acted out: the tile, the side, the razor.

**8 – 11** — Inside the temple, and the glory leaves.

**12 – 24** — The exile acted out, the parables, the Sabbath charge, the siege begins.

**25 – 32** — Seven nations sentenced, Tyre and Egypt the longest.

**33** — The watchman again, and the news: the city is smitten.

**34 – 37** — The Shepherd, the new heart, the dry bones, the two sticks.

**38 – 39** — Gog and Magog, the last gathering.

**40 – 48** — The house measured, the glory returns, the river, the city.

Runs one through four are all Block One. Each of the rest stands on its own.

## One Audience the Whole Way Through

Ezekiel is sent to “the house of Israel” (3:4), and he never switches peoples. What changes is their condition, not their identity. A rebellious house. Captives by a river. A scattered flock gathered. Two sticks made one. A land shared with the stranger who joins them.

Modern teaching splits that audience in half, Israel on one side and the assembly on the other, and then has to sort every promise into one bin or the other. Ezekiel does the opposite. He takes two sticks and makes them one in his hand, and in the last block he writes the stranger into the tribal inheritance.

### A Title Worth Noticing

Yahuah calls Ezekiel “son of man” more than ninety times. In this book it is the name of a mortal man standing before the throne, set apart from the One on it. Yahushua chose that same title for Himself more than any other. Ezekiel shows what the title meant to the people who first heard it: a man sent by Yahuah, not Yahuah Himself.

## The Double-Meaning Move

The habit that breaks Isaiah breaks Ezekiel the same way. A verse belongs to one block, a doctrine needs it in another, and so the verse is said to carry two meanings. The near meaning disappears, and the block goes with it.

1. **The king of Tyre** becomes the fall of Satan, though the chapter calls him a man, names his city, and lists his trade.
2. **The dry bones** become 1948, though Ezekiel attaches conditions no political state has met: one king from David’s line, no more idols, and Yahuah’s sanctuary in their midst forever (37:22-28).
3. **The new heart** becomes a feeling at an altar call, though the next verse says what it is for: to walk in the statutes (36:27).

Scripture does reuse Ezekiel’s language, and it says so openly. John takes up the river, the city, and the names Gog and Magog in plain view of his readers. Nothing is taken away from Ezekiel in the process. A pattern reused is honest. A meaning replaced is not.

## What the Timeline Rules Out

**A 1948 fulfillment.** Block Three is carried by the Shepherd and the new heart, and its terms are covenant terms. A vote of nations does not meet them.

**A seven-year gap.** Block Four follows Block Three with nothing wedged between. Daniel’s seventy weeks are finished, and Ezekiel never brings an orphan week into his book.

**A middle kingdom with a rebuilt stone temple.** Block Five comes after the last battle, not before it, and Ezekiel puts no thousand-year reign between them. Two details keep the house from being a stage set for a Messiah reigning on the present earth. The house was shown to the captives as a pattern, on a condition: “if they be ashamed of all that they have done” (43:10-11). And the prince in that house offers a sin offering “for himself” (45:22) and has sons (46:16), so he cannot be the sinless Messiah. The offerings themselves are not the problem. None of the five is thrown out. The sin offering is at rest because the one offering never wears out (Hebrews 10:14), and Ezekiel still names it in the house to come (45:17; 46:20). Why Ezekiel can still name the sin offering in the house to come is laid out in [*Three Verses After the End*](/blog/three-verses-after-the-end).

**A law with an expiration date.** The Sabbaths are the charge in Block One (20:12-24), the statutes are written into the new heart in Block Three (36:27), and the Sabbaths are hallowed in the house of Block Five (44:24). First block, middle block, last block. Anyone teaching that the law ended somewhere in the middle has to account for the last chapters.

**A scattered book.** Ezekiel is only confusing to a reader who is mining it.

## The Whole Book in Five Sentences

The glory sees the idols in its own house and leaves for the mountain on the east.

The nations that cheered the fall hear their own sentence, and the city falls.

A Shepherd from David’s line gathers the flock, gives it a new heart, and puts the statutes inside it.

The whole hostile world makes one last gathering and is ended by fire from heaven.

The glory comes home by the east gate, a river runs out to heal the Dead Sea, and the city is named Yahuah is there.

## Where It Lands

Ezekiel runs parallel to Revelation, and in the same order. Both open on the throne, both have the prophet eat a scroll, both name Gog and Magog at the end, and both close on a city with a river and leaves that heal. The story is the same because the ending is the same.

The glory that left the house in Ezekiel’s day comes home for good in the last chapters of the Scriptures, where John hears the same promise Ezekiel closed on.

> Behold, the tabernacle of Elohim is with men, and he will dwell with them.
>
> — Revelation 21:3
