## A Small Green Image

Inside the walls of the Grand Palace in Bangkok sits a small figure on a tall golden throne. It is the Emerald Buddha, about twenty-six inches high, carved from green stone. It is not really emerald. In the dim light of the temple it is hard to see past the gold that rises around it.

Thailand calls this image the palladium of the kingdom. That word means a protecting image, one the safety of the nation is said to depend on. Three times a year, for the hot season, the rainy season, and the cool season, the king of Thailand or his appointed representative climbs up to it and changes its gold clothing. The changing of the robes is believed to bring the right weather for the rice crop and blessing on the land.

The temple that holds it is a royal temple built inside the palace grounds. It was made for this one image, by a king, beside his throne.

## Carried Off as Spoil

The history of the image is a history of being carried. It first appears in 1434, when lightning cracked a chedi (a dome-shaped shrine) in Chiang Rai and monks found a figure under a coat of plaster. From there it was moved to Lampang and then to Chiang Mai. A king of Laos took it to Luang Prabang, and in 1564 he moved it to his new capital, Vientiane, where it stayed for over two hundred years.

In 1779 a Siamese general named Chao Phraya Chakri marched on Vientiane, looted the city, and brought the image back to Siam as spoil of war. A few years later that general took the throne for himself and became King Rama I, the first of the dynasty that still reigns in Thailand. He built Bangkok as his new capital and built the Temple of the Emerald Buddha inside his palace in 1784.

Through all those moves one belief followed the image: whoever held it held the right to rule. It was never only a religious object. It was a crown in the shape of a statue.

## Bel and Nebo on the Backs of Beasts

Isaiah saw this exact picture in Babylon. He describes the city's great gods, Bel and Nebo, loaded onto animals as the city falls: "their idols were upon the beasts," a heavy load for weary cattle (Isaiah 46:1). The gods "could not deliver the burden, but themselves are gone into captivity" (Isaiah 46:2).

Then Isaiah lays out what such a god is. Men hire a goldsmith to make it, "they bear him upon the shoulder, they carry him, and set him in his place." Once set there, "from his place shall he not remove," and if a man cries to it, "yet can he not answer, nor save him out of his trouble" (Isaiah 46:6-7).

That is the story of the Emerald Buddha in plain record. The protector of Vientiane did not protect Vientiane. When the army came, it was the image that was carried away. A god that must be guarded cannot be a guard.

Isaiah sets Yahuah against that picture in the same chapter. The idols are carried by their people. Yahuah carries His: "I have made, and I will bear; even I will carry, and will deliver you" (Isaiah 46:4).

## Dressed by the Hands That Made It

Jeremiah adds the clothing. Men cut a tree, "they deck it with silver and with gold; they fasten it with nails and with hammers, that it move not" (Jeremiah 10:4). "Blue and purple is their clothing: they are all the work of cunning men" (Jeremiah 10:9). And the last word on it: "they must needs be borne, because they cannot go" (Jeremiah 10:5).

Three times a year a king climbs up and dresses an image that cannot dress itself, so that it will send rain. Jeremiah answers that directly: "Are there any among the vanities of the Gentiles that can cause rain?" (Jeremiah 14:22). Yahuah is the One who sends the rain in its season (Deuteronomy 11:14).

Buddhist teachers often say an image of the Buddha is only a reminder of his teaching and not a god. The record on this image says otherwise. It is called the protector of the kingdom, it is dressed to bring the weather, and it is believed to give the right to rule. Whatever it is called, the Torah names that an idol: "Thou shalt not make unto thee any graven image... Thou shalt not bow down thyself to them, nor serve them" (Exodus 20:4-5).

## The King's Chapel at Bethel

Israel had its own king's idol. When the kingdom split, Jeroboam ruled the ten northern tribes, but the temple stood in Jerusalem, in the southern kingdom. Jeroboam reasoned that if his people kept going down to Jerusalem to offer, "then shall the heart of this people turn again" to the king of Judah, and "they shall kill me" (1 Kings 12:27).

So he made two calves of gold and told the people, "behold thy gods, O Israel, which brought thee up out of the land of Egypt" (1 Kings 12:28). He set one in Bethel and one in Dan. He made his own priests and set up a feast in a month "which he had devised of his own heart" (1 Kings 12:32-33).

The calves were not made because Jeroboam loved them. They were made to hold his throne together. The idol served the king, and the king served the idol, and the two could not be pulled apart. Years later the priest of Bethel said it out loud when he tried to drive the prophet Amos away: "it is the king's chapel, and it is the king's court" (Amos 7:13).

A royal temple inside the palace walls, built for an image that holds the kingdom together, is the king's chapel at Bethel standing again in Bangkok.

## How the King's Idol Ends

Scripture does not leave Jeroboam's calves standing. "This thing became sin unto the house of Jeroboam, even to cut it off, and to destroy it from off the face of the earth" (1 Kings 13:34). Hosea foretold the end of the calf at Bethel in the same terms Isaiah used for Bel: "It shall be also carried unto Assyria for a present to king Jareb" (Hosea 10:6). The idol that was meant to keep the kingdom was itself loaded up and carried off.

That is how every king's idol ends. It is carried in, dressed, and guarded, and then it is carried out by the next army as a prize.

## The Order Yahuah Set

Yahuah never gave Israel's king an image to hold his throne. He gave him a book. When a king took the throne, he was to write out his own copy of the law, keep it beside him, "and he shall read therein all the days of his life," so "that his heart be not lifted up above his brethren" (Deuteronomy 17:18-20).

In Bangkok the king stands over the image and dresses it. In Israel the king was to sit under the word and be ruled by it. One order makes the god serve the throne. The other makes the throne serve Yahuah.

The man who belongs to Yahuah through the blood of Yahushua has no need of a palladium. He is not carrying his god; his God is carrying him, "even to hoar hairs" (Isaiah 46:4).

## The Last King's Idol

The pattern does not end in Thailand. Revelation shows a final king's idol: an image made at the command of a ruling power, given breath to speak, and enforced so that all who would not serve it should be killed (Revelation 13:14-15). It is Bethel one last time, an image tied to a throne. And it ends the same way, with the beast and his false prophet taken and cast into the lake of fire (Revelation 19:20).

The city that comes after has no image and no royal shrine. John "saw no temple therein: for Yahuah Almighty and the Lamb are the temple of it" (Revelation 21:22).
