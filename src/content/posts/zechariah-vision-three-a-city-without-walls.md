Still the same night (1:7). The first vision promised that a measuring line would be stretched over Jerusalem (1:16). The second showed the horns that scattered the people cast out. Now Zechariah lifts his eyes and sees the line itself, in a man’s hand (2:1).

## The Vision

Zechariah sees a man with a measuring line, going out to measure the length and breadth of Jerusalem. Before he can start, one angel goes out and another runs to meet him with a message: Jerusalem will spread out like open villages, too full of people and cattle for any wall, and Yahuah Himself will be a wall of fire around it and the glory inside it. Then a call goes out to the captives still living in Babylon to flee home. A warning follows, that whoever touches His people touches the apple of His eye, and then a promise: Yahuah is coming to dwell in Zion, many nations will be joined to Him, and all flesh is told to be silent before Him.

## The Measuring Line

Zechariah asks the man where he is going. “To measure Jerusalem, to see what is the breadth thereof, and what is the length thereof” (2:2). A builder measures before he builds. Measuring is how a piece of ground is claimed and laid out. Jeremiah saw the same thing: “the measuring line shall yet go forth” over the rebuilt city, and it “shall not be plucked up, nor thrown down any more for ever” (Jeremiah 31:39-40). Ezekiel watched a man with a measuring reed walk the whole house of the glory’s return (Ezekiel 40:3).

The line can also measure for judgment. Before Jerusalem fell, Yahuah said He would stretch over it “the line of Samaria” (2 Kings 21:13), and Jeremiah mourned that He “stretched out a line” and did not hold back His hand from destroying (Lamentations 2:8). The same tool that once marked the city for ruin now marks it for building.

## Towns Without Walls

Before the man can start, an angel runs to stop him: “Jerusalem shall be inhabited as towns without walls for the multitude of men and cattle therein” (2:4). The man came to measure walls. Yahuah says the city will be too big for any wall to hold.

The word for towns without walls, *perazot*, means open country villages. Ezekiel uses it for the people of Yahuah at the close of the age, living “in the land of unwalled villages,” at rest, with “neither bars nor gates” (Ezekiel 38:11). Both prophets see the same thing: a people with no wall of stone around them and nothing to fear.

Stone walls did come later. Nehemiah rebuilt them about seventy-five years after this night, because the enemies were real (Nehemiah 2:17). But the promise in the vision reaches past any wall a man can build.

## A Wall of Fire

Here is the center of the vision. “For I, saith Yahuah, will be unto her a wall of fire round about, and will be the glory in the midst of her” (2:5).

Israel had seen that fire before. Yahuah went before them “by night in a pillar of fire” (Exodus 13:21), and when Pharaoh’s army closed in, the pillar moved and stood between the two camps (Exodus 14:19-20). Isaiah promised it again for the restored Zion: “the shining of a flaming fire by night: for upon all the glory shall be a defence” (Isaiah 4:5). The wall around the city is not stone. It is Yahuah Himself.

And the glory is inside. Ezekiel had watched the glory leave the temple and the city by the east (Ezekiel 10:18-19; 11:23). Zechariah hears that it is coming back to live in the midst. Fire on the outside, glory on the inside.

## Come Out of Babylon

Then the vision turns into a call. “Ho, ho, come forth, and flee from the land of the north … Deliver thyself, O Zion, that dwellest with the daughter of Babylon” (2:6-7). Most of the captives never came home. They had houses, businesses, and families in Babylon, and they stayed. The call goes out to them: the building has started, so come out.

## The Apple of His Eye

The one speaking says Yahuah of hosts has sent him to the nations that robbed His people, “for he that toucheth you toucheth the apple of his eye” (2:8). The apple of the eye is the pupil, the most tender part of the body, the part a man guards without thinking. Moses used the same picture: Yahuah kept Israel “as the apple of his eye” (Deuteronomy 32:10).

Notice how the speaker identifies himself, three times in the chapter: “Yahuah of hosts hath sent me” (2:8, 9, 11). He speaks Yahuah’s words in the first person, the way every prophet and messenger did, and then says plainly who sent him. One sends, one is sent. “The servant is not greater than his master; neither he that is sent greater than he that sent him” (John 13:16).

Then comes the reversal: “I will shake mine hand upon them, and they shall be a spoil to their servants” (2:9). The nations that robbed Israel will be robbed by the people they enslaved. Israel had seen this before, walking out of Egypt carrying Egypt’s silver and gold (Exodus 12:35-36). Isaiah used the same picture of Yahuah shaking His hand over Egypt (Isaiah 19:16). It takes only a shake of His hand.

## Many Nations Joined

“Sing and rejoice, O daughter of Zion: for, lo, I come, and I will dwell in the midst of thee” (2:10). And then: “many nations shall be joined to Yahuah in that day, and shall be my people” (2:11).

Joined, *lavah*, is the word Leah used when she named Levi: “now this time will my husband be joined unto me” (Genesis 29:34). Isaiah uses it for the stranger who joins himself to Yahuah, and he says what that stranger does: he keeps the Sabbath and takes hold of the covenant (Isaiah 56:6). The nations are not given a separate program. They are joined to the same house, under the same covenant, and they become “my people.”

The chapter ends with the only place in the Bible that uses the words “the holy land” (2:12), and a command: “Be silent, O all flesh, before Yahuah: for he is raised up out of his holy habitation” (2:13). Yahuah is getting up to act.

Just before that, Yahuah says He “shall inherit Judah his portion in the holy land, and shall choose Jerusalem again” (2:12). The land is not holy because of its dirt. It is holy because He dwells there. Moses said the same thing about the people: “Yahuah’s portion is his people; Jacob is the lot of his inheritance” (Deuteronomy 32:9). Yahuah’s inheritance was never mainly real estate. It is the people He lives among, and Paul speaks of “the riches of the glory of his inheritance in the saints” (Ephesians 1:18).

## Bringing It Home

The believer is the house (1 Corinthians 3:16), and Paul says the nations who come in are “fellowcitizens with the saints, and of the household of Elohim,” built together “for an habitation of Elohim through the Spirit” (Ephesians 2:19, 22). That is this vision fulfilled: many nations joined, and Yahuah dwelling in the midst.

Like the young man with the line, believers want walls they can measure. A building, a denomination, a membership roll, a set of men’s rules that tells them who is in and who is out. Walls feel safe. But Yahuah never promised His people the safety of stone. He promised Himself: fire around, glory within. The protection of the believer is not the walls of a church. It is the presence of Yahuah, and that presence stays where His commandments are kept and His name is honored (John 14:23).

And the call of 2:7 is still going out. Many who belong to Yahuah are still living comfortably in Babylon, settled in its customs, its holidays, and its teaching. The house is being built. Come out and join the work.

## Where It Lands

In Revelation an angel with a golden reed measures the holy city, its length and breadth and height (Revelation 21:15-16). The city needs no sun, “for the glory of Elohim did lighten it” (21:23), and its gates are never shut (21:25). The voice from heaven had already said, “Come out of her, my people” (Revelation 18:4). The measuring line, the glory in the midst, the open city, and the call out of Babylon all meet on the last pages.
