## The Setting

The first burden ended badly. The King came gentle on a donkey, the people sold Him for thirty pieces of silver, and they were left with a worthless shepherd. A reader would think the story was over. The second burden says it is not. It picks up right where the first one stopped and answers the next question: what happens after the King is refused?

## Why This Burden

The people in Zechariah’s day were afraid David’s line was finished. The first burden told them the King was still coming. The second burden goes further. It opens with Yahuah promising to defend Jerusalem and to make “the house of David” strong (12:8), and it names David’s house five times in two chapters. David’s house is not only still standing. It is the house through which the whole world will be washed clean.

It also finishes the promise of chapter 8, that their fasts would become feasts and the nations would come to seek Yahuah (8:19-23). The second burden ends with the nations keeping the feast. It runs forward in four stops.

## 1. The Pierced One (12:10)

“They shall look upon me whom they have pierced, and they shall mourn for him, as one mourneth for his only son.”

In AD 31, outside Jerusalem, a Roman soldier pushed a spear into the side of Yahushua as He hung dead on the cross. John was standing there, and he wrote that it happened so the scripture would be fulfilled: “They shall look on him whom they pierced” (John 19:34-37).

Zechariah said the mourning would be like the mourning at Megiddo (12:11). That is where Josiah, the last good king of David’s line, was killed, and all of Judah wept for him (2 Chronicles 35:22-25). Now they would weep for David’s Son.

## 2. The Fountain Opened (13:1)

“In that day there shall be a fountain opened to the house of David … for sin and for uncleanness.”

When the spear went in, “forthwith came there out blood and water” (John 19:34). The wound became the fountain.

A few weeks later, at Shavuot, Peter stood in Jerusalem and told the crowd that the Man they had pierced was the Messiah. They “were pricked in their heart” and asked, “what shall we do?” Three thousand were washed that day (Acts 2:36-41). Zechariah had said Yahuah would pour out His spirit and they would look and mourn (12:10). That morning it happened.

## 3. The Shepherd Struck, the City Falls (13:7-14:2)

“Smite the shepherd, and the sheep shall be scattered.”

On the night He was arrested, Yahushua quoted this verse about Himself, and that same night His disciples ran (Matthew 26:31, 56).

Zechariah saw what came after. The city would be taken, its houses robbed, and half its people carried off (14:2). Two parts of the land would be cut off, and one part brought through the fire and refined like silver (13:8-9).

Forty years after the cross, in AD 70, Rome surrounded Jerusalem and burned it. Yahushua had warned His followers ahead of time to flee to the mountains when they saw armies around the city (Luke 21:20-21), and early writers record that they did. The refined part came through the fire. Of them Yahuah says, “I will say, It is my people: and they shall say, Yahuah is my Elohim” (13:9).

## 4. One King, One Name, One Feast (chapter 14)

“Yahuah shall be king over all the earth: in that day shall there be one Yahuah, and his name one” (14:9).

The book ends with the whole earth saying what Israel was told to say in the Shema: one Yahuah (Deuteronomy 6:4). Living waters flow out from Jerusalem to the world (14:8). Yahushua stood in the temple on the last day of Sukkot and promised that same water, “rivers of living water,” flowing out of everyone who believes (John 7:37-38).

Then the nations come up year after year “to worship the King, Yahuah of hosts, and to keep the feast of tabernacles” (14:16). The fasts of chapter 7 have become the feast. And the words that were once engraved on one gold plate on the high priest’s forehead, “HOLINESS UNTO YAHUAH” (Exodus 28:36), are written on everything, down to the bells on the horses and the pots in the kitchen (14:20-21).

## Why Tell Them Now?

This burden carries the same call as the first, “Turn ye unto me” (1:3), and it adds the hope that makes turning worth it. Yahuah does not only warn that the King will be pierced. He shows what He will do with that wound: open a fountain for sin (13:1), and refine a people He calls His own (13:9).

The choice is the same one again, in new words. Two parts of the land are cut off, and one part comes through the fire. Of that part Yahuah says, “They shall call on my name, and I will hear them: I will say, It is my people” (13:9). Every father in Zechariah’s small town was raising children whose line would one day be one part or the other.

And it tells them what Yahuah is after. Not fasts kept out of habit (7:5), but a people holy down to the pots in the kitchen (14:20-21). That holiness starts in the house you live in now, long before the King arrives. Peter said the prophets served “not unto themselves, but unto us” (1 Peter 1:12), but what they carried was a call to turn, and it was spoken first to the people standing in front of them.

## What It Means

For them, it answered their fear. David’s line was not finished. Their King would come, be refused, and be pierced, and out of that wound would come a fountain for David’s house, a remnant brought through the fire, and the nations coming to Yahuah’s feast.

For us, it shows the cross was not a loss. The Shepherd was struck on purpose, and out of His side came the fountain. The nations who now call on Yahuah’s Name are the nations of chapter 14, brought in to worship one Yahuah, keep His feast, and be holy.

## Where It Lands

Revelation opens with this burden: “every eye shall see him, and they also which pierced him” (Revelation 1:7). And it closes with the living waters “proceeding out of the throne” (22:1), and Yahuah’s Name no longer on one priest’s forehead but on all His servants: “his name shall be in their foreheads” (22:4).
