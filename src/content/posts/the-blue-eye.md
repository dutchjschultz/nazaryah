## What Came West

The blue glass eye — a dark blue circle around white, light blue, and a black center — hangs in Turkish homes, shops, and taxis, is pinned to babies' clothing, and is set into bracelets and necklaces. It is now sold across America as jewelry and décor, often simply called the "evil eye."

In Turkey it is called the *nazar boncuğu*, the "nazar bead." *Nazar* is the Arabic word for sight or gaze. The bead is meant to catch and turn back the evil eye, the envious look believed to cause sickness, accidents, and loss. The belief is very old; Greeks and Romans held it before Islam, and it spread through the Muslim world and the Mediterranean. Like the hamsa, many Muslim teachers forbid the bead as trust in an object, yet it remains one of the most common charms in the Muslim world.

The Arabic *nazar* is a separate word from the Hebrew *nazar*, which means to separate or set apart, as in the Nazarite vow. The two only sound alike.

## What the Bead Teaches

The bead teaches that the danger is outside: another person's jealous look reaches out and harms the one who is seen. The cure is to wear a glass eye that looks back.

## What Scripture Calls the Evil Eye

Scripture speaks of the evil eye often, and it means something else entirely. In Hebrew it is *ayin ra'ah*, and it describes a stingy, grudging heart. "Eat thou not the bread of him that hath an evil eye" (Proverbs 23:6). "He that hasteth to be rich hath an evil eye" (Proverbs 28:22). Israel was warned not to let "thine eye be evil against thy poor brother" when the year of release drew near (Deuteronomy 15:9).

Yahushua used the same idiom. To the workers who grumbled at the landowner's generosity He said, "Is thine eye evil, because I am good?" (Matthew 20:15). And He taught, "if thine eye be evil, thy whole body shall be full of darkness" (Matthew 6:23).

In Scripture the evil eye harms the one who has it. It is a sin inside a man, not a curse coming at him. No bead can fix it, because the problem is not the other man's look — it is one's own heart.

## The Verdict

The nazar bead turns Scripture upside down. It blames an outside glance for what the Bible places in the heart of the one looking, and it puts trust in glass instead of in Yahuah. A believer guards against the evil eye by giving freely, not by wearing a charm. The bead belongs under the oak.

Revelation gives the only cure for blind eyes: "anoint thine eyes with eyesalve, that thou mayest see" (Revelation 3:18).
