## Introduction

A common teaching in Christian circles holds that the Law of Yahuah was a heavy burden laid on Israel, a “yoke of bondage” that the Messiah came to lift. Galatians 5:1 is the verse most often quoted for it. If that teaching is right, it creates a hard problem with the Exodus itself. Yahuah broke Israel out of slavery in Egypt, and within weeks He gave them a Law at Sinai. On that reading, He traded one bondage for another.

Deuteronomy 13:1–5 answers the problem inside a single paragraph. The answer rests on one Hebrew word that the English Bible translates two different ways, so the English reader never sees that it is the same word.

## Part I — The Passage and Its Test

> **Deuteronomy 13:1–5**
>
> *If there arise among you a prophet, or a dreamer of dreams, and giveth thee a sign or a wonder, and the sign or the wonder come to pass, whereof he spake unto thee, saying, Let us go after other gods, which thou hast not known, and let us serve them; thou shalt not hearken unto the words of that prophet, or that dreamer of dreams: for Yahuah your God proveth you, to know whether ye love Yahuah your God with all your heart and with all your soul. Ye shall walk after Yahuah your God, and fear him, and keep his commandments, and obey his voice, and ye shall serve him, and cleave unto him. And that prophet, or that dreamer of dreams, shall be put to death; because he hath spoken to turn you away from Yahuah your God, which brought you out of the land of Egypt, and redeemed you out of the house of bondage, to thrust thee out of the way which Yahuah thy God commanded thee to walk in. So shalt thou put the evil away from the midst of thee.*

### A Sign That Comes True

Notice what the text does NOT say. It does not say the false prophet is caught by a failed prediction. His sign comes to pass. His word comes true. By the measure most people use, he has proven himself. Yet Israel is told not to listen to him.

The test is not whether the sign works. The test is where the sign leads. A true sign that points away from Yahuah is still a false prophet's sign.

> ***נָסָה (nasah, H5254)*** — to test, to try, to prove. Verse 3 says Yahuah “proveth” His people. The wonder is permitted so that what is in the heart comes out into the open.

The question being asked of Israel is a question of love and loyalty, and the passage measures it by one thing: whom they will serve.

### The Six Verbs of Verse 4

Against the false prophet's call to “go after other gods … and serve them,” verse 4 sets six verbs. Israel is to walk after Yahuah, fear Him, keep His commandments, obey His voice, serve Him, and cleave unto Him.

Keeping the commandments sits in the middle of that list, surrounded by words of relationship. It is not set apart as a cold legal duty. It is one strand of a life bound to a person.

> ***דָּבַק (dabaq, H1692)*** — to cling, to stick to, to be joined. This is the word of Genesis 2:24, where a man shall “cleave unto his wife.”

Think about that. The servant of verse 4 is not a chained laborer. His service ends in union. And the fifth verb, “serve,” is the key that opens the whole passage.

## Part II — One Word for Bondage and Service

### The Hebrew Words

> ***עָבַד (abad, H5647)*** — to serve, to work, to labor. This is the verb in verse 4: “ye shall serve him.” It is also the verb in verse 2: “let us serve them.”
>
> ***עֶבֶד (ebed, H5650)*** — a servant, a slave, a bondman. The noun formed from abad. Its plural is avadim.

Verse 5 says Yahuah redeemed Israel “out of the house of bondage.” In Hebrew that phrase is beit avadim, literally “house of servants” or “house of slaves.” The English reader sees two different ideas, serving and bondage. The Hebrew reader sees one word in two places.

### The Same Word in Egypt

The account of Egypt is built on this root. Pharaoh's cruelty is described with it:

> **Exodus 1:13–14**
>
> *And the Egyptians made the children of Israel to serve with rigour: and they made their lives bitter with hard bondage, in morter, and in brick, and in all manner of service in the field: all their service, wherein they made them serve, was with rigour.*

Serve, bondage, service, serve. Every one of them belongs to the abad family. Now here is the key. When Yahuah demands Israel's release, He uses the same word for what He intends to do with them:

> **Exodus 4:23**
>
> *And I say unto thee, Let my son go, that he may serve me.*

Yahuah did not say, “Let my son go, that he may be his own master.” He said, “that he may serve me.” The Exodus was never a release from serving. It was a change in who would be served. Torah says so outright:

> **Leviticus 25:42, 55**
>
> *For they are my servants, which I brought forth out of the land of Egypt: they shall not be sold as bondmen. … For unto me the children of Israel are servants; they are my servants whom I brought forth out of the land of Egypt: I am Yahuah your God.*

### Three Masters in One Chapter

Deuteronomy 13 holds three masters in view, and all three are described with the same word. Israel once served Pharaoh in the house of avadim (verse 5). The false prophet calls Israel to serve other gods (verse 2, and again in verses 6 and 13). Yahuah commands Israel to serve Him (verse 4).

The act is the same in every case. What makes it bondage or freedom is the master.

Torah also shows the difference in the character of each service. Under Pharaoh, the service was “with rigour” and made life “bitter.” Under Yahuah, the result is the opposite:

> **Leviticus 26:13**
>
> *I am Yahuah your God, which brought you forth out of the land of Egypt, that ye should not be their bondmen; and I have broken the bands of your yoke, and made you go upright.*

The Law was given to a people whose yoke had just been broken, and its stated purpose was that they should walk upright. A Law given to make men stand straight cannot be the yoke that bent them over.

## Part III — Deliverance Before Commandment

### The Order of Verse 5

Verse 5 follows a fixed order. First, Yahuah “brought you out of the land of Egypt.” Second, He “redeemed you out of the house of bondage.” Only after that does the verse speak of “the way which Yahuah thy God commanded thee to walk in.”

> ***פָּדָה (padah, H6299)*** — to ransom, to redeem, to buy out by paying a price. Yahuah is the One who redeemed. The ransom was paid before the road was given.

The commandments are the road for the redeemed. They are not the price of redemption.

### The Same Order at Sinai

The Ten Commandments open the same way. Before the first commandment is spoken, Yahuah names Himself by what He has already done:

> **Exodus 20:2**
>
> *I am Yahuah thy God, which have brought thee out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of bondage.*

The phrase is again beit avadim. The order of events confirms it. The blood of the Pesach lamb was on the doorposts in Egypt (Exodus 12) weeks before Israel stood at Sinai. Deliverance came through blood first. The walk of the commandments came after.

This is the pattern of two-stage salvation: entry through the blood, then the walk by the commandments. No Israelite at Sinai was keeping the Law in order to be brought out of Egypt. He had already been brought out.

### Where the Modern Teaching Breaks

The modern teaching treats any keeping of the Law as an attempt to earn favor, and so it concludes that the Law must be bondage. But Torah never presents the Law as the way out of Egypt. It presents the Law as the way a freed people walks once they are out. The teaching has the order backward, and so it mistakes the road for the prison.

## Part IV — The New Testament Witness

### The Yoke of Bondage in Galatians

Galatians 5:1 is read as if “the yoke of bondage” means the Law of Yahuah. The very next verses define the yoke Paul has in mind:

> **Galatians 5:1–4**
>
> *Stand fast therefore in the liberty wherewith Messiah hath made us free, and be not entangled again with the yoke of bondage. Behold, I Paul say unto you, that if ye be circumcised, Messiah shall profit you nothing. For I testify again to every man that is circumcised, that he is a debtor to do the whole law. Messiah is become of no effect unto you, whosoever of you are justified by the law; ye are fallen from grace.*

The bondage is “whosoever of you are justified by the law.” It is the Law used as the entry, the means of being declared righteous, in place of the blood. This is the same error answered at Jerusalem, where certain men taught, “Except ye be circumcised after the manner of Moses, ye cannot be saved” (Acts 15:1). Peter's “yoke … which neither our fathers nor we were able to bear” (Acts 15:10) answers that claim about how a man is saved. It is not a verdict against walking in the commandments after he is saved.

### Bondage “Again”

The word “again” in Galatians 5:1 matters. The Galatians were mostly gentiles. Their earlier bondage was not the Law of Moses. Paul names it in the chapter before:

> **Galatians 4:8–9**
>
> *Howbeit then, when ye knew not God, ye did service unto them which by nature are no gods. But now, after that ye have known God, or rather are known of God, how turn ye again to the weak and beggarly elements, whereunto ye desire again to be in bondage?*
>
> ***δουλεύω (douleuo, G1398)*** — to serve as a slave, to be in bondage. The Greek counterpart of abad. Both “did service” and “be in bondage” in these verses are this one word.

The Galatians had served gods that are no gods. That is exactly what the false prophet of Deuteronomy 13:2 calls Israel to do. Paul's warning is Deuteronomy 13 in Greek: do not go back to serving the wrong master.

Then Paul uses the same word once more, this time for the free man:

> **Galatians 5:13–14**
>
> *For, brethren, ye have been called unto liberty; only use not liberty for an occasion to the flesh, but by love serve one another. For all the law is fulfilled in one word, even in this; Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself.*

“Serve” is again douleuo. Liberty in Paul's hands does not end service. It redirects it. And the service of the free man is measured by a commandment quoted straight from Torah (Leviticus 19:18). Within the space of one chapter, Paul calls the Galatians out of bondage and into Leviticus.

### The Law of Liberty

Scripture does not merely deny that the Law is bondage. It calls the Law the place of freedom. David joins the two directly:

> **Psalm 119:45**
>
> *And I will walk at liberty: for I seek thy precepts.*

The liberty comes from seeking the precepts, not from escaping them. The same psalm tells what the Law is:

> **Psalm 119:142**
>
> *Thy righteousness is an everlasting righteousness, and thy law is the truth.*

Yahushua tells what the truth does:

> **John 8:32, 34**
>
> *And ye shall know the truth, and the truth shall make you free. … Verily, verily, I say unto you, Whosoever committeth sin is the servant of sin.*

Set these side by side and the chain is complete. The Law is the truth. The truth makes free. The one who commits sin is the servant, the bondman, of sin. And Scripture defines sin by the Law: “sin is the transgression of the law” (1 John 3:4). In the words of Yahushua, bondage is the life that breaks the Law, not the life that keeps it.

> ***ἐλευθερία (eleutheria, G1657)*** — liberty, freedom. The word Paul uses in Galatians 5:1 and 5:13, and James uses in “the perfect law of liberty.”
>
> **James 1:25**
>
> *But whoso looketh into the perfect law of liberty, and continueth therein, he being not a forgetful hearer, but a doer of the work, this man shall be blessed in his deed.*

James repeats it in 2:12: “So speak ye, and so do, as they that shall be judged by the law of liberty.” John adds, “this is the love of God, that we keep his commandments: and his commandments are not grievous” (1 John 5:3). Pharaoh's service was grievous, full of rigour and bitterness. Yahuah's is not.

Yahushua offered a yoke of His own and described it in the same terms:

> **Matthew 11:29–30**
>
> *Take my yoke upon you, and learn of me; for I am meek and lowly in heart: and ye shall find rest unto your souls. For my yoke is easy, and my burden is light.*

The choice was never yoke or no yoke. It was which yoke.

### Servants of Righteousness

Paul states the principle without any qualification in Romans:

> **Romans 6:16–18, 22**
>
> *Know ye not, that to whom ye yield yourselves servants to obey, his servants ye are to whom ye obey; whether of sin unto death, or of obedience unto righteousness? But God be thanked, that ye were the servants of sin, but ye have obeyed from the heart that form of doctrine which was delivered you. Being then made free from sin, ye became the servants of righteousness. … But now being made free from sin, and become servants to God, ye have your fruit unto holiness, and the end everlasting life.*

No man is masterless. Every man serves, and the only question is whom. To be “made free” in Romans 6 is not to stop serving. It is to “become servants to God.” This is Exodus 4:23 spoken again to the assembly: let My son go, that he may serve Me.

## Conclusion

Deuteronomy 13:1–5 sets the false prophet's call and Yahuah's command side by side, and both use the same verb. Serve other gods, or serve Yahuah. Behind both stands the house of avadim in Egypt, the service Israel was redeemed out of. The word never changes. The master does.

The Law of Yahuah is not a second Egypt. It was given to a people already redeemed by blood, as the way for the redeemed to walk. Used as a ladder to righteousness, the Law becomes a yoke, because it is being made to do what only the blood can do. That is the bondage Galatians warns against. Walked as the life of one already delivered, the Law is exactly what Scripture says it is: the truth, the law of liberty, the walk of a people made to go upright.

Israel did not leave one slavery for another. Israel changed masters, and the new Master's service is freedom.
