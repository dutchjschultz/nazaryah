## Introduction

Deuteronomy 12:22 is one of those verses that stops a careful reader. It sits in a book full of purity laws, and yet it says that a clean person and an unclean person may eat the same meat at the same table. On the surface that sounds like the Law contradicting itself.

It is not. The verse is one of the clearest windows in all of Scripture into how purification actually worked. Once it is understood, a great deal of modern teaching about the Law falls apart, because that teaching treats every “unclean” in the Torah as one thing, and the Torah never did.

> **Deuteronomy 12:22**
> *Even as the roebuck and the hart is eaten, so thou shalt eat them: the unclean and the clean shall eat of them alike.*

To see what the verse means, three questions need answers. Who is the unclean person? What meat is being eaten? And what did uncleanness actually keep a person away from?

## Part I — The Words

The verse turns on a handful of Hebrew words. Each one is simple, and each one matters.

### Clean and Unclean

> **טָמֵא (tame, H2931)** — unclean, defiled. Used of animals that are not food, and also of a person in a temporary state of ritual impurity.

> **טָהוֹר (tahor, H2889)** — clean, pure. Used of animals that are food, and of a person free of ritual impurity.

Here is the key. The same pair of words is used for two different subjects. Applied to an animal, unclean describes what the creature is, and nothing ever changes it. Applied to a person, unclean describes a state, and it ends with washing and waiting. Deuteronomy 12:22 uses the words of people, not animals.

### Holy and Common

> **קֹדֶשׁ (qodesh, H6944)** — holiness, a holy thing. Something set apart to Yahuah.

> **חֹל (chol, H2455)** — common, ordinary, not set apart. The KJV renders it “unholy” or “profane.”

This second pair is a different line altogether. Holy and common is about whether a thing belongs to the altar. Clean and unclean is about what a thing is fit for. The Torah told the priests to keep the two lines apart.

> **Leviticus 10:10**
> *And that ye may put difference between holy and unholy, and between unclean and clean.*

Notice what the text does not say. It does not say holy and clean are the same thing, or that common and unclean are the same thing. Two lines, two pairs. A thing can be common and still perfectly clean. Most of the food in any Israelite house was exactly that.

### The Roebuck and the Hart

> **צְבִי (tsvi, H6643)** — the gazelle, rendered roebuck in the KJV.

> **אַיָּל (ayyal, H354)** — the deer, rendered hart in the KJV.

Both are clean animals. Israel was allowed to eat them (Deuteronomy 14:5). But neither was ever offered on the altar. No gazelle and no deer was ever a sacrifice. They were always common meat, and every Israelite knew how common meat was handled. That is why Yahuah used them as the example.

## Part II — The Old Testament Thread

### The Wilderness Rule

In the wilderness, the whole nation camped around the tabernacle. During those years, any ox, lamb, or goat that was killed had to be brought to the door of the tabernacle first.

> **Leviticus 17:3–4**
> *What man soever there be of the house of Israel, that killeth an ox, or lamb, or goat, in the camp, or that killeth it out of the camp, and bringeth it not unto the door of the tabernacle of the congregation, to offer an offering unto Yahuah before the tabernacle of Yahuah; blood shall be imputed unto that man.*

That rule worked when every family lived a short walk from the altar. It could not work once Israel spread across the land. A family living days away could not carry every sheep to the sanctuary just to have supper.

### The Change in Deuteronomy 12

Deuteronomy 12 answers that problem before Israel crosses the Jordan.

> **Deuteronomy 12:20–21**
> *When Yahuah thy Elohim shall enlarge thy border, as he hath promised thee, and thou shalt say, I will eat flesh, because thy soul longeth to eat flesh; thou mayest eat flesh, whatsoever thy soul lusteth after. If the place which Yahuah thy Elohim hath chosen to put his name there be too far from thee, then thou shalt kill of thy herd and of thy flock, which Yahuah hath given thee, as I have commanded thee, and thou shalt eat in thy gates whatsoever thy soul lusteth after.*

The phrase “enlarge thy border” describes the land growing wider as Israel settled it. It is not a permission to hunt beyond the borders. The issue is distance from the altar. Yahuah allowed an ox, a sheep, or a goat to be killed at home, inside the town gates, as ordinary food. That meat was never offered. It was common. And verse 22 says it is to be handled exactly like the gazelle and the deer.

The same permission appears earlier in the chapter (Deuteronomy 12:15), so the point is made twice.

### Who the Unclean Person Was

A person became ritually unclean through the ordinary events of life. Touching a dead body made a person unclean for seven days (Numbers 19:11). Touching the carcass of an animal that died on its own made a person unclean until evening, even if the animal was a clean kind (Leviticus 11:39–40). Childbirth, bodily discharges, and certain skin conditions did the same (Leviticus 12; 13; 15).

Think about that list. A son who buried his own father became unclean. A mother who gave birth became unclean. A man who pulled his neighbor’s dead ox out of a ditch became unclean. None of these is a sin. Burying one’s father is a duty. Bearing a child is a blessing. Ritual uncleanness was a condition of living in a world of death and decay, and it had a set end.

### What Uncleanness Kept a Person From

This is where the whole verse opens up. Uncleanness kept a person away from holy things. It did not keep him away from his home, his family, or his dinner table.

> **Leviticus 7:19–20**
> *And the flesh that toucheth any unclean thing shall not be eaten; it shall be burnt with fire: and as for the flesh, all that be clean shall eat thereof. But the soul that eateth of the flesh of the sacrifice of peace offerings, that pertain unto Yahuah, having his uncleanness upon him, even that soul shall be cut off from his people.*

That was the line, and the penalty for crossing it was severe. Meat from a peace offering was holy, and an unclean person could not touch it. But meat killed at home was never holy. The line simply did not reach it. That is why Deuteronomy 12:22 can say the unclean and the clean eat it alike. They are not eating an offering. They are eating supper.

### The Blemished Firstborn

Deuteronomy 15 applies the same ruling to a second case. A firstborn animal belonged to Yahuah and went to the altar. But if it had a blemish, it could not be offered.

> **Deuteronomy 15:21–22**
> *And if there be any blemish therein, as if it be lame, or blind, or have any ill blemish, thou shalt not sacrifice it unto Yahuah thy Elohim. Thou shalt eat it within thy gates: the unclean and the clean person shall eat it alike, as the roebuck, and as the hart.*

Once the blemish took the animal off the altar, it became common meat, and the same rule applied. The KJV adds the word “person” here, which settles who the unclean one is.

### The Rules That Never Moved

Even at home, even with common meat, two rules stayed in force. The animal had to be a clean kind. Deuteronomy 12 speaks of the herd and the flock, the ox, the sheep, and the goat, and the clean-animal list of Deuteronomy 14 comes two chapters later in the same book. Nothing in this passage lets an unclean person, or anyone else, eat swine. And the blood was never eaten.

> **Deuteronomy 12:23–24**
> *Only be sure that thou eat not the blood: for the blood is the life; and thou mayest not eat the life with the flesh. Thou shalt not eat it; thou shalt pour it upon the earth as water.*

The setting changed from the altar to the backyard. The blood law did not change at all. It belonged to Yahuah whether the animal died at the tabernacle door or behind the house.

## Part III — The Thread Carried Forward

### Two Words in Peter’s Mouth

The same two lines appear again in the book of Acts. When Peter saw the sheet let down from heaven, he answered with two words, not one.

> **Acts 10:14**
> *But Peter said, Not so, Yahuah; for I have never eaten any thing that is common or unclean.*

The Greek words are koinos (G2839), common, and akathartos (G169), unclean. Peter did not say the same thing twice. He named both lines the Torah had always kept apart. The sheet was about the nations being welcomed to the table (Acts 10:28), not about the definition of food changing. But Peter’s own words show that a believing first-century Israelite still knew that common and unclean were two different things, exactly as Deuteronomy 12 taught.

### Priests Who Know the Difference

Teaching the difference between these lines was the work of the priest from the beginning (Leviticus 10:10–11). The prophets carry that work forward to the restored house of Yahuah.

> **Ezekiel 44:23**
> *And they shall teach my people the difference between the holy and profane, and cause them to discern between the unclean and the clean.*

The apostles call the believing assembly a royal priesthood (1 Peter 2:9). A priesthood that cannot tell holy from common, or common from unclean, cannot do the first thing a priest was given to do.

### Drawing Near

The whole purpose of purification in the Torah was approach. It answered the question of who may come near to the holy things of Yahuah. The letter to the Hebrews uses exactly that language for the believer.

> **Hebrews 10:22**
> *Let us draw near with a true heart in full assurance of faith, having our hearts sprinkled from an evil conscience, and our bodies washed with pure water.*

Sprinkling and washing are the purification words of the Torah. The way into the holy was opened by Yahuah through the blood of His Son. That is the entry. After it comes the walk, and the walk is the commandments.

## Part IV — What This Means

### Unclean Does Not Mean Sinful

Much of modern Christian teaching reads every “unclean” in the Law as a picture of sin. From there the conclusion follows quickly: the cross dealt with sin, so the whole subject of clean and unclean was swept away. Deuteronomy 12:22 shows that reading to be far too simple.

In this verse an unclean person sits at the family table with full permission from Yahuah. Nobody is calling him wicked. His uncleanness is not rebellion. It is a matter of nearness to the holy. When every category in the Law is flattened into “sin,” the Law stops making sense, and then it is easy to declare it finished.

### Three Lines, All Still Standing

Look at what this single passage holds at once. The clean-animal line is in force, because only the herd and the flock are being killed. The blood line is in force, because the blood goes on the ground. The holy-and-common line is in force, because the unclean person is kept from the altar meat and only from the altar meat. Nothing in Deuteronomy 12 is relaxed. Each rule is kept in its proper place.

That is the pattern of the whole Torah. It is not a pile of restrictions. It is a set of distinctions, given so that people would know what belongs to Yahuah, what belongs to daily life, and what was never food at all.

### The Order of Things

None of this is the way in. Deliverance comes first, through the blood Yahuah provided in His Son. But a believer who has entered is not then turned loose without a map. The same Law that set these lines at the family table still teaches the believer how to walk, and Yahushua said plainly that not one jot or tittle would pass from it until heaven and earth pass (Matthew 5:18).

## Conclusion

Deuteronomy 12:22 is not a loophole. It is a lesson in how carefully the Torah separated things that look alike. Common meat is not holy meat. An unclean person is not an unclean animal. A temporary state is not a sin. And through all of it, the clean-animal law and the blood law never moved an inch.

The line between the holy and the common runs all the way to the last page of the Book. At the end, the table is holy, and only the clean come near: “there shall in no wise enter into it any thing that defileth” (Revelation 21:27). What the Law allowed at the common table will not be allowed at that one, and “blessed are they that do his commandments, that they may have right to the tree of life” (Revelation 22:14).
