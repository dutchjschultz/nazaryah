## What Yahuah Is Doing

Yahuah means to be known. Not guessed at and not argued over, but known, by every nation on the earth.

His way of doing that has never changed. He puts somebody in place to carry it — somebody who lives it out in the open where the rest of the world can watch.

Isaiah is the record of who was holding that assignment. It changes hands twice before the book is finished, and the whole book is readable once a person knows which hand it is in.

## When This Is Happening

These chapters finish the second of Isaiah's four stretches of time — chapters 40 through 48, the Babylon captivity and the road home, roughly 605 to 538 BC.

The people hearing this are living in a foreign country as the property of an empire. By the end of chapter 48 they are told to pack up and walk out of it.

## What Should Have Happened Next

Chapter 42 ended with Yahuah calling His own servant blind and His own messenger deaf.

A man who hired a worker that useless would fire him. That is not what happens. For six straight chapters Yahuah keeps calling them by the same name.

## Blind, and Still Witnesses

> “Bring forth the blind people that have eyes, and the deaf that have ears … Ye are my witnesses, saith Yahuah, and my servant whom I have chosen.”
>
> — Isaiah 43:8, 10

Two verses apart. The blind people and the chosen servant are the same people.

A witness is somebody who was there and can say what he saw. He is calling blind men His witnesses on purpose, because what makes them witnesses is not their eyesight. It is that He chose them.

## Said Again, and Again

Chapter 44 opens with it twice in two verses — hear, O Jacob my servant; fear not, O Jacob, my servant (44:1–2).

> “Remember these, O Jacob and Israel; for thou art my servant: I have formed thee; thou art my servant: O Israel, thou shalt not be forgotten of me.”
>
> — Isaiah 44:21

Chapter 45 says it again, and adds something: for Jacob my servant's sake, and Israel mine elect, I have even called thee by thy name (45:4). That sentence is spoken about a pagan king, and the king is being used for the servant's sake.

## A Pagan King Named Out Loud

Cyrus is named in 44:28 and again in 45:1, generations before he was born. He is called an anointed deliverer, and Ezra records him sending the captives home.

Being used to deliver did not make him divine. These same chapters say there is no saviour beside Yahuah (43:11). A man can be handed a job without being handed anything else.

## Why the Idols Are in These Chapters

Running alongside all of this is a long teardown of idols, and it belongs here for a reason.

A man cuts down a tree. He burns half of it to cook his supper and warm his hands. Out of what is left he carves a figure, bows to it, and asks it to deliver him (44:14–17).

Then comes the comparison that settles it. When Babylon fell, the gods had to be loaded onto pack animals and hauled out of the city.

> “Hearken unto me … which are borne by me from the belly … and even to hoar hairs will I carry you.”
>
> — Isaiah 46:3–4

One kind of god has to be carried. The other kind does the carrying. A blind servant belonging to the second kind is still better off than a seeing man belonging to the first.

## The Last Time

Chapter 48 is where this stretch ends, and it carries the final use of the title for the nation.

> “Go ye forth of Babylon … say ye, Yahuah hath redeemed his servant Jacob.”
>
> — Isaiah 48:20

That is the last time in the book of Isaiah that the nation is called His servant. Two verses later the stretch closes: there is no peace, saith Yahuah, unto the wicked (48:22).

## A Second Line of Evidence

Isaiah's four stretches of time were not worked out from the servant. They came from somewhere else: one sentence repeated at the close of two of them, and a change of tense between chapters 39 and 40.

The servant is a separate thread, traced a separate way. And it stops exactly where this stretch stops. The nation holds the title through 48:20 and never again after it.

Part Four is the other half of that seam.

## Where It Lands

The order to walk out of Babylon is not the last time it is given.

> “Come out of her, my people, that ye be not partakers of her sins.”
>
> — Revelation 18:4

Same command, same city by name, at the end of the Book.

## In One Sentence

Yahuah called them blind and kept calling them His servant anyway, right up to the day He told them to walk out of Babylon.
