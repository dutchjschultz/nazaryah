***kavod** — heaviness, the substance of the One who sits*

## Part One — The Surface

### 1.1 — What People Think Glory Means

Glory is one of the most common words in the Bible, but it is also one of the most misunderstood. Most people hear "glory" and think of bright light, glowing clouds, or the emotional feeling in a worship service. Those things are not completely wrong, but they barely scratch the surface. To reduce glory to something you see or something you feel is to miss the whole point of what Yahuah has been showing us from the beginning of Scripture to the end.

In many churches today, "glory" has become a worship atmosphere word. It describes the feeling in the room when the music is moving. It describes the emotional intensity of a service. People say "the glory fell" and mean that something stirred inside them. Real encounters with Yahuah can certainly touch the emotions, but reducing glory to a feeling strips the word of its biblical weight. Glory is real whether anyone feels it or not.

Others assume glory is simply brightness or spectacle. The cloud in the wilderness, the light at the transfiguration, the radiance from Moses' face — people see these and conclude that glory is what you see when God shows up. But those are outward signs of something deeper. The light is not the glory. The glory is the character being revealed. When Ezekiel saw the glory leave the temple, the problem was not that the building got darker. The problem was that Yahuah's presence — His nature, His covenant faithfulness — was withdrawing from His people.

Still others treat glory as proof of divinity. The assumption is that any connection to Yahuah's glory automatically means that person is God. But the biblical pattern shows that Yahuah's *kavod* rests on people, places, and objects that He chooses for His purposes — the tabernacle, the temple, Moses, the nation of Israel, and ultimately Yahushua. Being a vessel of Yahuah's glory is not the same as being God. It is being chosen to reveal who God is.

This chapter is built on a simple idea: glory, in the Bible, is the revelation of Yahuah's character — who He is and what He is like. The Messiah, Yahushua of Nazareth, was the one chosen and foreknown before the world began to carry out and display that character for all mankind. The cross is the moment where that display reaches its peak. Everything in the Old Testament — the tabernacle, the temple, the glory cloud, the veil over Moses' face, and the prophecies about the coming Messiah — was all building toward that one moment of revelation.

## Part Two — The Root

### 2.1 — Kavod — The Weight of God's Character

**כָּבוֹד**

***Kavod***

*Weight, heaviness, substance. The full gravity of who Yahuah is — His presence, His nature, His character made real and visible in the world.*

In the Old Testament, the main Hebrew word for "glory" is *kavod* (כָּבוֹד). At its root, this word simply means "weight" or "heaviness." When used for a person, it could mean their wealth, their reputation, or the seriousness of their presence. When used for Yahuah, it takes on its deepest meaning: the full weight of who God is — His presence, His nature, His character made real and visible in the world.

This matters more than most people realize. *Kavod* is not just brightness. It is substance. It is weight you can feel. When the Old Testament says Yahuah's glory filled the tabernacle or the temple, it is not just talking about a light display. It is saying that the heaviness of Yahuah's own nature was pressing into the physical world, making itself known in a way that people could actually encounter.

### 2.2 — Kaved — The Root Beneath the Root

The root of *kavod* is *kaved* (כָּבֵד), which means heavy, weighty, or burdensome. This same root appears in Exodus 14:4 when Yahuah says He will get glory — *kavod* — through Pharaoh. Yahuah's character would be displayed through the events of the Exodus: His power, His faithfulness to His promises, His justice against the oppressor, His mercy toward His people. The weight of who Yahuah is was being revealed through what He did. That is *kavod*.

### 2.3 — Shakan — Glory Moves In

**שָׁכַן**

***Shakan***

*To dwell, to settle down, to take up residence. The root of Shekinah. Not a visit — a permanent move.*

In Exodus 40:34–35, when the tabernacle is finished, the glory of Yahuah fills it so powerfully that Moses himself cannot go inside. The cloud covers the tent of meeting, and the weight of Yahuah's presence settles into this structure of animal skins and wood.

The Hebrew word for what Yahuah's presence does here is *shakan* (שָׁכַן) — to dwell, to settle down, to take up residence. This is the root of what later became known as the Shekinah glory. It does not mean God passed through or visited briefly. It means He moved in. He settled His weight — His *kavod* — into a physical space and stayed. The fullness of His character and presence was now living among His people in a tent.

But a tent is temporary by nature. It moves. It wears out. It was never meant to be the final arrangement. It was pointing forward.

### 2.4 — Qaran and Panim — Power Radiating from the Face

**קָרַן**

***Qaran***

*To radiate, to send out beams. Root: qeren (קֶרֶן) — horn, symbol of power and authority.*

In Exodus 34, Moses comes down from Mount Sinai after being in the direct presence of Yahuah. His face is radiating. The people see it and they are afraid. So Moses puts a veil over his face.

The word used for Moses' face "shining" is not the normal Hebrew word for light or brightness. It is *qaran* (קָרַן). The root of this word is *qeren*, which means "horn." In Hebrew, horns represent power, authority, and strength. This is why the Latin Vulgate translated it as "horns," and why Michelangelo put horns on his famous statue of Moses. They got the root right but missed the deeper meaning. Moses' face was not simply glowing like a lamp. It was radiating authority and power — beams projecting outward from having been in the direct presence of Yahuah. His face was displaying the weight — the *kavod* — of Yahuah's authority.

The word for the veil itself is *masveh* (מַסְוֶה). This word appears only here in the entire Old Testament — nowhere else. It is completely unique to this event. That tells us this is not an ordinary covering. It has a specific purpose tied to this exact moment: the concealing of glory.

And the word for face — *panim* (פָּנִים) — is the same word used for Yahuah's own presence throughout the Old Testament. When Moses stood "before the face of God," that is *panim*. The *panim* of Moses was radiating after being in the *panim* of Yahuah. This word becomes very important when we get to the New Testament.

## Part Three — The Ancient Ground

### 3.1 — The Tabernacle — Glory Takes Up Residence

The tabernacle was the first structure where Yahuah's *kavod* *shakan* — settled, moved in, took up residence. Exodus 40:34–35 describes the cloud covering the tent and the weight of Yahuah's presence filling it so powerfully that Moses could not enter. The fullness of His character was now dwelling among His people in a tent made of animal skins and wood.

The tabernacle was never meant to be permanent. It moved. It wore out. It was a picture of something coming — a day when the glory would not dwell in a structure, but in a person.

### 3.2 — The Departure of Glory — Ezekiel's Vision

One of the most devastating moments in Israel's history is not recorded on a battlefield. It happens in a prophetic vision. In Ezekiel chapters 10 and 11, the prophet watches as the *kavod* of Yahuah — the glory that had filled the tabernacle and later Solomon's temple — begins to leave.

The *kavod* rises from the mercy seat — the *kapporet* (כַּפֹּרֶת), the place of atonement, the very seat where Yahuah's presence dwelt between the cherubim. From there it moves to the threshold of the temple. Then to the east gate. Then out to the Mount of Olives. And then it is gone.

The *kavod* that had *shakan* — settled, dwelt, taken up residence — was now leaving. The same weight that had moved into the tabernacle in Exodus, that had filled Solomon's temple so powerfully that the priests could not stand (1 Kings 8:11), was withdrawing. Israel was left with a building, but the building was empty. The structure was still standing, but the weight of Yahuah's presence had departed. The temple became a shell — religion without the reality of Yahuah's character behind it.

### 3.3 — Moses' Veil — What Was Really Being Hidden

Most people read the story of Moses' veil and think it was simply because the brightness was too much for the people. But there is far more going on, and Paul reveals it in 2 Corinthians 3:13.

Most English translations say Moses veiled his face so Israel could not see the glory "fading away" or "coming to an end." But the Greek tells a different story. The word translated "fading" is *katargeo* (καταργέω). This word does not naturally mean a slow dimming. It means "to render inactive, to nullify, to make of no effect." Paul uses it throughout his letters, and it never means gradual fading anywhere else.

The word translated "end" is *telos* (τέλος), and this is the key. *Telos* does not just mean "end" as in "it stopped." *Telos* means goal, purpose, outcome — what something is aimed at. It is the same word used in Romans 10:4: "The Messiah is the *telos* of the law" — the goal, the aim, the destination of the law.

So what Paul is actually saying is this: Moses put the veil over his face so that Israel could not see the purpose — the goal — of what was being displayed. The veil was not hiding a fading light. The veil was hiding what the glory on Moses' face was pointing to. Israel saw the brightness, the power, the authority radiating from his *panim* — but they could not perceive the meaning. The glory had a destination, a *telos*, and the veil blocked their view of it.

Paul then says that same kind of veil remains over the hearts of people who read the Old Testament but do not see what it is pointing to. They read the words but miss the deeper purpose underneath.

## Part Four — The Bridge

### 4.1 — Doxa — From Greek Opinion to Hebrew Weight

In the Greek New Testament, the word used for glory is *doxa* (δόξα). In everyday Greek, *doxa* just meant "opinion" or "reputation." But when the Old Testament was translated into Greek, the translators used this word to carry the meaning of *kavod*. So by the time the New Testament writers use *doxa*, it is packed with that same Hebrew weight. It means Yahuah's nature and character revealed. Not what people think about God — but what God actually is, made known. However, something is always lost in translation, and throughout this chapter we go back to the Hebrew to recover what the Greek and English sometimes flatten out.

### 4.2 — The Tabernacle Becomes a Person

When the apostle John describes Yahushua coming into the world, he uses language that goes straight back to the tabernacle. In John 1:14, he writes: "The Word became flesh and tabernacled among us, and we beheld his glory, glory as of the only begotten from the Father, full of grace and truth."

John's word choice is deliberate. The Greek word he uses is *eskēnōsen* (ἐσκήνωσεν), which means "pitched a tent, took up dwelling." It echoes the Hebrew *shakan* directly. The glory that once *shakan* — settled, moved in, took up residence — in a tent of animal skins, now *shakan* in a human life. The tabernacle in the wilderness was a picture of what Yahuah would one day do through a person. The character of Yahuah, His favor and His truth, would dwell in a man and be seen by human eyes. What Moses could not enter, John says he beheld — he saw it with his own eyes. The glory became accessible. No longer behind a curtain. Walking among people.

### 4.3 — The Veil Removed — From Moses' Face to the Messiah's

Now look at what Paul writes in 2 Corinthians 4:6: "For God, who said, 'Let light shine out of darkness,' has shone in our hearts to give the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Yahushua the Messiah."

There it is. The *panim* of Moses — veiled — glory hidden — Israel could not see the purpose. The *panim* of the Messiah — unveiled — glory revealed — now we see clearly. Moses' face was the preview. The glory radiating from it had a *telos* — a destination. That destination was the face of Yahushua the Messiah. The veil kept Israel from seeing where it was all heading. But now, in the Messiah, there is no veil. The glory of Yahuah's character is fully on display in a human face, and anyone can look at it openly.

And this is what Paul means in 2 Corinthians 3:18 when he says: "We all, with unveiled face, beholding the glory of the Lord, are being transformed into the same image from one degree of glory to another." Under the old arrangement, the glory on Moses' *panim* was covered. The people could not see its purpose. But now, for those who see what Yahuah has done through the Messiah, the veil is gone. And the result is not just seeing — it is transformation. We are being changed into the same image, from glory to glory. The revelation of Yahuah's character is no longer hidden behind a covering on one man's face. It is an ongoing work that changes the person from the inside out.

### 4.4 — Hebrews 1:3 — The Exact Imprint

Hebrews 1:3 says Yahushua is "the radiance of God's glory and the exact imprint of his nature." The Greek word for "exact imprint" is *charaktēr* (χαρακτήρ) — like the impression a seal leaves in wax. Yahushua is the stamp of Yahuah's character in human form. This does not have to mean Yahushua is God Himself. What it means is that everything you need to know about Yahuah's character — His mercy, His justice, His faithfulness, His love — is perfectly pressed into this man. He is the human image of the invisible God's nature, chosen and empowered to make that nature known to the world.

## Part Five — The Discovery

### 5.1 — The Spirit That Settles — Isaiah 11:2

Colossians 2:9 says "in him the whole fullness of deity dwells bodily." Many read this and assume it means Yahushua is God walking around in a human body. But consider what "fullness" means in light of the Old Testament. Isaiah 11:2 describes the Spirit that would rest on the Messiah: "The Spirit of Yahuah shall rest upon him, the Spirit of wisdom and understanding, the Spirit of counsel and might, the Spirit of knowledge and the fear of Yahuah."

The Hebrew word for "rest" here is *nuach* (נוּח) — to settle down, to remain, to find a resting place. It is related to *shakan* in that both carry the idea of permanent settling, not just a temporary visit. The Spirit of Yahuah would not just come upon the Messiah for a moment. It would *nuach* — settle down and stay. These are the seven Spirits of God spoken of throughout Scripture — the complete expression of Yahuah's character and power.

The "fullness of deity" dwelling in Yahushua means that the full measure of Yahuah's Spirit — every aspect of His character and power — was given to this man without limit. Yahushua did not operate on a partial anointing. He carried the complete expression of Yahuah's nature through the Spirit. That is what made him the perfect vessel of Yahuah's glory — not that he was God Himself, but that Yahuah's Spirit dwelt in him fully, enabling him to display the Father's character in everything he said and did.

### 5.2 — The Return of the Kavod

This is what makes the New Testament events so remarkable, and this connection needs to be felt for what it is. Centuries after Ezekiel watched the *kavod* depart from the temple, Yahushua of Nazareth walks into that same temple. He teaches in its courts. He confronts the corruption in it. He drives out the money changers. He heals in its porches. He declares truth in its halls.

And here is the connection that should stop every reader in their tracks: Yahushua eventually stands on the Mount of Olives — the exact location where Ezekiel watched the *kavod* of Yahuah depart from Israel. The exact spot. This is not a coincidence, and it cannot be overlooked. Anyone in that day who knew the prophets would have felt the weight of it. The glory left from the Mount of Olives. Now a man who carries the full character of Yahuah — His justice, His compassion, His faithfulness — is standing in that same place. The *kavod* that departed through Ezekiel's vision was returning through the life of the Messiah.

What Ezekiel watched leave was coming back. Not in a cloud this time, but in a person. The *kavod* that had *shakan* in a tent, that had *shakan* in a temple, had now *shakan* in the Messiah (John 1:14). And he was standing in the very place where the old glory had departed, bringing it back — not to fill a building, but to fill the whole earth with the knowledge of Yahuah's character. The empty temple was being replaced. Not the stones — but what the stones always represented: the dwelling of Yahuah's glory among His people.

### 5.3 — The Cross — Where Glory Reaches Its Fullness

In John 12:23–24, Yahushua says: "The hour has come for the Son of Man to be glorified. Truly, truly, I say to you, unless a grain of wheat falls into the earth and dies, it remains alone; but if it dies, it bears much fruit."

Yahushua defines his glorification not as a coronation, not as a military victory, not as a show of supernatural power — but as death. The grain of wheat has to fall into the ground and die. This is the moment where the full weight — the *kavod* — of Yahuah's character is put on display for all of humanity to see.

In John 12:32–33 he says: "And I, when I am lifted up from the earth, will draw all people to myself." John adds: "He said this to show by what kind of death he was going to die." There is a double meaning in "lifted up." In the Old Testament, the Hebrew word *rum* (רוּם) means both to physically lift up and to exalt in honor and glory. Isaiah 52:13 uses this very word about the suffering servant: "Behold, my servant shall act wisely; he shall be high and lifted up (*rum*) and exalted." The same servant who is described as disfigured and crushed in Isaiah 53 is the one who is *rum* — lifted up, exalted. The humiliation is the exaltation. The suffering is the glory.

In John 17:1, right before his arrest, Yahushua prays: "Father, the hour has come; glorify your Son that the Son may glorify you." The cross glorifies both the Father and the Son at the same time. Yahuah's character is glorified because the cross puts every part of who He is on display at once. His justice — sin is not swept under the rug. His love — He gives His Son for the sake of the world. His faithfulness — every promise spoken through the prophets about the suffering servant is fulfilled. His mercy — through the death of one, many are brought back to Yahuah.

And Yahushua is glorified because through his obedience, even to the point of death, he perfectly carried out the character and purpose of the Father. Philippians 2:8–11 captures this: "He humbled himself by becoming obedient to the point of death, even death on a cross. Therefore God has highly exalted him and bestowed on him the name that is above every name." The road to glory runs straight through the cross. It always did.

### 5.4 — A Note on Foreknowledge and Glory

Some people take John 17:5 — where Yahushua says "Glorify me in your own presence with the glory I had with you before the world existed" — as proof that Yahushua literally existed as a divine being sharing Yahuah's own glory before creation. This is a common reading in Trinitarian theology, and it is often connected to Isaiah 42:8, where Yahuah says, "My glory I give to no other." The reasoning is: if Yahushua had glory before the world began, and Yahuah does not share His glory with anyone, then Yahushua must be God.

But two things need to be considered. First, Isaiah 42:8 is spoken in a specific context. Yahuah is addressing Israel within the framework of Deuteronomy 32:8–9, where the nations were divided among angelic beings, but Yahuah kept Israel as His own portion. When He says "I give my glory to no other," He is making a statement about covenant loyalty against the rival gods of the nations. He is telling Israel: I am your God, not those others. It is not a statement about the inner nature of a future messiah.

Second, foreknowledge is one of the biggest themes in the Bible, and it has to be taken seriously. The Hebrew concept here is *yada* (יָדַע) — to know, but not just intellectually. *Yada* means intimate, purposeful knowledge. When Yahuah "knew" something before creation, it means He had set His purpose and intention on it. It was determined, planned, and certain in His mind. 1 Peter 1:20 says the Messiah was "foreknown before the foundation of the world but was made manifest in the last times." Revelation 13:8 speaks of "the Lamb slain from the foundation of the world." Nobody argues that the crucifixion literally happened before creation. It existed in the purpose and plan of Yahuah.

In the same way, the glory Yahushua speaks of in John 17:5 can be understood as the glory that existed in Yahuah's plan from the beginning — the glory of the cross, the glory of the messianic mission, held in the foreknowledge of Yahuah and now being carried out in real time. This distinction matters because it lines up with what the rest of Scripture teaches: Yahushua is the fulfillment of Yahuah's plan, the agent of His purpose, the one in whom Yahuah's character is made known. The glory he has is given by the Father (John 17:22), not something he possesses as a co-equal God.

## Part Six — The Restoration

### 6.1 — Tselem — The Image Restored

**צֶלֶם**

***Tselem***

*Image, representation, likeness. A form that displays the nature of the original. Not physical appearance — moral and spiritual character.*

Yahushua says in John 17:22: "The glory that you have given me I have given to them." While Yahuah's glory is uniquely His, Scripture is clear that He shares it with purpose. Glory, understood as the revelation of Yahuah's character, is meant to be carried, displayed, and passed along.

This connects directly to what it means to be made in the image of God. The image of God is not about physical appearance — Yahuah is Spirit. The image of God is about holiness and purity. It is a moral and spiritual condition. The Hebrew word for image, *tselem* (צֶלֶם), means a representation, a likeness that displays the nature of the original. Adam carried this *tselem* before sin — he reflected Yahuah's character in holiness, purity, and righteousness. When sin entered, that image was corrupted. Humanity still bore a distorted version of it, but the holiness and purity were gone.

This is why the New Testament speaks of believers being made into a "new creation" (2 Corinthians 5:17) and being "renewed in knowledge after the image of its creator" (Colossians 3:10). What the Messiah restores is the *tselem* — the image of God — the holiness and purity that reflect Yahuah's character. This means glory, the image of God, and sanctification are all part of the same picture. To be sanctified is to have Yahuah's image restored. To have Yahuah's image restored is to carry His glory. They are inseparable.

### 6.2 — Glory and Trust — The Connection

It is worth noting that Hebrews 11:1 tells us "trust is the substance of things hoped for, the evidence of things not seen." That word "substance" points to a firm foundation, solid ground to stand on. Trust is not a feeling — it is a firm foundation. Glory and trust are connected in this way. Glory is the weight of Yahuah's character revealed, and trust is the firm ground on which we stand to receive it. Neither one is based on emotion. Both are rooted in something real and solid.

### 6.3 — Putting Glory Back Where It Belongs

Glory, from the first pages of the Bible to the last, is the revelation of Yahuah's character. The Hebrew word *kavod* tells us it is weight — the full substance of who Yahuah is. The word *shakan* tells us it is a glory that moves in and stays. The word *qaran* tells us it radiates with power and authority. The word *panim* tells us it is connected to presence and to face — the face of Moses that was veiled, and the face of the Messiah where it is finally revealed. The word *tselem* tells us that humanity was created to carry this glory as the image of God, and that the Messiah restores what was lost.

In the Old Testament, this revelation came in shadows and patterns — a cloud filling a tent, a face radiating after a divine encounter, a presence dwelling above the mercy seat. These were real moments of glory, but they were partial. The *masveh* over Moses' face showed that the purpose of the glory was hidden. The departure of *kavod* from Ezekiel's temple showed what happens when the substance behind the symbol is rejected.

But Yahuah's plan, foreknown (*yada*) from before the foundation of the world, was always driving toward something greater. In the Messiah, Yahushua of Nazareth, the glory that once filled a tent took up residence (*shakan*) in a human life. The character of Yahuah — His justice, His love, His faithfulness, His mercy — was carried and displayed by a man who had the full measure of Yahuah's Spirit resting (*nuach*) on him. The seven Spirits of Isaiah 11:2 — the complete expression of Yahuah's nature — settled on the Messiah without limit. This is what Colossians 2:9 means when it says the fullness of deity dwelt in him bodily. Not that he was God Himself, but that Yahuah's Spirit filled him completely, empowering him to live out and display the Father's character in every word and every action.

And the cross is where all of it comes together. What the world saw as defeat, Yahushua called glorification. What looked like the absence of God was actually the fullest display of His nature ever seen. On the cross, the full weight — the *kavod* — of who Yahuah is was laid bare: a God who is just and does not ignore sin, a God who loves and gives His Son for the world, a God who is faithful and fulfills every word spoken through the prophets, a God who is merciful and brings the undeserving back to Himself.

This is what glory has always been about. Not spectacle. Not brightness. Not a theological argument about divine nature. Glory is the weight of Yahuah's character pressing into the world through His chosen vessel, the Messiah, so that all mankind might see, know, and be transformed by who Yahuah truly is.
