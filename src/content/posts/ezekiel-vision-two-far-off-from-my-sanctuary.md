“In the sixth year, in the sixth month, in the fifth day of the month” (8:1), about 592 BC, fourteen months after the first vision. Ezekiel is sitting in his house in Babylon, and the elders of Judah are sitting in front of him. They have come to hear a word. The temple in Jerusalem is still standing, and the people back home are still sure it will keep them safe. In the first vision Yahuah showed Ezekiel His throne on wheels. In this one He takes Ezekiel by the hair of his head, carries him to Jerusalem, and walks him through the building everyone was trusting.

## The Vision

Ezekiel is shown four rooms of abomination: an idol at the north gate, seventy elders burning incense in the dark, women weeping for Tammuz, and twenty-five men with their backs to the temple bowing to the sun. A man in linen marks the foreheads of those who grieve over it, and six men strike everyone else, beginning at the sanctuary. Coals are scattered over the city, and step by step the glory of Yahuah leaves the house and stops on the mountain to the east.

## The Image of Jealousy

The first thing Ezekiel sees, right at the north gate, is “the seat of the image of jealousy, which provoketh to jealousy” (8:3). To understand the name, go back to where jealousy first appears with Yahuah’s name on it. At Sinai, in the commandment against images, He said, “Thou shalt not bow down thyself to them, nor serve them: for I Yahuah thy Elohim am a jealous El” (Exodus 20:5). Later He said, “Yahuah, whose name is Jealous, is a jealous El” (Exodus 34:14). Jealousy is a husband’s word. Israel was His bride, and an idol in His house was another lover brought into the marriage bed.

Moses had warned it would happen: “They provoked him to jealousy with strange gods” (Deuteronomy 32:16). Now an idol stands at the gate, and Yahuah asks Ezekiel the question the whole vision answers: “Son of man, seest thou what they do? even the great abominations that the house of Israel committeth here, that I should go far off from my sanctuary?” (8:6). Their own idols were driving Him out.

## Seventy Elders in the Dark

Ezekiel is told to dig through a wall, and he finds a hidden room. On every wall are painted “every form of creeping things, and abominable beasts, and all the idols of the house of Israel,” and in front of them stand “seventy men of the ancients of the house of Israel,” every man with “his censer in his hand” (8:10-11).

Seventy elders have a history. At Sinai, seventy elders went up the mountain with Moses, “and they saw the Elohim of Israel: and there was under his feet as it were a paved work of a sapphire stone” (Exodus 24:9-10). These were meant to be the men closest to Yahuah. Now the seventy are bowing to pictures of crawling animals in a dark room. Paul described the same fall: men “changed the glory of the uncorruptible Elohim into an image made like to corruptible man, and to birds, and fourfooted beasts, and creeping things” (Romans 1:23).

Ezekiel is given one name: Jaazaniah the son of Shaphan (8:11). Shaphan was the scribe who read the newly found book of the Law to King Josiah and set off Judah’s great return to it (2 Kings 22:8-10). One generation later, his son is standing in the dark with a censer in his hand.

Then comes their reason: “Yahuah seeth us not; Yahuah hath forsaken the earth” (8:12). Remember the first vision. The wheels of the throne were “full of eyes round about” (1:18). The men in the dark thought no one was watching. The One whose wheels are covered with eyes had just walked Ezekiel into their room.

## Weeping for Tammuz

At the door of the north gate of Yahuah’s house, “there sat women weeping for Tammuz” (8:14). Tammuz was a god of Babylon and its neighbors, mourned every year as a dying god. The women were weeping for a foreign god’s death while their own city’s judgment was only a few years away.

## Backs to the Temple

The worst is saved for last. In the inner court, “between the porch and the altar,” stand about twenty-five men “with their backs toward the temple of Yahuah, and their faces toward the east; and they worshipped the sun toward the east” (8:16).

Every detail matters. Between the porch and the altar was the priests’ place. The tabernacle and the temple were built with their doors facing east and the most holy place at the west end (Exodus 27:13). A man coming to worship walked west, toward Yahuah. To face east, these men had to turn their backs on Him. Moses had warned against this very thing: “lest thou lift up thine eyes unto heaven, and when thou seest the sun, and the moon, and the stars … shouldest be driven to worship them” (Deuteronomy 4:19). The Nazaryah page [*Sun Worship*](/sun-worship) traces this counterfeit from Babel forward.

## The Man With the Inkhorn

Now judgment comes to the house. Six men arrive “from the way of the higher gate, which lieth toward the north,” each with a weapon, and with them “one man among them was clothed with linen, with a writer’s inkhorn by his side” (9:2). Linen was the dress of the priest, and of the high priest on the Day of Atonement (Leviticus 16:4). Before anyone is struck, this man is sent through the city first: “set a mark upon the foreheads of the men that sigh and that cry for all the abominations that be done in the midst thereof” (9:4). The word for mark is *tav*, the name of the last letter of the Hebrew alphabet, which in the old script was written as a simple cross-mark.

A mark that spares has a history too. In Egypt the blood on the doorposts was the sign, and Yahuah said, “when I see the blood, I will pass over you” (Exodus 12:13). Here it is a mark on the forehead, and it goes only on people who are grieved by what the rest of the city has made normal. Not on the people who attended the temple. On the people who mourned over it.

Then the order to the six: “come not near any man upon whom is the mark; and begin at my sanctuary. Then they began at the ancient men which were before the house” (9:6). Judgment starts with the elders, at the house of Yahuah. Peter wrote the same rule for believers: “judgment must begin at the house of Elohim” (1 Peter 4:17).

## Coals Over the City

The man in linen is sent back in, this time between the wheels, to “fill thine hand with coals of fire from between the cherubims, and scatter them over the city” (10:2). When Isaiah saw the throne, a coal from the altar touched his lips and his sin was purged (Isaiah 6:6-7). The same fire that cleans one man’s lips burns a city that will not be cleaned.

## The Glory Leaves in Stages

To feel the weight of this, go back to when the glory came. When Moses finished the tabernacle, “the glory of Yahuah filled the tabernacle,” so that Moses could not enter (Exodus 40:34-35). When Solomon dedicated the temple, the cloud filled the house so that “the priests could not stand to minister” (1 Kings 8:10-11).

Ezekiel watches it leave, and it does not leave all at once. First the glory goes up from the cherub to the threshold of the house (9:3; 10:4). Then it rises from the threshold and stands over the cherubim, and Ezekiel knows these creatures: “This is the living creature that I saw by the river of Chebar” (10:15, 20). Then the whole throne lifts and stops “at the door of the east gate of Yahuah’s house” (10:19). Finally, “the glory of Yahuah went up from the midst of the city, and stood upon the mountain which is on the east side of the city” (11:23). That is the Mount of Olives.

Step by step, stopping at every doorway, as if waiting to be called back: “I will go and return to my place, till they acknowledge their offence” (Hosea 5:15). No one called. The incense kept burning, the men kept bowing to the sun, the services went on, and the glory was gone.

## A Little Sanctuary

The answer is the turn of the whole vision. The people still in Jerusalem were telling the captives, “Get you far from Yahuah: unto us is this land given in possession” (11:15). They thought the ones in the building were the ones close to Yahuah. Yahuah answers the opposite: “Although I have cast them far off among the heathen … yet will I be to them as a little sanctuary in the countries where they shall come” (11:16).

The glory left the people with the building and went to the people without one, with a promise: “I will give them one heart, and I will put a new spirit within you; and I will take the stony heart out of their flesh, and will give them an heart of flesh: That they may walk in my statutes, and keep mine ordinances, and do them” (11:19-20). A new heart, so the statutes can be kept. The house of stone failed. Yahuah would build His house inside them.

## Why This Vision, and Why Now

The elders in Ezekiel’s house still hoped Jerusalem would stand. This vision took that hope apart from the inside. The glory did not abandon a faithful house. It was pushed out of an unfaithful one, one doorway at a time, and within six years Babylon burned the empty building. The first vision showed Ezekiel the throne. This one showed him why it had to leave.

## Bringing It Home

A building can keep its services running long after the glory has gone. Everything looked like worship, and Yahuah called it abomination. Yahushua stands outside the door of a church in the same condition, at Laodicea, knocking (Revelation 3:20).

The question the vision asks every believer is the one the mark asks: do you sigh and cry over what has been made normal, or have you made peace with it? The mark went on the grieved, not the comfortable. And every heart has its chamber of imagery. “Yahuah seeth us not” was a lie then and is a lie now.

But the promise holds. To the ones carried away, Yahuah became a little sanctuary, and He still gives a new heart and a new spirit so His people can walk in His statutes.

## Where It Lands

Revelation keeps Ezekiel’s order. Before the winds of judgment are released, the command goes out: “Hurt not the earth, neither the sea, nor the trees, till we have sealed the servants of our Elohim in their foreheads” (Revelation 7:3). The marked stand with the Lamb, “having his Father’s name written in their foreheads” (Revelation 14:1). And the glory that walked out of a defiled house never has to leave again. In the new city John “saw no temple therein,” for “the glory of Elohim did lighten it, and the Lamb is the light thereof” (Revelation 21:22-23).
