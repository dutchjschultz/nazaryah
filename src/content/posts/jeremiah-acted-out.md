## Why Act It Out

Yahuah’s complaint in Jeremiah is that He kept speaking and His people would not hear (7:13). So He changed how He spoke. Again and again He told Jeremiah to do something strange in public: bury a belt, smash a jar, wear a yoke, buy a field in a war zone. People who tune out a sermon still stop and look at a man walking through town with a wooden yoke on his neck.

Each lesson below has three parts: what Jeremiah did, what it meant, and where it points.

## 1. The Rotted Belt — Chapter 13

**What he did.** Jeremiah bought a linen belt, wore it, and did not wash it. Then he walked to the Euphrates River and hid it in a crack in the rock. Many days later he went back and dug it up. It was rotted, “profitable for nothing” (13:1-7).

**What it meant.** “As the girdle cleaveth to the loins of a man, so have I caused to cleave unto me the whole house of Israel… that they might be unto me for a people, and for a name, and for a praise, and for a glory: but they would not hear” (13:11). Israel was meant to cling to Yahuah like a belt. Instead they went off toward Babylon’s river, and it ruined them (13:9-10). Linen was the priest’s cloth (Exodus 28:42). Israel was called to be “a kingdom of priests” (Exodus 19:6).

**Where it points.** The bride of the Lamb is dressed in “fine linen, clean and white: for the fine linen is the righteousness of saints” (Revelation 19:8). The linen is clean again.

## 2. The Potter — Chapter 18

**What he did.** Yahuah sent Jeremiah to watch a potter at work. A pot went wrong in the potter’s hands, so the potter squashed it and made it into another pot (18:1-4).

**What it meant.** “As the clay is in the potter’s hand, so are ye in mine hand” (18:6). While the clay is still soft, it can be reshaped. If a nation turns from its evil, Yahuah will turn from the judgment He spoke against it (18:7-8). The door was open. The people answered, “There is no hope: but we will walk after our own devices” (18:12).

**Where it points.** Paul uses the same picture: the potter has power over the clay (Romans 9:21).

## 3. The Smashed Jar — Chapter 19

**What he did.** This time Jeremiah bought a finished clay jar, already hardened in the fire. He took the elders and priests out to the valley of Hinnom, where children had been burned as offerings (19:5), and smashed the jar in front of them (19:1-10).

**What it meant.** “Even so will I break this people and this city, as one breaketh a potter’s vessel, that cannot be made whole again” (19:11). Chapter 18 was soft clay that could still be remade. Chapter 19 is fired clay that can only be broken. The two lessons sit side by side on purpose: there comes a point when the heart has hardened. For saying it, Jeremiah was beaten and put in the stocks (20:2).

**Where it points.** The valley of Hinnom is where the word *Gehenna* comes from, the word Yahushua used for the place of fire (Matthew 10:28).

## 4. The Figs — Chapter 24

**What he saw.** This one was shown, not acted. After the first group of captives was taken to Babylon in 597 BC, Yahuah showed Jeremiah two baskets of figs in front of the temple. One basket was very good. The other was so bad it could not be eaten (24:1-3).

**What it meant.** The surprise is which was which. The good figs were the ones carried off to Babylon. Yahuah sent them “for their good,” and He would give them “an heart to know me… for they shall return unto me with their whole heart” (24:5-7). That is the same promise as the new covenant: “I will put my law in their inward parts, and write it in their hearts” (31:33). The bad figs were the ones who stayed in Jerusalem feeling lucky, and the ones who ran to Egypt (24:8). Ezekiel heard how they talked. They said the captives were “far from Yahuah” and “unto us is this land given” (Ezekiel 11:15). They thought staying comfortable proved Yahuah was pleased with them. Being taken away was not the curse. Staying hard-hearted was.

**Where it points.** Yahushua looked for fruit on a fig tree and found only leaves (Mark 11:13-14). Yahuah still looks for fruit, not appearances.

For a fuller study of the fig tree in Scripture, see [*The Fig Tree*](/blog/the-fig-tree).

## 5. The Yoke — Chapters 27 and 28

**What he did.** Jeremiah made a wooden yoke, the bar oxen wear, and put it on his own neck. He sent word to the kings around Judah: put your necks under Babylon’s yoke and live (27:2, 12).

**What it meant.** Babylon was Yahuah’s discipline, and fighting it meant fighting Him. A prophet named Hananiah took the yoke off Jeremiah’s neck, broke it, and said Babylon would be finished in two years (28:10-11). Yahuah answered, “Thou hast broken the yokes of wood; but thou shalt make for them yokes of iron” (28:13). That is the “yoke of iron” Moses warned about for those who would not serve Yahuah with joy (Deuteronomy 28:47-48). Hananiah was dead within the year (28:17).

**Where it points.** Yahushua offered a different yoke: “Take my yoke upon you… and ye shall find rest unto your souls. For my yoke is easy” (Matthew 11:29-30). He took those words from Jeremiah 6:16, the old paths.

## 6. The Field — Chapter 32

**What he did.** Babylon’s army was camped around Jerusalem, and Jeremiah was locked in the prison court. His cousin came and asked him to buy the family field at Anathoth, because the right of redemption was his (32:7-8). Jeremiah paid seventeen shekels of silver, signed the deed in front of witnesses, and had it sealed in a clay jar “that they may continue many days” (32:9-14).

**What it meant.** Nobody buys land in a war that is already lost, unless he knows the war is not the end. “Houses and fields and vineyards shall be possessed again in this land” (32:15). The purchase followed the Law of redemption, where the nearest relative buys back what the family lost (Leviticus 25:25).

**Where it points.** Yahuah is the Redeemer who buys back what His people lost. Jeremiah’s prayer over the deed says it: “there is nothing too hard for thee” (32:17).

## 7. The Rechabites — Chapter 35

**What he did.** Jeremiah brought a family called the Rechabites into a room at the temple, set out bowls of wine, and told them to drink. They refused. More than two hundred years earlier, their forefather Jonadab had told them to drink no wine and live in tents, and they had kept his word ever since (35:5-10; 2 Kings 10:15).

**What it meant.** The lesson was not about wine. It was about listening. These people kept the word of a man for two centuries, while Judah would not keep the word of Yahuah, who had been “rising early and speaking” to them all along (35:14). Because the Rechabites obeyed their father, Yahuah promised that Jonadab would never lack a man to stand before Him (35:19).

**Where it points.** “If ye love me, keep my commandments” (John 14:15). The Rechabites kept a father’s word. Judah would not keep the Father’s.

## 8. The Stones in Egypt — Chapter 43

**What he did.** After Jerusalem fell, the survivors ran to Egypt against Yahuah’s word and took Jeremiah with them (43:4-7). At the city of Tahpanhes, Yahuah told him to bury large stones in the clay of the brick pavement at the entrance of Pharaoh’s house (43:8-9).

**What it meant.** “I will… take Nebuchadrezzar the king of Babylon, my servant, and will set his throne upon these stones that I have hid” (43:10). They ran to Egypt to get away from Babylon, and Babylon’s king would set his throne on the very spot. Moses had warned that disobedience would end with the people brought back into Egypt (Deuteronomy 28:68). Babylonian records tell of Nebuchadnezzar campaigning against Egypt in 568 or 567 BC.

**Where it points.** There is no hiding from Yahuah’s word by changing countries. “Whither shall I flee from thy presence?” (Psalm 139:7).

## What the Eight Lessons Say Together

The belt, the jar, and the yoke show what happens when a people will not hear. The potter shows the door that stays open while the clay is soft. The figs and the field show that Yahuah was building even while He tore down. The Rechabites show what hearing looks like. The stones in Egypt show that running does not work.

## Where It Lands

The smashed jar comes back at the end. To the assembly that overcomes, Messiah promises power over the nations: “and he shall rule them with a rod of iron; as the vessels of a potter shall they be broken to shivers: even as I received of my Father” (Revelation 2:27). The power He gives, He received from His Father. The jar Jeremiah broke in the valley is the picture of every power that will not hear.
