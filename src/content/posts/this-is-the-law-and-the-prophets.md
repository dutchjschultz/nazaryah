## Two Halves of One Command

Two blocks of the sermon sit far apart on the page and belong together. In the first, a man is the one being asked: struck, sued, pressed into service, approached for a loan (Matthew 5:39-42). In the second, he is the one asking (Matthew 7:7-11). Yahushua ties the two ends together and names what He has been summarizing: "Therefore all things whatsoever ye would that men should do to you, do ye even so to them: for this is the law and the prophets" (Matthew 7:12).

## The Cheek

"Resist not evil: but whosoever shall smite thee on thy right cheek, turn to him the other also" (Matthew 5:39). A right-handed man striking a right cheek delivers a backhand. It is the blow of insult, not of assault, and the Law had already forbidden a man to settle that account himself: "Thou shalt not avenge, nor bear any grudge against the children of thy people" (Leviticus 19:18). Injury cases went before judges (Exodus 21:22), not before the injured party.

"Say not thou, I will recompense evil; but wait on Yahuah, and he shall save thee" (Proverbs 20:22). Refusing to be your own court is obedience, not weakness.

## The Cloke

"And if any man will sue thee at the law, and take away thy coat, let him have thy cloke also" (Matthew 5:40). The cloke is the outer garment, and the Law had already ruled on it: "If thou at all take thy neighbour's raiment to pledge, thou shalt deliver it unto him by that the sun goeth down: for that is his covering... wherein shall he sleep?" (Exodus 22:26-27; repeated at Deuteronomy 24:12-13). A creditor could hold it during the day and had to return it before dusk.

So the man suing for the cloke is demanding what the Law would not let him keep overnight. The disciple hands it over anyway, having broken nothing, and the man across from him is left holding the evidence. That is not a doormat. That is a believer keeping a statute his adversary is breaking.

## The Mile

"And whosoever shall compel thee to go a mile, go with him twain" (Matthew 5:41). The word is angareuo, impressment by an occupying power, used again when a soldier compels Simon of Cyrene to carry the tree (Matthew 27:32). The first mile is extracted by law. The second cannot be. It is the only mile the man owns.

The Law already required as much toward a man with no claim at all: "If thou see the ass of him that hateth thee lying under his burden... thou shalt surely help with him" (Exodus 23:5).

## Give to Him That Asketh

"Give to him that asketh thee, and from him that would borrow of thee turn not thou away" (Matthew 5:42). Moses said it first: "Thou shalt not harden thine heart, nor shut thine hand from thy poor brother: but thou shalt open thine hand wide unto him, and shalt surely lend him sufficient for his need" (Deuteronomy 15:7-8).

## Then He Turns It Around

"Ask, and it shall be given you; seek, and ye shall find; knock, and it shall be opened unto you" (Matthew 7:7). The man on the receiving end of the request in chapter 5 is now the one making it, and the argument runs from lesser to greater: if evil men know how to give good gifts to their children, how much more the Father (Matthew 7:11).

The promise is not novel either: "Ye shall seek me, and find me, when ye shall search for me with all your heart" (Jeremiah 29:13; also Deuteronomy 4:29; Psalm 34:10).

## The Seal

Then comes the word therefore, and it does real work. Because the Father gives to those who ask, the man who is asked gives in turn. Yahushua closes the logic by naming what He has been expounding: "for this is the law and the prophets."

He is not replacing the Law with a maxim. He is summarizing it, and He names which writings are being summarized. The material underneath is what the sermon has been quoting all along: "thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself" (Leviticus 19:18), and the stranger loved on the same terms (Leviticus 19:34).

It also closes the sermon's frame. The law and the prophets are named at the opening (Matthew 5:17) and named again here, and eleven verses later the verdict falls on the men who worked lawlessness.

## Where the Pulpit Buried It

These verses are preached as passivity. Absorb the blow, surrender the coat, ask nothing back, and call it meekness. Read that way, Yahushua spends four verses teaching a believer to be a doormat, not twenty verses after defending the Law down to the stroke of a letter.

The golden rule takes the heavier damage. It is quoted as the entire content of the teaching, a moral minimum standing in place of the commandments: treat people well and nothing else is required. The verse refuses that. It names the law and the prophets as the thing being summarized, and a summary requires something behind it. Strip those out and the sentence has nothing left to summarize.

*One of ten witnesses to a single sermon. This one is the asked — the open hand, on the giving side and the receiving side alike.
