Islam has no official symbol. Its teaching forbids images of Allah and of the prophets, and many Muslim scholars say plainly that the star and crescent is not an Islamic sign at all. Even so, it crowns mosque domes, stands on the flags of Turkey, Pakistan, Algeria, Tunisia, Malaysia, and others, and marks food labeled halal. Over the last two centuries it has become the shape the world reads as Islam.

## How It Became the Symbol

The crescent is older than Islam by many centuries. It appeared with a star on coins of the Greek city of Byzantium before Messiah, and the Persian Sassanid kings used crescent images as well. When the Ottoman Turks took Constantinople in 1453, the crescent stayed with the city. Over the following centuries it became the mark of the Ottoman state, and the red flag bearing a white star and crescent was standardized in the 1840s.

Because the Ottoman sultan claimed to lead the whole Muslim world, the emblem of his empire came to stand for the religion itself. When new Muslim nations drew up flags in the twentieth century, many placed it on theirs.

## The Reasons Muslims Give

Those who accept the symbol point to the place the moon holds in Muslim practice. The Islamic calendar is purely lunar: twelve months of twenty-nine or thirty days, each begun when the thin crescent is sighted after dusk. The fast of Ramadan opens and closes on such a sighting, and the days of the pilgrimage to Mecca are fixed the same way. The Quran says the new moons are measures of time for people and for the pilgrimage (Surah 2:189), and it describes the moon's phases as a sign of the Creator's order (Surah 36:39). One chapter of the Quran is named for the night star (Surah 86).

To a Muslim who accepts the emblem, the crescent speaks of the calendar of worship, the rhythm of prayer and fasting, and the Creator who ordered the heavens. The star is often explained as light and guidance, or simply as the beauty of the night sky over the desert.

## What It Means to Those Who Fly It

For millions, the star and crescent is not a statement of doctrine. It is identity, a mark of belonging to the Muslim world and its history. Others reject it outright as a borrowed Ottoman flag and point instead to the plain black and white banners of early Islam.
