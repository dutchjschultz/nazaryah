The wheel, *dharmachakra* in Sanskrit, the wheel of the Dharma, is Buddhism's oldest and most widely shared symbol. It appears on temple gates and banners across Thailand, Sri Lanka, Tibet, and Japan, and a twenty-four-spoked form sits at the center of India's national flag.

## How It Became the Symbol

Buddhist tradition says that after Siddhartha Gautama reached what Buddhists call enlightenment, he went to the Deer Park at Sarnath, near Varanasi, and gave his first sermon to five former companions. Buddhists call that sermon the first turning of the wheel of the Dharma: the moment his teaching was set in motion in the world.

For several centuries after his death, Buddhist artists did not carve images of the Buddha himself. They showed his footprints, an empty seat, a tree, or a wheel in his place. The Indian emperor Ashoka, who embraced Buddhism in the third century before Messiah, raised stone pillars across his realm. The most famous, at Sarnath, is crowned with lions standing over wheels, and that carving is now the national emblem of India.

## The Reasons Buddhists Give

Each part of the wheel carries a lesson. The eight spokes stand for the Noble Eightfold Path: right view, right intention, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, and right concentration. The hub is commonly explained as moral discipline, the center that holds the whole together, and the rim as the concentration that binds the practice. The circle itself stands for the completeness of the teaching.

A wheel also rolls. To Buddhists it pictures a teaching that moves outward from its first sermon into every land, and a path that carries the one who walks it forward. Buddhism also speaks of another wheel, *samsara*, the endless cycle of birth, death, and rebirth driven by *karma*, the weight of one's deeds. The Dharma wheel is understood as the way off that cycle, toward *nirvana*, the extinguishing of craving and the end of rebirth.

## What It Means to Those Who Honor It

For the Buddhist, the wheel is a promise that suffering has a cause and an end, and that the end can be reached by walking the path. The Thai book *The 38 Blessings* by Yaowares Bunnag follows the same road: thirty-eight steps of right conduct that climb to "Attaining Nirvana" and close with "Allowing bliss to the mind." The companion study, The Dharma Wheel: The Error It Carries, weighs the symbol against the Word.
