## The Setting

The people are home from Babylon with a small temple, a governor, and a high priest, but no king. In chapter 8 Yahuah promised their fasts would become feasts and the nations would come to seek Him (8:19-23). The first burden shows what happens on the way there. It runs forward in time, in four stops.

## Why a Message About the King, and Why Now

No one was attacking them. Persia ruled, and the Persian king Darius was paying for the temple out of his own treasury (Ezra 6:8). The trouble was different. They had no king. The last king of David’s line had been carried to Babylon, and his grandson Zerubbabel was only a governor under Persia (Haggai 1:1). It looked like David’s throne was finished.

Some may have hoped Zerubbabel would be the king, since Yahuah had called him His “signet” (Haggai 2:23). The burden answers them. The King is coming, but not yet, and not as a governor or a conqueror. Empires will rise and fall first, and an evil Greek king will come against them. Then their King will come, gentle, on a donkey. And they are warned that when He comes, many of them will not want Him.

So the answer to their fear is that David’s line is not finished. The King is still coming out of Judah (10:4), and He comes through the very man they were watching. Matthew lists Zerubbabel in the line of Yahushua (Matthew 1:12-13). The governor was not the King, but the King was coming from him.

## 1. Alexander Comes Down the Coast (9:1-8)

Zechariah names a string of cities: Damascus, Hamath, Tyre, Sidon, Ashkelon, Gaza, Ekron, Ashdod. Each one falls.

About 190 years later, Alexander the Great took that exact road. Damascus fell, Sidon gave up, Tyre was burned in 332 BC after a seven-month siege, and Gaza fell after two months. Jerusalem sat right in his path and was left standing, as Yahuah said: “I will encamp about mine house because of the army” (9:8).

## 2. The War With Greece (9:13)

“I have … raised up thy sons, O Zion, against thy sons, O Greece.”

After Alexander died, Greek kings ruled Judah. In the 160s BC one of them, Antiochus IV, set an idol in the temple and outlawed the Sabbath and the Law. The Maccabees led the people in revolt, drove the Greeks out, and cleansed the temple.

## 3. The King on the Donkey (9:9-10; chapter 10)

“Behold, thy King cometh unto thee … lowly, and riding upon an ass.”

This is Messiah, and Zechariah set Him right after Alexander on purpose. One king comes on a warhorse with an army. The other comes gentle on a donkey and “shall speak peace unto the heathen” (9:10). Yahushua rode into Jerusalem on that colt (Matthew 21:4-5).

His first act is a rescue: “by the blood of thy covenant I have sent forth thy prisoners out of the pit wherein is no water” (9:11). They are called “prisoners of hope” (9:12), still held, but held by a promise. The way out is covenant blood, and Yahushua named it at His last Passover: “this is my blood of the new testament, which is shed for many” (Matthew 26:28).

Chapter 10 says why He comes. The people are scattered “because there was no shepherd” (10:2), and Yahuah will gather both houses, Judah and Joseph (10:6). Yahushua saw the crowds “as sheep having no shepherd” (Matthew 9:36).

## 4. The Shepherd Sold (chapter 11)

Zechariah acts out the shepherd, and the people pay him “thirty pieces of silver” (11:12), the Law’s price for a dead slave (Exodus 21:32). He throws it “to the potter in the house of Yahuah” (11:13) and breaks his two staffs, Favor and Brotherhood.

At the cross every piece came true. Judas sold Yahushua for thirty pieces of silver, threw it down in the temple, and the priests bought a potter’s field (Matthew 27:3-10). Yahuah had said He would hand the people “into the hand of his king” (11:6), and the chief priests cried, “We have no king but Caesar” (John 19:15).

The chapter opens with fire on the cedars of Lebanon (11:1), the wood the temple was built from. In AD 70 Rome burned the temple. And the chapter closes with the shepherd they got instead, one who “shall eat the flesh of the fat” (11:16).

## Why Tell Them Now?

A promise five hundred years away does not get anyone out of bed. But the burden was not a bedtime story. Zechariah’s book opens with the real message: “Turn ye unto me, saith Yahuah of hosts, and I will turn unto you … Be ye not as your fathers” (1:3-4).

The burden shows them where their own road leads. The shepherds of chapter 11 sell the flock and say, “Blessed be Yahuah; for I am rich” (11:5). Within a couple of generations, that was the leaders of Jerusalem: nobles charging their own brothers interest (Nehemiah 5:7), and priests putting blind and lame animals on the altar (Malachi 1:8). The heart that sells the flock is the same heart that would one day sell the King for thirty pieces of silver.

So the burden puts a choice in front of them. Be the great men weighing out the silver, or be “the poor of the flock that waited upon me,” who “knew that it was the word of Yahuah” (11:11). Their children would one day stand in front of the King, and what kind of people they would be depended on what their fathers became.

## What It Means

For them, it was a map: hard times from the Greeks, Yahuah guarding His house, and a King coming, gentle, not on a warhorse, whom many would sell cheap.

For us, it shows Messiah was no surprise. Five hundred years ahead, Yahuah named the donkey, the scattered sheep, the thirty pieces of silver, and the potter. And the ones who saw it were not the great men: “the poor of the flock … knew that it was the word of Yahuah” (11:11).

The second burden, chapters 12 to 14, picks up from here: they look on the One they pierced, a fountain is opened for sin, and the nations come to keep the feast.

## Where It Lands

The King who came on a donkey returns on a white horse, “Faithful and True” (Revelation 19:11). The Shepherd sold for thirty pieces of silver is the Lamb who “shall feed them” (Revelation 7:17).
