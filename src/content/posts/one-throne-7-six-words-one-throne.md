## Part One — Pull One Thread

Up to now this book has handed you one word at a time. That was on purpose. You cannot clean an artifact while it is still buried next to five others. But the words were never separate, and now they all come back to the table together — because the most important thing about these six is the one thing no single chapter could show you. They hold each other up. Misread one and you have not made one mistake. You have started a slide in the other five.

Take the heaviest one first. Sin means to miss a mark, and everything about the word depends on the mark being there. Now watch what happens the moment the mark is removed — which is exactly what the modern church did when it set the Torah aside.

Forgiveness goes first. Forgiveness is a debt lifted and carried off. But a debt has to be measured before it can be lifted. Take away the standard and there is no debt to name, so forgiveness stops being a costly transaction and shrinks into a mood — "just let it go." Nobody set out to lose the word forgiveness. They lost it by losing sin.

Prayer goes next. Prayer at full weight is a man standing in the gap before the Judge, pressing a case over a real charge. Take away the charge and there is no gap to stand in. Prayer drifts into pleasant talk with no Judge and nothing at stake. Nobody set out to lose the word prayer. They lost it by losing sin.

Blessing follows close behind. The blessing of Deuteronomy 28 hangs by a chain, and the chain is the standard — "if thou shalt hearken unto the voice of Yahuah." Cut the standard and the blessing comes loose and floats down onto whoever happens to feel lucky. Nobody set out to lose the word blessing. They lost it by losing sin.

One word fell, and three more bent. That is what a system does. You did not pull six threads; you pulled one, and the cloth puckered everywhere.

Then there is a second kind of dangerous word — not the one that knocks the others down when it falls, but the one that hides the fact that anything fell at all. That word is kosmos. Kosmos is the rival kingdom, the system at war with the throne. As long as you can see it, you can see that your words are being raided. Lose it — shrink it down to "the planet" or "bad habits" — and you can no longer see the kingdom that has been quietly draining your vocabulary. You sit inside a church using six hollow words and feel perfectly safe, because the one word that would have warned you has been the first one disarmed.

So this is not the chapter where we line the six words up again to admire them. This is the chapter where we put them all in the same room and watch them lean on each other — and watch what tips over when one is pulled out from under the rest.

## Part Two — The Nature of Lost

Before we go further, we have to be honest about a word this whole book has leaned on without ever defining it: lost. What does it actually mean for a word — or a truth — to be lost?

We tend to hear "lost" as "gone." Erased. No longer here. But that is almost never what the Scriptures mean by it.

**אבד**

***avad***

*to wander off, to be lost — not to cease to exist*

Think about the three lost things Yahushua set side by side: a sheep, a coin, a son. The lost sheep did not stop being a sheep. The lost coin did not stop being silver — it was lying in the house the entire time. The lost son was still alive, still a son, even with the pigs. In not one of the three did "lost" mean destroyed. It meant present but disconnected — there, but out of place, out of reach, out of use. The coin still had the king's face stamped on it. It just was not in the purse where it could buy anything.

That is exactly what happened to these six words. They were never deleted from the Bible. Open any page and they are right there, the King's face still stamped on them. They are lost the way the coin was lost — present but out of use, in the house but not in the hand.

And here is the part that should make every reader nervous. The coin was not stolen. Nobody broke in. It rolled into a crack and the dust settled over it, one quiet layer at a time. That is how truth is usually lost — not by a robbery you would notice, but by a drift you never feel.

> **Hebrews 2:1**
>
> *Therefore we ought to give the more earnest heed to the things which we have heard, lest at any time we should let them slip.*

The word translated "slip" is a sailing word — a boat that was tied to the dock slowly working loose while everyone aboard is asleep. Nobody cut the rope. The current just did its patient work. Or hear it the way the old proverb said it: "Remove not the ancient landmark, which thy fathers have set." You do not steal a man's field in a day. You move the boundary stone a few inches every season, and in a generation his fence is in your pasture and he never noticed the night it moved.

That is the whole story of these six words. Nobody held a council and voted to drain them. The Hebrew was carried into Greek, the Greek into Latin, the Latin into English, and at every border a little sediment settled — a shade of meaning left behind, a courtroom flattened into a feeling. Then the pulpit preached the drifted version with such confidence, for so many generations, that the slide began to look like solid ground. The landmark had been moving for centuries, an inch at a time, and the people standing on the wrong side of it were certain they had always lived there.

So that is what "lost" means in this book. Not gone. Adrift. Still stamped with the King's face, still lying somewhere in the house, waiting for someone to light a candle, sweep, and seek until it is found.

## Part Three — In the Same Arena

The fastest way to prove these words are one system is to catch them working together in a single passage. When two or three of them stand in the same few verses, you can watch them hand off to each other — and you can watch exactly what would break if you misread even one.

### 3.1 — Isaiah 53: A Tripod That Cannot Lose a Leg

> **Isaiah 53:6, 12**
>
> *All we like sheep have gone astray; we have turned every one to his own way; and Yahuah hath laid on him the iniquity of us all ... and he bare the sin of many, and made intercession for the transgressors.*

Three of our words run through one Person here, and they are locked so tightly you cannot move one without moving the others. "Yahuah hath laid on him" — the word is *paga*, the prayer word, the collision word, the same root as intercession. "The iniquity of us all" — *awon*, the heavy sin word. "He bare the sin of many" — *nasa*, the forgiveness word, the lifting and the carrying away.

Now watch them work. The sin had to exist and be measured before it could be laid on anyone, so sin's standard is doing silent work in the background of the whole verse. The laying-on is *paga*, a collision — the charge slams into the Servant. And the carrying-off is *nasa* — he lifts the measured debt and walks away with it. Prayer, sin, and forgiveness are not three topics in this verse. They are three moves in one motion.

And here is the test. Pull one thread. Misread sin — say there was no real mark, no measured debt — and there is nothing to lay on him and nothing to carry; the whole verse goes slack and the cross becomes a sad accident instead of a transaction. Misread the prayer word — treat it as gentle pleading instead of collision — and you miss that he stepped into the path of the blow on purpose. Each word props up the other two. Isaiah built a tripod. Kick one leg and the whole thing goes over.

### 3.2 — Psalm 51: Four Words in One Breath

> **Psalm 51:1–4, 15**
>
> *... blot out my transgressions. Wash me throughly from mine iniquity, and cleanse me from my sin ... Against thee, thee only, have I sinned, and done this evil in thy sight ... O Master, open thou my lips; and my mouth shall shew forth thy praise.*

David hands us four of the words in one prayer — and the whole psalm is itself the fifth, because a psalm like this is *palal*, a man throwing himself into the gap before the Judge over his own charge.

He asks Yahuah to "blot out" his transgressions — *machah*, one of the forgiveness words, to wipe a record clean. He names his sin plainly. And then he says the line that holds the entire system together: "Against thee, thee only, have I sinned." Watch what that line does. It nails the standard to a Person. Sin is not missing some floating, abstract mark; it is missing Yahuah. The measure and the throne turn out to be the same direction. David is not weighing himself against a rulebook. He is weighing himself against the One on the seat.

Then look how the psalm ends: "open thou my lips; and my mouth shall shew forth thy praise." That is *barak* — blessing, praise sent up. And notice the order. Forgiveness clears the charge first; only then do the lips open to send praise up. You cannot bless from under an unanswered charge. The blessing waits on the forgiveness, which waited on the sin to be named, which was measured against Yahuah Himself. Four words, one chain, every link pulling in the same direction.

### 3.3 — John 17: The Last Two Walk In

> **John 17:1, 3**
>
> *Father, the hour is come; glorify thy Son, that thy Son also may glorify thee ... And this is life eternal, that they might know thee the only true Elohim, and Yahushua Messiah, whom thou hast sent.*

One more, quickly, to bring the last two words into the room. In John 17 Yahushua prays — palal again — and in the same breath uses glory and kosmos. He asks the Father to glorify him so that he may glorify the Father: glory handed upward, never grasped. A few verses on he says he is not praying for the kosmos but for those the Father has pulled out of it — the rival kingdom named as the very thing he is rescuing people from. And he defines eternal life as knowing "the only true Elohim" and the One He sent. Prayer, glory, and kosmos in a single prayer, and all three bend toward the same Person. The Son aims the glory up, names the rival kingdom down, and points the whole prayer at the throne. Six words now, across three passages, and not one of them ever points back at the speaker.

## Part Four — Every Arrow Points Up

By now the pattern is impossible to miss. Take any one of these words, brush the sediment off, set it back on its feet — and it does not just stand. It turns, and it points at the seat. Every single time. Restore prayer and it points to a Mediator standing before the throne. Restore blessing and it is praise climbing toward the One on it. Restore glory and it is the weight of His character filling the room. Restore sin and it is a charge measured against His standard. Restore forgiveness and it is a pardon issued by His authority. Restore the kosmos and it is the rival throne, finally visible. There is no neutral direction anywhere in this vocabulary. Every word is an arrow, and every arrow points up.

Which tells you why these six, and not some other six. The enemy is not careless, and he is not random. He attacks the words and the doctrines that decide one thing — who gets worshiped — and he always has.

Look at his very first move. He did not walk into the garden and announce, "There is no God." He asked a question about a word: "Yea, hath Elohim said?" He did not deny the Almighty. He edited a sentence. That is the whole strategy in a single verse — never a frontal assault on the throne, always a quiet edit of the words that point to it. It is the same trick at the golden calf, where the people did not invent a foreign god; Aaron called the calf "a feast to Yahuah." They kept the right name and changed the object. And it is the same trick Paul names as the root of all of it: men "worshipped and served the creature more than the Creator." The word under "served" there is latreuo — the lifelong service that belongs to Yahuah alone. The fall is always a service redirected, a worship pointed at the wrong seat.

Scripture guards that seat with a line it never crosses. There is the bow — proskyneo, a single act of homage you may rightly offer a king or a messenger — and there is the service, latreuo, a whole life of devotion. In all twenty-one places latreuo appears in the New Testament, it is directed to the Father. Never once to anyone else. The bow may travel; the service has one address.

So when every knee bows to the Son, watch where the motion lands:

> **Philippians 2:10–11**
>
> *That at the name of Yahushua every knee should bow ... and that every tongue should confess that Yahushua Messiah is Master, to the glory of Elohim the Father.*

The bow goes through the Son. The glory goes to the Father. The lifelong service stays with Yahuah. The Son is the Mediator who stands before the throne — "one Elohim, and one mediator between Elohim and men" — never the One on it. Move him into the Father's seat, hand him the latreuo that belongs to Yahuah alone, and you have done the calf's trick one more time: the right name, the wrong object. The attack on the words and the attack on the throne were never two different attacks. They were always the same one.

This is why these six words were worth a whole book. Not for scholarship. For worship. You cannot worship in truth with words that have been hollowed out, because the words are the instrument — they are how the worship finds its target. Drain them, and you get a church that uses every right word and bows to a fog: sincere, busy, religious, and aimed at nothing in particular. Restore them, and the worship finds its address again. The arrows line back up. The prayer finds its Mediator, the blessing finds its direction, the glory finds its weight, the charge finds its standard, the pardon finds its authority, and the rival kingdom finds the daylight that exposes it.

Six words, lost the way a coin is lost — in the house the whole time. Found, they all do the one thing they were built to do. They point home.
