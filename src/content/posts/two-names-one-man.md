## The Claim

Pulpits across modern Christianity teach that Saul of Tarsus received a new name when he was saved. The sermon is familiar: "From Saul to Paul." Saul the persecutor dies on the Damascus road, and Paul the apostle rises in his place, his new name the badge of his new life. Some teachers go further and say he chose the name Paulus, meaning "small," as an act of humility. Neither idea comes from Luke. Both trace back to the Latin church fathers Jerome and Augustine, more than three centuries after Acts was written.

None of this is in the text. Scripture never records a renaming, never assigns the name Paul a spiritual meaning, and never places the change at the Damascus road. What the text does record is a man who carried two names from birth, one Hebrew and one Roman, and a writer who used whichever one fit the setting. Saul is not the only one. The same pattern runs through Barnabas, Simeon Niger, Thomas, Peter, and Tabitha, and it exposes something larger than one sermon illustration: the Apostolic writings are Hebrew names and Hebrew speech carried across to Greek-speaking readers, and the confusion begins when readers mistake the gloss for the name.

## What a Renaming Looks Like in Scripture

When Yahuah changes a man's name, Scripture says so in plain words, gives the new name, and usually gives the reason.

> **GENESIS 17:5 — KJV**
>
> *Neither shall thy name any more be called Abram, but thy name shall be Abraham; for a father of many nations have I made thee.*

> **GENESIS 32:28 — KJV**
>
> *Thy name shall be called no more Jacob, but Israel: for as a prince hast thou power with Elohim and with men, and hast prevailed.*

Sarai becomes Sarah in the same way (Genesis 17:15). Even in the Apostolic writings the pattern holds. When Yahushua gives Simon a new title, the text states it outright and translates it:

> **JOHN 1:42 — KJV**
>
> *Thou art Simon the son of Jona: thou shalt be called Cephas, which is by interpretation, A stone.*

A renaming has a speaker, a declaration, and a new name. Acts 9 has none of the three. The account of Saul's calling runs through the vision, the blindness, the visit of Ananias, the immersion, and the preaching in Damascus, and not one verse speaks of a new name.

## Saul After the Road

If the Damascus road had renamed him, the narrative would call him Paul from that point forward. It does the opposite. Ananias addresses him as Saul at the very moment his sight returns:

> **ACTS 9:17 — KJV**
>
> *And Ananias went his way, and entered into the house; and putting his hands on him said, Brother Saul, the Master, even Yahushua, that appeared unto thee in the way as thou camest, hath sent me.*

From there Luke goes on calling him Saul through chapter after chapter of his life as a believer:

- He preaches in Damascus as Saul (Acts 9:22, 24).
- He comes to Jerusalem as Saul (Acts 9:26).
- Barnabas goes to Tarsus to seek Saul (Acts 11:25).
- The famine relief is sent "by the hands of Barnabas and Saul" (Acts 11:30; 12:25).
- He is listed among the prophets and teachers at Antioch as Saul (Acts 13:1).
- The command to set the two men apart for the work names "Barnabas and Saul" (Acts 13:2).
- The Roman proconsul at Paphos calls for "Barnabas and Saul" (Acts 13:7).

By the most common reckoning, more than a decade of believing service passes under the name Saul. Then, at Paphos, the text says:

> **ACTS 13:9 — KJV**
>
> *Then Saul, (who also is called Paul,) ... set his eyes on him.*

The Greek reads *Saulos de, ho kai Paulos*: "Saul, the one also Paul." The phrase *ho kai* is the ordinary formula for a man known by a second name. It does not say "who was now named," or "who was renamed," or "whom Yahuah called." It says *also*. Both names were his already. Luke is simply telling his reader that the man he has been calling Saul is the same man the Roman world knows as Paul.

## The Voice From Heaven Spoke Hebrew

The strongest witness against the renaming tradition comes from the mouth of Yahushua Himself. The Greek text of Acts preserves two different forms of the name Saul:

- **Saulos** (Strong's G4569), the name given a Greek ending, used in Luke's own narration.
- **Saoul** (Strong's G4549), the bare Hebrew name *Sha'ul* (H7586, "asked"), left without a Greek ending, used for this man only when a speaker addresses him directly in Hebrew.

Every time the Hebrew form appears for this man, someone is speaking to him in his own tongue: the voice on the road (Acts 9:4; 22:7; 26:14) and Ananias (Acts 9:17; 22:13). Paul himself, telling the story years later, makes the point explicit:

> **ACTS 26:14 — KJV**
>
> *And when we were all fallen to the earth, I heard a voice speaking unto me, and saying in the Hebrew tongue, Saul, Saul, why persecutest thou me?*

This is Paul's own testimony, given in Acts 26, long after the switch at Acts 13:9. He does not report that the Master renamed him. He reports that the Master called him Saul, in Hebrew. In Acts 22 he tells the same story to a Jerusalem crowd while speaking "in the Hebrew tongue" (Acts 21:40; 22:2), and in his own retelling he is still Saul from beginning to end. The risen Messiah never once addresses him as Paul anywhere in Scripture.

The detail matters beyond Paul. Luke's Greek preserves the Hebrew sound of the name precisely where Hebrew was spoken. The Greek text is carrying Hebrew speech, and it shows its seams.

## Why the Switch Happens at Paphos

The change in Luke's usage happens at a specific place and for a visible reason. At Paphos the two men stand before Sergius Paulus, the Roman proconsul of Cyprus (Acts 13:7). From that chapter forward the mission turns outward to the cities of the Roman world, and Luke follows suit, using the name that world knew.

One correction belongs here. *Paulos* (G3972) is not a Greek name. It is the Latin *Paulus*, a Roman family name, written in Greek letters. Acts 22:28 shows Paul was born a Roman citizen, and a Roman citizen of Jewish birth would commonly carry a Latin name alongside his Hebrew one. Saul of the tribe of Benjamin (Philippians 3:5; Romans 11:1), named after the first king of Israel, a Benjamite (1 Samuel 9:1-2), carried Paulus for his dealings with Rome. One name was for his people; the other was for the empire.

This also explains why every one of his letters opens with *Paulos*. They were written to assemblies in Rome, Corinth, Galatia, Ephesus, Philippi, Colossae, and Thessalonica, in the Roman world, so he signed with the name that world knew him by. A man writing to Gentile cities under his Roman name has not shed his Hebrew one. In the same letters he calls himself "an Hebrew of the Hebrews."

## Where the Renaming Came From

Since Scripture never supplies a renaming story, it had to come from somewhere else. It entered through the Latin church fathers of the late fourth and early fifth centuries, the same writers the Roman Catholic Church later honored as Doctors of the Church.

Jerome, the translator of the Latin Vulgate, proposed in his commentary on Philemon that Saul took the name Paulus as a trophy of his first notable convert, the proconsul Sergius Paulus, in the manner of a Roman general who added the name of a conquered people to his own. He repeated the idea in *De viris illustribus* 5. Yet in that same commentary Jerome admitted that no Scripture records why Saul was called Paul. He offered the theory as his own guess.

Augustine carried the guess further, and in two directions. In *Confessions* 8.4 he adopted the trophy idea, writing that Paul chose to be called Paulus as a mark of a great victory. Elsewhere (*On the Spirit and the Letter* 7.12; Sermons 101 and 168) he taught that Paul took the name because *paulus* means "little," marking himself as "the least of the apostles" (1 Corinthians 15:9). These are two incompatible explanations from one man, and both assume a name change the text never records.

Not every voice in that era agreed. Rufinus, who translated Origen's commentary on Romans into Latin, remarked that the trophy theory has no precedent in the divine Scriptures. His objection was set aside, and the theory lived on.

The line of descent is plain. The modern pulpit's "From Saul to Paul" and its "Paul means small" both trace back to the guesses of Jerome and Augustine, carried forward with the full authority of the Latin church behind them. The fault is not in Luke's text, which plainly says *also*. It lies in the tradition Rome built over that text and handed down as though Scripture had said it.

## The Same Pattern Across the Assembly

Saul is not an exception. The Apostolic writings repeatedly name a man by his Hebrew name and then hand the reader a second name in Greek, Latin, or Aramaic. Each one is the same pattern: one man, two labels, and a writer bridging two languages.

### Joses, Called Barnabas

> **ACTS 4:36 — KJV**
>
> *And Joses, who by the apostles was surnamed Barnabas, (which is, being interpreted, The son of consolation,) a Levite, and of the country of Cyprus.*

His given name is *Yoseph* (H3130), Joseph. The King James follows the Greek form *Ioses* (G2500), a shortened, Greek-shaped rendering of the same Hebrew name; other manuscripts read *Ioseph* outright. On top of that given name the apostles placed a surname, *Barnabas* (G921), from the Aramaic *bar*, "son," and a word tied to prophecy (Strong's gives "son of prophecy").

Luke then translates it for his readers as "son of *paraklesis*" (G3874), consolation or exhortation. The two meanings are not in conflict. Paul defines the work of prophecy in exactly these terms:

> **1 CORINTHIANS 14:3 — KJV**
>
> *But he that prophesieth speaketh unto men to edification, and exhortation, and comfort.*

"Exhortation" there is the same word, *paraklesis*. A son of prophecy is a son of exhortation. And Acts 13:1 lists Barnabas first among the "prophets and teachers" at Antioch. Three languages stand in one verse: a Hebrew given name in Greek form, an Aramaic surname, and a Greek translation of that surname. Nothing was changed. Everything was carried across.

### Simeon, Called Niger

> **ACTS 13:1 — KJV**
>
> *Now there were in the church that was at Antioch certain prophets and teachers; as Barnabas, and Simeon that was called Niger, and Lucius of Cyrene, and Manaen, which had been brought up with Herod the tetrarch, and Saul.*

*Symeon* (G4826) is the Hebrew *Shim'on* (H8095), "hearing," the name of Jacob's second son. *Niger* (G3526) is Latin for "black," a Roman descriptive name. Again one man carries a Hebrew name and a Latin one, and the text sets them side by side without suggesting either replaced the other.

The same verse holds the whole pattern in miniature. Barnabas carries an Aramaic surname. Simeon carries a Latin one. Lucius bears a Latin name. Manaen is the Hebrew *Menachem*, "comforter," in Greek form. And Saul stands at the end of the list, still called Saul, eight verses before Luke mentions that he is also Paul. Five teachers in one assembly, and every name is a crossing between languages.

### Thomas, Called Didymus

> **JOHN 21:2 — KJV**
>
> *There were together Simon Peter, and Thomas called Didymus, and Nathanael of Cana in Galilee, and the sons of Zebedee, and two other of his disciples.*

*Thomas* (G2381) is not a Greek name at all. It is the Hebrew *ta'om* (H8380), "twin," the word used of Esau and Jacob in Rebekah's womb (Genesis 25:24), carried in its Aramaic form. *Didymos* (G1324) is the Greek word for "twin." John writes "Thomas called Didymus" three times (John 11:16; 20:24; 21:2). He is giving the same word twice, once in the disciple's own tongue and once in the reader's.

This example shows the mechanism clearly. Thomas and Didymus are not two names in the sense of a Hebrew name and a Roman name. They are one word translated. The writer is doing exactly what a translator does when the original term is worth keeping but the reader will not understand it: he keeps it and glosses it. And the church kept the untranslated Hebrew word as a proper name for two thousand years without knowing it was a word.

## The Interpreted Names

The Apostolic writings carry a recurring formula: "which is, being interpreted," "which is by interpretation." Each time, the writer stops to translate a Hebrew or Aramaic word for a reader who could not understand it. The formula appears across the gospels and Acts:

- **Cephas**, "which is by interpretation, A stone" (John 1:42). *Kephas* (G2786) is Aramaic; *Petros* (G4074) is its Greek translation. Yet Paul calls him Cephas in his letters (1 Corinthians 1:12; 15:5; Galatians 2:9), and Yahushua still addresses him as "Simon, son of Jonas" at the end (John 21:15-17).
- **Tabitha**, "which by interpretation is called Dorcas" (Acts 9:36). Aramaic *Tabitha* (G5000) and Greek *Dorkas* (G1393) both mean "gazelle."
- **John, whose surname was Mark** (Acts 12:12). The Hebrew *Yochanan* paired with the Latin *Marcus*.
- **Emmanuel**, "which being interpreted is, El with us" (Matthew 1:23).
- **Golgotha**, "which is, being interpreted, The place of a skull" (Mark 15:22).
- **Rabbi**, "which is to say, being interpreted, Master" (John 1:38).

And the most consequential of them all:

> **JOHN 1:41 — KJV**
>
> *We have found the Messias, which is, being interpreted, the Christ.*

*Messias* (G3323) is the Hebrew *Mashiach* (H4899), "anointed." *Christos* (G5547) is the Greek word chosen to translate it. John himself shows that "Christ" is not a name but a translation of a Hebrew title. The disciples did not say they had found the Christ. They said they had found the Mashiach, and John rendered it for Greek readers. The church then built its entire vocabulary on the translation and forgot the original.

## What the Pattern Proves

Taken together, the double names establish four things.

**First, no one in the Apostolic writings is renamed by translation.** Every second name is a gloss, a surname, or a civic name for the Roman world. The Hebrew name remains, and the people closest to the man, including Yahushua Himself, keep using it.

**Second, the writers were carrying Hebrew across to Greek readers.** The "being interpreted" formula only makes sense if the original speech, the original names, and the original titles were Hebrew. A Greek-thinking author writing for Greeks has nothing to interpret. These texts constantly stop to translate because Hebrew is underneath them.

**Third, the Greek text shows its seams.** Luke's use of *Saoul* in Hebrew speech and *Saulos* in his own narration is not careless. It preserves the sound of what was actually said. The same text that later tradition treats as originally Greek keeps leaving Hebrew fingerprints wherever someone speaks.

**Fourth, the confusion is the reader's, not the writer's.** Luke never says Saul was renamed. John never says Thomas meant anything other than twin. Beginning with Jerome and Augustine, tradition read the gloss as the name, then read the name as a spiritual event, then preached the event as doctrine. "From Saul to Paul" is a sermon built on a parenthesis.

This is the same error that runs through so much of modern Christian teaching. The Greek word is read as if it were the source, the Latin name is treated as if it were the man, and the Hebrew underneath is lost. Paul's name was never changed. The reader's language was.

## Ready Answer

Acts 13:9 says Saul was "*also* called Paul," not renamed; Luke calls him Saul for a decade after his conversion, and the risen Yahushua calls him Saul, in Hebrew, in Paul's own retelling in Acts 26:14.

## The Name That Is Coming

Scripture does promise a renaming, but it is not on the Damascus road, and it does not come by a translator's hand. It waits at the end, and it is given by Yahushua to those who overcome:

> **REVELATION 2:17 — KJV**
>
> *To him that overcometh will I give to eat of the hidden manna, and will give him a white stone, and in the stone a new name written, which no man knoweth saving he that receiveth it.*

> **REVELATION 3:12 — KJV**
>
> *Him that overcometh will I make a pillar in the temple of my Elohim ... and I will write upon him the name of my Elohim, and the name of the city of my Elohim, which is new Jerusalem ... and I will write upon him my new name.*

That is the only new name the believer is promised, and it will need no interpreter. It will be written by the Son, bearing the name of His Elohim, Yahuah, on those who have kept His commandments and the testimony of Yahushua to the end.
