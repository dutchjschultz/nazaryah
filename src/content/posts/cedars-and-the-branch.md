## The Pattern

Scripture draws the kingdoms of men as trees. Twice the prophets paint a great empire as a towering tree with birds in its boughs, beasts in its shade, and nations living under its branches. Twice Yahuah sends the axe. Between those two fallen trees stands a third tree, small at its planting, set in the ground by Yahuah Himself. That third tree is the Branch, and the whole lesson turns on the difference between the trees men grow and the tree Yahuah plants.

## Pharaoh: The Cedar of Ezekiel 31

Ezekiel was sent to ask Pharaoh a question: *"Whom art thou like in thy greatness?"* (Ezekiel 31:2). The answer was a mirror. The Assyrian had been *"a cedar in Lebanon"* (31:3), the cedar (erez, H730) being the tallest and proudest tree the Hebrew world knew. Every fowl nested in its boughs, every beast of the field brought forth young under its branches, and *"under his shadow dwelt all great nations"* (31:6).

The trees of Eden, the garden of Elohim, could not hide it and envied it (31:8-9). Those trees are not angels or hidden powers of the garden. The chapter itself identifies them as the other kingdoms and their rulers, the great ones who go down into the pit alongside the fallen cedar and are *"comforted in the nether parts of the earth"* (31:16-18). Eden is the stage; the trees are nations.

The charge against the cedar is one sentence: *"his heart is lifted up in his height"* (31:10). So strangers cut it down, and its branches lay broken in every valley (31:12). The chapter closes by naming the tree outright: *"This is Pharaoh and all his multitude"* (31:18).

The New Testament carries the same verdict in plain words: Yahuah resists the proud and gives favor to the humble (James 4:6; 1 Peter 5:5).

## Nebuchadnezzar: The Tree of Daniel 4

Decades later the king of Babylon dreamed the same tree. It stood in the midst of the earth, its height reached heaven, the beasts sheltered under it and the fowls dwelt in its boughs, and *"all flesh was fed of it"* (Daniel 4:10-12). A watcher came down and ordered it hewn (4:13-14). Daniel gave the interpretation without softening it: *"It is thou, O king"* (4:22).

Nebuchadnezzar was never turned into a tree. He was the tree in the dream, and the cutting down was fulfilled when he was driven from men to live with the beasts of the field for seven times (4:25, 33). The trigger was the same as Pharaoh’s. Walking on his palace he boasted, *"Is not this great Babylon, that I have built"* (4:30), and while the words were still in his mouth the kingdom departed.

The purpose of the whole vision is stated in Daniel 4:17: the Most High rules in the kingdom of men, gives it to whom He will, and sets over it *"the basest of men."* Yahuah lifts the low and brings down the high. The New Testament repeats it in the mouth of Miriam: He has put down the mighty from their seats and exalted them of low degree (Luke 1:52).

## Two Witnesses, One Pattern

Pharaoh and Nebuchadnezzar stand as two witnesses to a single pattern. The marks are the same in both: great height, a shade that covers the nations, birds and beasts depending on the tree, a heart lifted up, and the axe. Egypt and Babylon are the two great houses of bondage in Israel’s story, and both are drawn as trees that grew too tall for heaven to tolerate. Any nation that sets itself up as the shade under which the world must live stands where Pharaoh and Babylon stood.

Yohanan the Immerser announced the same axe over Israel: *"the axe is laid unto the root of the trees"* (Matthew 3:10). Every tree that does not bring forth good fruit is hewn down, whether it is Egypt, Babylon, or the house of Israel itself.

The pattern did not close with Egypt and Babylon. America speaks of itself today in the same voice as the cedar: the greatest nation on earth, the shade under which the world finds its security, its wealth, and its freedom. Its present government proclaims a new golden age and credits its own hand for all that the nation provides to everyone. That is Nebuchadnezzar on the palace roof, *"Is not this great Babylon, that I have built"* (Daniel 4:30). Scripture never measures a nation by its height; it measures whether the heart is *"lifted up in his height"* (Ezekiel 31:10). Yahushua gave the same verdict in one line: whoever exalts himself shall be abased (Luke 14:11). The cedar of Lebanon and the tree of Babylon stand as warnings to any land that boasts in its own branches.

## People Are Trees

The picture is not only for empires. The man who delights in the Law of Yahuah is *"like a tree planted by the rivers of water"* that brings forth fruit in season and whose leaf does not wither (Psalm 1:3; Jeremiah 17:8). The wicked man is a tree as well. David saw him in great power, *"spreading himself like a green bay tree,"* then passed by and he was gone (Psalm 37:35-36).

The New Testament keeps both halves. Yahushua is the vine and His disciples are the branches that bear fruit by abiding in Him (John 15:5), while false teachers are trees whose fruit withers, *"twice dead, plucked up by the roots"* (Jude 12). The difference between the righteous tree and the wicked one is not size; it is where the roots are planted and who planted them.

## The Answer: The Tender Twig

Ezekiel holds the key a few chapters before the cedar of Pharaoh. In Ezekiel 17:22-24 Yahuah takes a tender twig (rak, H7390) from the top of the high cedar and plants it on the high mountain of Israel. It brings forth boughs, bears fruit, and becomes *"a goodly cedar,"* and under it dwell *"all fowl of every wing."* Then all the trees of the field learn the lesson of the whole study: Yahuah has brought down the high tree and exalted the low tree, dried up the green tree and made the dry tree flourish (17:24).

Every feature of Pharaoh’s cedar and Nebuchadnezzar’s tree reappears here, but turned right side up. The birds that sheltered under Egypt and Babylon now find their nest in the tree Yahuah planted. The prophets name that twig. He is the rod out of the stem of Jesse and the Branch (netser, H5342) growing out of his roots (Isaiah 11:1), the righteous Branch (tsemach, H6780) raised to David (Jeremiah 23:5), and the man whose name is The Branch (Zechariah 6:12). Matthew draws on the same word when he writes that Yahushua would be called a Nazarene (Matthew 2:23).

Isaiah describes how small He looked at the planting: *"as a tender plant, and as a root out of a dry ground"* (Isaiah 53:2). That is the low tree and the dry tree of Ezekiel 17:24. Yahushua told the same story in His own parable. The mustard seed is the least of all seeds, yet it grows into a tree, *"so that the birds of the air come and lodge in the branches thereof"* (Matthew 13:31-32). That is Ezekiel’s language and Daniel’s language, deliberately reclaimed for the Kingdom of Yahuah.

One detail answers the Trinitarian reading directly. In Ezekiel 17:22-23 there are two parties: Yahuah who takes and plants, and the twig that is planted. The Branch does not plant Himself. Yahuah raises Him up (Jeremiah 23:5), and the Branch grows where the Father set Him.

## The Stump That Remained

In Daniel 4:15 and 4:26 the tree is hewn, but the stump of its roots is left in the earth, banded with iron and brass, and the kingdom returns once the king learns that the heavens rule. Yahuah’s judgment cut the tree, yet He kept a root alive. Isaiah uses the same picture for Israel, a holy seed left in the stump after the felling (Isaiah 6:13), and the Branch itself rises out of the stump (geza, H1503) of Jesse (Isaiah 11:1). The Root of David is the One who prevails (Revelation 5:5).

## The Tree at the End

The trees of men fall and the Branch stands. Babylon, the last great tree of the kingdoms of men, is cut down for good in Revelation 18. In its place John sees one tree on either side of the river flowing from the throne of Yahuah and of the Lamb, the tree of life, whose leaves are *"for the healing of the nations"* (Revelation 22:2). The nations that once lived under the shade of Pharaoh and Nebuchadnezzar find their healing under the tree Yahuah planted.
