## What Yahuah Is Doing

Lamentations is the record of Yahuah keeping His word. He told Israel at the edge of the land what would happen if they refused His commandments (Deuteronomy 28:15), and in 586 BC Babylon burned Jerusalem and the temple. The book does not hurry past that. It walks through the whole loss slowly, and the way it is built shows how complete the loss was.

Four of its five poems are built on the Hebrew alphabet. The grief is told from the first letter to the last, nothing held back and nothing left out. Then, in the last chapter, the order falls away.

## The Pattern

The Hebrew alphabet has twenty-two letters, from *aleph* to *tav*. A poem built on it is called an acrostic: the first line begins with aleph, the next with bet, then gimel, and on down to the end.

- **Chapter 1** has twenty-two verses, one for each letter, in order.
- **Chapter 2** has twenty-two verses, one for each letter.
- **Chapter 3** has sixty-six verses, three in a row for each letter. Verses 1, 2, and 3 all begin with aleph, verses 4, 5, and 6 all begin with bet, and so on through the alphabet.
- **Chapter 4** has twenty-two verses, one for each letter.
- **Chapter 5** has twenty-two verses, the same count as the alphabet, but the lines do not follow its order.

The poems also get shorter as they go. In chapters 1 and 2, each letter carries a verse of three lines, and in chapter 3 each letter carries three one-line verses. In chapter 4, each letter carries two lines. In chapter 5, each verse is a single line. The voice runs down like breath running out.

## Why Build Grief on the Alphabet

**It says the whole thing.** An English speaker says “from A to Z” to mean all of it. An acrostic does the same. Every letter is spent on the loss, because the loss touched everything: the walls, the temple, the priests, the children, the king, the feasts, and the Sabbaths (2:6-9).

**It keeps grief in order.** Raw sorrow runs in circles. A poem bound to the alphabet has to move forward one letter at a time. The mourner cannot stay on one line forever. Lamentations lets the sorrow be as deep as it is, but it does not let it wander.

**It is easy to carry.** A people taken captive could not carry scrolls, but they could carry a song. A poem that runs letter by letter can be learned by heart and passed down. Yahuah told Moses to write a song and teach it to Israel so that it would be a witness against them when the evils came (Deuteronomy 31:19-21). Lamentations is the song the people sang when they did.

## The Other Alphabet: Psalm 119

The longest acrostic in Scripture is Psalm 119. It gives eight verses to each letter, 176 verses in all, and nearly every verse names the Law: His statutes, His testimonies, His precepts, His commandments. It is praise for the Torah from aleph to tav.

Lamentations is the same tool turned the other way. Psalm 119 is the blessing of keeping the Law, spelled out from beginning to end. Lamentations is the curse of refusing it, spelled out from beginning to end. The writer of Psalm 119 says, “Rivers of waters run down mine eyes, because they keep not thy law” (Psalm 119:136). Lamentations says, “Mine eye runneth down with rivers of water for the destruction of the daughter of my people” (3:48). Same rivers, same reason. The psalmist wept ahead of time. Lamentations weeps after the fact.

Other acrostics in Scripture follow the same plan, among them Psalms 25, 34, 37, 111, 112, and 145, and the woman of strength in Proverbs 31:10-31. The pattern was well known. Lamentations used it for a funeral.

## The Mouth Before the Eye

In the alphabet as it is usually written, the letter *ayin* comes before the letter *pe*. Chapter 1 follows that order. Chapters 2, 3, and 4 swap them, putting pe before ayin.

The two letters carry pictures in their names. *Ayin* means eye. *Pe* means mouth. In the three chapters with the swapped order, the verses tell the same story the letters do.

- In chapter 2, the pe verse opens with the enemy’s mouth: “All thine enemies have opened their mouth against thee” (2:16). The ayin verse follows: “Yahuah hath done that which he had devised” (2:17).
- In chapter 3, the pe verses open the same way: “All our enemies have opened their mouths against us” (3:46). The ayin verses that follow begin with the eye itself: “Mine eye trickleth down, and ceaseth not” (3:49), and “Mine eye affecteth mine heart” (3:51).
- In chapter 1, where the order is normal, the ayin verse is the weeping eye: “mine eye, mine eye runneth down with water” (1:16).

First the enemy’s mouth mocks, then the mourner’s eye weeps. The letters keep the order of what happened.

**What the scrolls show.** The swap is also the place where copies of Lamentations differ. The copy of Lamentations found among the Dead Sea Scrolls has chapter 1 in the pe-before-ayin order as well, matching chapters 2 through 4. This suggests that all four chapters may once have run pe before ayin, and that a later copyist set chapter 1 back into the usual order. It is a real place where the handling of the text can be traced, and it is a small one. The words of the verses are the same; only their order differs.

## When the Letters Run Out

Chapter 5 keeps the number twenty-two and lets go of the order. It is the people’s prayer, spoken together as “we”: “Remember, O Yahuah, what is come upon us” (5:1). The count stays, and the alphabet is gone.

*Author’s note: the author of this study holds a strong conviction that chapter 5 was written in alphabet order like the four chapters before it, and that the order was lost over time. Chapter 5 still keeps two of the book’s patterns: it has twenty-two lines, the number of the alphabet, and it finishes the shortening of the lines, three lines per letter in chapters 1 through 3, two in chapter 4, and one in chapter 5. No manuscript shows the alphabet order in chapter 5, so this study stands on the text as it has come down.*

## Bringing It Home

Lamentations does not hurry grief, and it does not hide the cause. The alphabet makes the mourner name every loss, and in the middle of the alphabet the mourner names the sin: “We have transgressed and have rebelled” (3:42). The order of the letters is the order of an honest heart: the whole loss told, the whole fault owned, and then the turn back to Yahuah (3:40).

The same Law that sent the curse also held the way home. If His people in captivity turn and obey His voice, He turns their captivity (Deuteronomy 30:1-3). The book spells the curse in full so that the turning can be full too.

## Where It Lands

Yahuah has always named Himself in terms of beginning and end: “I am the first, and I am the last; and beside me there is no Elohim” (Isaiah 44:6). In Revelation, He who sits upon the throne takes up the same name in the Greek alphabet: “I am Alpha and Omega, the beginning and the end” (Revelation 21:6). In the same breath He says, “Behold, I make all things new” (Revelation 21:5).

Lamentations ends by asking Yahuah to renew its days. The One who holds the whole alphabet answers with all things made new, and no more sorrow, nor crying (Revelation 21:4).
