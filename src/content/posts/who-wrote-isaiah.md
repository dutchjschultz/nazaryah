## What Yahuah Is Doing

Yahuah means to be known. Not guessed at and not argued over, but known, by every nation on the earth.

His way of doing that has never changed. He puts somebody in place to carry it, and He sends men to say so out loud.

One of the men He sent was named Isaiah the son of Amoz, and for two hundred and fifty years people have argued that he only wrote half of the book with his name on it.

## The Problem of Arithmetic

Anyone reading this book carefully runs into a gap in the numbers, and it is a fair one.

Isaiah is called in the year King Uzziah died, about 740 BC. Sennacherib comes up against Jerusalem in 701 BC, and Isaiah is standing there for it. His work ends somewhere around 690 to 680 BC.

Cyrus sends the captives home in 538 BC. That is a hundred and sixty-three years after Sennacherib, and roughly a hundred and fifty years after Isaiah was dead and buried.

So a man who died around 685 BC names a Persian king who had not been born yet, twice, by name (44:28; 45:1). That is the whole reason this argument exists.

## What the Argument Rests On

Strip it down and the case is one sentence: a man cannot name a king who has not been born, so whoever wrote those chapters must have been alive after Cyrus.

That is not a finding. It is a starting assumption, and everything else is built on top of it.

The split was proposed by a German scholar named Doederlein in 1775 and hardened into the three-part division by Bernhard Duhm in 1892 — the same man who invented the four Servant Songs. Both ideas came off the same desk within a hundred years of each other.

## The Book Answers This Itself

Naming things ahead of time is not an awkward feature of these chapters. It is the argument they are making, and it runs in three steps.

### Step one: the claim

Yahuah is in a courtroom with the idols, and the test He sets is exactly this one — tell us what is coming.

> “Let them shew the former things … or declare us things for to come. Shew the things that are to come hereafter, that we may know that ye are gods.”
>
> — Isaiah 41:22–23

Then He says plainly that this is what He Himself does, and the wording is about timing every time. Before they spring forth I tell you of them (42:9). Declaring the end from the beginning, and from ancient times the things that are not yet done (46:10). I have declared the former things from the beginning, and they came to pass (48:3).

### Step two: the reason

Chapter 48 is about idol worship. That is its subject, and anyone reading it straight through will see idols and nothing else at first. But look at the reason it gives for telling things early.

> “I have even from the beginning declared it to thee; before it came to pass I shewed it thee: lest thou shouldest say, Mine idol hath done them, and my graven image … hath commanded them.”
>
> — Isaiah 48:5

The protection is in the order of events. He said it first, so that when it happened nobody could turn around and hand the credit to a carved block.

Two verses later He does it again with a different lest. The new things are declared now, before the day, lest thou shouldest say, Behold, I knew them (48:6–7). Same method, a different excuse being shut down.

### Step three: what that costs the late reading

This verse is not evidence about who held the pen, and nobody should claim it is. It is evidence that these chapters claim their contents were announced before the events happened.

So if the Cyrus material was written after Cyrus came and went, the claim in verse 5 is not true of it. There was no advance notice, and so no protection against crediting an idol. The reason the chapter gives for its own existence stops working.

The late reading does not simply move a date to a more comfortable century. It makes the method these chapters describe into something that never happened.

## The Part the Theory Cannot Supply

If somebody else wrote the Cyrus chapters, the next question is who.

There is no answer. He is called Deutero-Isaiah, which is not a name. It is Latin for the second Isaiah. Two hundred and fifty years of work has not produced a candidate, a birthplace, or a date.

And this is a book that names people. Isaiah the son of Amoz. Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz and Hezekiah. Shebna and Eliakim. Rabshakeh. Sennacherib. Cyrus, by name, twice. The one man it supposedly will not name is the one who wrote chapter 53.

Nobody noticed him afterward either. The finest writer in the Hebrew Scriptures after Moses leaves behind no name, no tradition, no mention in Ezra or Nehemiah or Chronicles, and no heading in any copy of the book.

Two dated records point the other direction. A Jewish writer about 180 BC praises Isaiah and credits him with comforting those who mourned in Zion and showing what would come to pass to the end of time — which is the language of chapters 40 and 61. It is not Scripture, but it is a date, and it is two centuries before the Messiah.

Josephus, writing in the first century, records that Cyrus read the prophecy about himself and that this is why he issued the decree. A record, not an authority. But there is no competing record on the other side.

## What the Oldest Copy Shows

The Great Isaiah Scroll was found at Qumran and is dated to roughly 125 BC. It is the oldest complete copy of the book in existence, older than any other by a thousand years.

It runs chapter 39 into chapter 40 with no break, no heading, and no scribal mark of any kind. The scribes who copied it had never heard that this was two books.

## What the Apostolic Writings Do

John quotes from both halves inside one paragraph and names one man for both.

He quotes Isaiah 53:1 and calls it Esaias the prophet, then quotes Isaiah 6:10 two verses later and says Esaias said again (John 12:38–40). Front half and back half, one name, four verses apart.

Paul does the same inside one chapter — Isaiah 53:1 at Romans 10:16 and Isaiah 65:1 at Romans 10:20, both as Esaias.

And the scroll handed to Yahushua in the assembly at Nazareth is called the book of the prophet Esaias, and what He reads out of it is chapter 61 (Luke 4:17).

## One More Thing Inside the Book

Isaiah has a title for Yahuah that almost nobody else uses: the Holy One of Israel. It appears about twenty-five times in this book and only a handful of times in all the rest of the Scriptures.

Those twenty-five are split almost evenly between the front half and the back half. Whoever wrote chapter 60 had the same habit of speech as whoever wrote chapter 1.

## The Dates Inside the Book

The book is not vague about when it is happening, and the record outside it lines up.

Isaiah 6:1 dates his commission to the year King Uzziah died, about 740 BC.

Chapters 36 to 39 are plain narrative and appear almost word for word in 2 Kings 18 to 20. Sennacherib's campaign is 701 BC, and he recorded it himself on a clay prism now in the British Museum.

Isaiah 39:6 names Babylon as a threat still coming. Isaiah 40:2 speaks of the sentence as already served. Between two chapters the captivity goes from future to finished.

Isaiah 44:28 and 45:1 name Cyrus. Ezra 1 records his decree, about 538 BC, and the Cyrus Cylinder records his policy of sending captive peoples home.

Isaiah 48:20 orders them out of Babylon. Ezra 1 and 2 record them going.

## What Tense the Apostles Quote In

The useful test is not which verses get quoted. It is what tense they get quoted in.

The Babylon chapters were finished history five hundred years before a single Apostolic letter was written, and nobody quotes them as something still coming.

The chapters from 49 to 57 get quoted as done or as presently in force — chapter 53 read aloud by a traveller and explained as already fulfilled (Acts 8:32–35), chapter 54's barren woman quoted as a present fact (Galatians 4:27), chapter 56's house of prayer quoted by Yahushua inside the temple courts (Matthew 21:13).

The chapters from 58 to 66 get quoted as still ahead — the Redeemer coming to Zion (Romans 11:26), the new heavens and new earth (2 Peter 3:13), the fire that is not quenched (Mark 9:48).

No verse from the last stretch is ever quoted as fulfilled, and no verse from the Babylon stretch is ever quoted as future. Different men, different letters, decades apart, and none of them crosses that line.

Chapters 1 to 39 are left out of this test on purpose. That stretch holds history and promise together and gets quoted both ways, so it proves nothing either direction. Any honest handling of the book has to say so.

## The Order Is the Harder Problem for Them

Here is the part worth holding on to. More than one author makes the organization of this book harder to explain, not easier.

The same sentence closes two of the stretches, word for word. One title, the Holy One of Israel, is spread almost evenly across halves that are supposed to have different authors. The servant changes hands at the exact chapter where one stretch ends and the next begins.

Three men writing in three different centuries do not produce that. Either one man wrote it, or an editor of remarkable skill stitched it together and left no trace of himself — and nobody can name him either.

## Where This Has to Be Handled Honestly

The three-part split those scholars drew sits close to the stretches of time this book is read in here. That is not theft in either direction. The seams are real and everybody who reads carefully finds them.

The disagreement is not about where the seams are. It is about what put them there — three different men writing in three different centuries, or one man shown three different periods.

The book says which one it is, and it says so in the chapters the argument is about.

## But What If a Disciple Expanded It?

This is where most people land once they look at the book honestly. They can see it is well ordered, so the suggestion is that Isaiah had students who carried his work on and added to it. That would explain the unity and the later knowledge at the same time.

It sounds like a reasonable middle. It has three problems.

### Isaiah mentions his students once, and tells them to seal it

> “Bind up the testimony, seal the law among my disciples.”
>
> — Isaiah 8:16

He had students. The Hebrew word means his taught ones. And the one instruction he is recorded giving them is to bind it up and seal it.

The verse does not say how much. It names two things, the testimony and the law, and never defines where either one stops. So the honest answer about scope is that nobody can measure it from this verse.

The calendar does set a ceiling. This is spoken around 734 BC. Most of the book had not happened yet, let alone been written, so whatever is being sealed here, it is not sixty-six chapters.

But four verses later the same two words come back, and that is where the weight is.

> “To the law and to the testimony: if they speak not according to this word, it is because there is no light in them.”
>
> — Isaiah 8:20

The law and the testimony are the measuring stick. Every man who opens his mouth gets checked against them. And in verse 16 that same pair is handed to the students and sealed.

So whatever the contents were, the job is not in question. The students are holding the standard other people get measured by. They are keepers of a sealed deposit, not writers adding to it.

That is what has to be answered by anyone who says a student expanded the book. The one verse that puts Isaiah and his students in the same sentence has them guarding a closed thing, not continuing an open one.

### And sealing is the same thing chapter 48 is doing

There is no real difference between sealing a testimony and sealing a prophecy. Once a prophecy is spoken and written down, it is testimony. It is attested word.

What matters is what the sealing is for, and in Scripture it is for one of two things.

Sometimes it holds a word back until its hour. Daniel is told to shut up the words and seal the book, even to the time of the end (12:4). That one is not for his generation.

The other kind is evidence. Jeremiah buys a field, signs the deed, seals it and puts it in a clay jar so it will keep for many days (32:10–14). The sealed copy is the untouched original, produced later to prove what it said.

Isaiah 8 is the second kind, and he says why in the next verse: I will wait upon Yahuah, that hideth his face from the house of Jacob (8:17). Nobody is listening now. So close it, keep it, and when it happens the sealed copy proves it was said first.

That is the same mechanism chapter 48 describes from the other end. There, Yahuah says He told them before it came to pass so that afterward nobody could credit an idol. Here, Isaiah seals the word so that afterward nobody can say it was written up later.

One is the reason for handing the envelope over early. The other is the envelope being sealed. They are two halves of one practice, forty chapters apart.

### It moves the problem instead of answering it

Whoever wrote the Cyrus chapters either knew Cyrus was coming or he did not.

If he knew, then a man was shown something before it happened, and the whole reason for inventing a second author is gone.

If he did not know, then he wrote it afterward and presented it as foretold — and Isaiah 48:5 is a false claim no matter whose hand held the pen.

Changing the name on the page does not touch the actual question.

### A man writes chapter 53 and never signs it

Every other prophetic book names its man in the first verse. Jeremiah, Ezekiel, Hosea, Joel, Amos, Obadiah, Jonah, Micah, Nahum, Habakkuk, Zephaniah, Haggai, Zechariah, Malachi. Fourteen books, fourteen names.

A student good enough to write chapter 53 leaves behind no name, in a collection where naming the writer is the rule.

### What is already being conceded

Notice what the person has granted before the argument even starts: the book is well ordered and the prophecies land. That is the harder thing to account for, not the easier one.

One man explains order. A school spread over two centuries has to explain how different men landed on the same seams, kept a rare title at the same rate in both halves, and handed the servant from a nation to one man at exactly the chapter where the time period changes.

### What can be granted honestly

Scribes copied this book for centuries. Spelling was updated. Chapter numbers were added more than a thousand years later, and the headings in a printed Bible are a translator’s work, not Isaiah’s.

None of that is the same as somebody writing six chapters and putting another man’s name on them. Granting the small thing is what makes refusing the large one credible.

### The question underneath all of it

The authorship argument is not really about authorship. It comes down to one question: does Yahuah tell people what is coming before it happens?

If He does, this book needs no second author. If He does not, a second author does not rescue it, because chapter 48 is still claiming something that never occurred.

That is the disagreement. Everything else is downstream of it.

## Where It Lands

The last book of the Scriptures reaches back into Isaiah constantly, and it never sorts the front half from the back.

Come out of her, my people (Revelation 18:4) is the order given in Isaiah 48:20 and 52:11. The new heaven and new earth of Revelation 21 is the new heavens and new earth of Isaiah 65 and 66. Death swallowed up in Isaiah 25 is every tear wiped away.

Whoever wrote those chapters, the last book treats the whole scroll as one witness.

## In One Sentence

A man naming a king who was not born yet is not the weak point of this book. It is the argument the book is making, and it says so out loud.
