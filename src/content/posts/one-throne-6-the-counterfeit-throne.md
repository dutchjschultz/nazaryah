***kosmos** — the fallen human order, the rival kingdom outside the gate*

## Part One — The Surface

Ask a believer what "the world" means in Scripture and you will get one of three answers: the planet, the people on it, or "everything God made." None of these are what John means by the word.

John uses the Greek word *kosmos* (κόσμος) more than any other New Testament writer—roughly 78 times in his Gospel and 23 times in his first epistle. He is not using it loosely. In John's hands, *kosmos* means a moral-spiritual order. It is human life organized without Yahuah. A realm where darkness is preferred, truth is resisted, and sin runs the show. It is the opposite of the Father's Kingdom.

The church has mostly lost this meaning. "The world" in John 3:16 gets read as "everybody on the planet." In 1 John 2:15, it becomes "worldly pleasures." In John 17:14, it turns into "secular culture." Each reading grabs a piece but misses the whole. Worse, some of these misreadings have been used to prop up the Trinity doctrine. Once you define *kosmos* the way John actually uses it, those arguments fall apart.

## Part Two — The Root

**κόσμος**

***Kosmos***

*Arrangement, order, system. In John's usage: the organized human realm under sin's rule—the moral-spiritual order opposed to Yahuah's Kingdom.*

In classical Greek, *kosmos* just meant "order" or "arrangement." Something structured and set in place. By the first century it could mean the physical world, the people in it, or the way society was organized.

John takes this word and narrows it. For him, the *kosmos* is the system—the arranged order of human life apart from Yahuah. It is where sin rules, where lies are preferred over truth, and where a spiritual rebel governs in place of the Father. It is a counterfeit kingdom. An organized rebellion.

This is why so many verses seem contradictory until you understand the word. God loves the world but commands us not to love the world. Jesus came to save the world but says he does not pray for the world. The world is the object of God's compassion and also the enemy of His children. The answer is simple: *kosmos* is a condition, not a location. God loves the people trapped in the condition. He is opposed to the condition itself.

### 2.1 — The Septuagint and Kosmos

If John is being so deliberate with *kosmos*, did the Greek Old Testament—the Septuagint—already use it this way? No. And that makes his choice even more telling.

In the Septuagint, *kosmos* almost always means "ornament" or "decoration." In Exodus 33:5–6, Israel removes their ornaments—*kosmos*. In Isaiah 3:18–21, it refers to jewelry. The word carries its root meaning—"something arranged in order"—without any moral weight.

It is in the Wisdom of Solomon, widely read in the first century, where *kosmos* starts to mean "the world" more broadly. Wisdom 2:24 says death entered the *kosmos* through the devil's envy. That is closer to John but still not carrying the full weight he gives it.

John had other Greek words he could have used. He could have written *gē* (earth), *oikoumenē* (the inhabited world), or *aiōn* (the present age). He chose *kosmos* because its root meaning—"ordered system"—was exactly the right name for what he was describing: an order, a system, arranged against Yahuah. The word was not forced on him. He chose it on purpose.

## Part Three — The Ancient Ground

John did not invent this concept. The Old Testament has it everywhere—just scattered across different Hebrew words instead of packed into one Greek term.

The *goyim* (גּוֹיִם)—the nations—are the peoples outside Yahuah's covenant, walking in their own ways, governed by their own gods. Psalm 2:1–2 pictures them raging against Yahuah and His anointed—organized rebellion with rulers. That is exactly what John calls the *kosmos*.

The prophets use light-and-darkness language that John later picks up directly. Isaiah 9:2 describes people walking in darkness who see a great light. Isaiah 60:1–3 says darkness covers the earth and the people, but Yahuah's glory rises over His own. Two camps. One in light, one in darkness. John's framework runs on the same contrast.

The Torah draws a line between Israel and "the ways of the nations" (Leviticus 18:3, Jeremiah 10:2). The nations have a system—their own values, their own worship, their own way of organizing life. Yahuah tells His people not to walk in it. That is the same structural divide John draws between those "of God" and those "of the world." The vocabulary changes. The concept does not.

So the idea is fully present in the Old Testament: a human realm in rebellion against Yahuah, with its own rulers, values, and destiny. John just gives it one name.

## Part Four — The Bridge

There is something important to understand about John and Paul. They are dealing with a reality that Old Testament believers never experienced firsthand. The prophets pointed to it. The patterns in the Torah foreshadowed it. But when the Messiah actually came, he did not bring the physical kingdom Israel expected. He brought a spiritual kingdom and a spiritual temple. And that changed everything.

Before the cross, there was one world—the fallen order under sin's authority—and the hope of a coming kingdom. After the cross and resurrection, there are now two worlds running at the same time. There is the *kosmos*—the old order, still operating under sin and its ruler. And there is the new creation—a spiritual world that came from heaven through the Spirit of Yahuah, entered through the blood and life of the Messiah. Born from above. The Kingdom of God, present and active, but not visible in the way Israel had expected.

This is why John's vocabulary sounds different from the Old Testament. He is not just describing the rebellion. He is describing the *alternative* to the rebellion—a new creation where people are born of the Spirit, taken out of the *kosmos*, and placed into the Father's Kingdom while still physically living in the same world. The OT prophets saw this coming (Jeremiah 31:31–34, Ezekiel 36:26–27), but they did not experience the two worlds running side by side.

Paul's language does the same thing. When he says you were dead and are now alive (Ephesians 2:1–5), that sounds strange if you only have one world in your thinking. But if there are two worlds—the *kosmos* where sin reigns and the new creation where the Spirit reigns—then being "dead" means you were locked in the old system, and being "alive" means you have been transferred into the new one. A resurrection while you still breathe. A new birth while your body stays the same. This is not something Old Testament believers could fully grasp, because the new creation had not yet arrived. John and Paul are describing life on both sides of the cross, and their vocabulary reflects a reality that is newer than anything Moses or Isaiah lived in.

So when you read *kosmos* in John, remember: he is not just labeling the bad side. He is drawing a line between two present realities. The old order (the *kosmos*) and the new creation (the Kingdom). Every person is in one or the other. And faith in the Messiah is what moves you from one to the other.

## Part Five — The Discovery

### 5.1 — The World Means People

Here is a fact that should end most debates before they start: John routinely uses *kosmos* to refer to people. Not to the planet. Not to physical creation. To human beings who belong to the fallen order.

This is not to say *kosmos* is exactly the same as the English word "people." But it does not matter whether the Greek word is a precise equivalent. What matters is that John uses it to refer to people—and that is beyond question.

The Lamb takes away the sin of the *kosmos* (John 1:29)—lambs do not take away the sins of mountains or trees. God sent His Son so the *kosmos* might be saved (John 3:17)—people are saved, not deserts. Jesus gives his flesh for the life of the *kosmos* (John 6:51)—human beings receive life. The *kosmos* hates Jesus (John 7:7)—people hate, planets do not. Jesus is the light of the *kosmos* (John 8:12)—and John 1:4, 9 already told us the light was the light of men. The *kosmos* cannot receive the Spirit (John 14:17)—people receive or reject, not geography. The *kosmos* will no longer see Jesus (John 14:19)—people see, the planet does not. The *kosmos* hates the disciples (John 15:18–19)—people love and hate. The Spirit convicts the *kosmos* of sin (John 16:8)—people are convicted. Jesus does not pray for the *kosmos* (John 17:9)—he prays for his own and not for the fallen people outside.

The pattern is relentless. In verse after verse, the *kosmos* does things only people do: it hates, loves, sees, receives, rejects, rejoices, and is convicted. Those born of God are explicitly said to be "not of the world." Those who are not born of God are still "of the world." The *kosmos* is the human population under sin's government.

### 5.2 — A System That Hates the Light

John's *kosmos* is not neutral. It is hostile. It actively opposes Yahuah, His Son, and everyone who belongs to them.

Jesus says the world hates him because he testifies that its works are evil (John 7:7). The *kosmos* has "works," and those works are evil, and the Son's presence makes them visible. In John 15:18–19, he explains the logic: the world loves its own, but because he chose the disciples out of the world, the world hates them. It is not geography. It is allegiance. Being pulled out of the system makes you a target.

In John 17:14, Jesus says the world hates his disciples because they have received the Father's word. The word of the Father is what separates people from the *kosmos*. Receive it, and you become foreign to the system. The system responds with hatred.

The root problem is in John 3:19–20: light came into the world, and men loved darkness rather than light. This is not confusion. It is preference. The *kosmos* does not misunderstand the truth. It hates the truth, because truth exposes what the system is built on.

### 5.3 — The Ruler and the Beast

**ἄρχων τοῦ κόσμου**

***Archōn tou kosmou***

*The ruler of this world. A governing spiritual influence behind the anti-God system.*

The *kosmos* is not random chaos. It has a ruler. Jesus uses the phrase "the ruler of this world" three times in John's Gospel.

In John 12:31, he says the ruler of this world will be cast out—the cross is a regime change. In John 14:30, the ruler is coming but has no claim on Jesus—no sin, no foothold. In John 16:11, the ruler has already been judged—the sentence is pronounced.

This tells you what the *kosmos* really is. It is not just bad behavior. It is a kingdom-like structure with a spiritual authority behind it. Organized rebellion. A counterfeit kingdom. First John 5:19 says it plainly: "The whole world lieth in the evil one." The Greek verb *keitai* means to lie, to be situated. The whole system rests in the lap of its ruler.

And the ruler does not stay invisible. Revelation 13 shows what happens when the *kosmos* takes institutional form. The dragon—the same adversary John calls "the ruler of this world"—gives his power, his throne, and his authority to a beast (Revelation 13:2). That beast is not a new enemy. It is the *kosmos* made visible, organized into a system that governs, enforces, and demands worship. It mirrors every part of Yahuah's covenant: God places His law on the heart and hand as a sign of what governs thought and action (Deuteronomy 6:6–8), and the beast claims the same territory—the hand and forehead (Revelation 13:16)—not as a literal stamp on the skin, but as an image of allegiance, declaring whose authority a person submits to in mind and deed. God seals His people with His name (Revelation 14:1), and the beast demands the same loyalty under a different name. God made humanity in His image—*tselem* (Genesis 1:26–27)—to bear His character and reflect His authority. The beast produces its own image (Revelation 13:14–15)—a counterfeit identity, a constructed religious likeness that looks devoted to God while quietly departing from His covenant. This is the golden calf pattern repeating at the largest possible scale: worship redesigned to feel faithful while serving a different master. Romans 1:21–23 describes the same exchange in different language—mankind traded the glory of the incorruptible God for images of corruptible things. The *kosmos* is the soil where that exchange happens. The beast is the tree that grows from it. The image of the beast is the fruit—a whole population bearing the likeness of the rebellion instead of the likeness of the Father. And the new creation reverses the entire process: believers are renewed into the image of Yahuah through the Messiah (Colossians 3:10, Romans 8:29), bearing the Father's name on their foreheads instead of the mark of the system that enslaved them. Two images. Two kingdoms. Two identities. That is the *kosmos* and the Kingdom, drawn to their sharpest possible contrast.

### 5.4 — Rescue, Not Approval

If the *kosmos* is hostile and sin-ruled, how can God love it? Because the people and the system are not the same thing. The system is the problem. The people are trapped in it. God loves the people. He opposes the system.

John 3:16 says God loved the *kosmos* enough to give His Son. That love is directed at humanity in its fallen condition—people under the rule of sin. God's response to the *kosmos* is not approval. It is rescue. He sent the Son not to condemn but to save (John 3:17). First John 2:2 says the provision covers the sins of the whole *kosmos*—every person under sin's dominion is within reach. But the goal is to bring people out, not to bless the system they are in.

John 1:29 calls Jesus "the Lamb of God, which taketh away the sin of the world." Not sins, plural—the sin. The singular condition. The root of the rebellion. The Lamb does not just forgive individual acts. He addresses what makes the *kosmos* what it is.

### 5.5 — The World You Must Not Love

God loves the world. Believers must not love the world. There is no contradiction. God loves the people trapped in the system. Believers must not love the system itself.

First John 2:15–17 defines exactly what "the world" is as a system: the lust of the flesh (desire apart from God's design), the lust of the eyes (craving without reference to God's will), and the pride of life (self-sufficiency with no need of Yahuah). These are not a list of forbidden activities. They are the operating principles of the *kosmos*. They are what makes the system run.

The mutual exclusion is total: "If any man love the world, the love of the Father is not in him." You cannot love both. This is not about avoiding restaurants or movies. It is about where your allegiance lies. And the system is temporary: "The world passeth away, and the lust thereof." The *kosmos* is already sentenced and already passing away. The Father's Kingdom endures.

### 5.6 — Born From Above

**ἄνωθεν**

***Anōthen***

*From above; again. Used by John for spiritual rebirth—being born of the Spirit, entering the Father's Kingdom. Not a geographical origin but a spiritual one.*

John divides all of reality into two camps. No spectrum. No gray zone. Every person belongs to one side or the other.

In John 8:23, Jesus says: "Ye are from beneath; I am from above: ye are of this world; I am not of this world." Trinitarians grab this verse and claim Jesus is saying he descended from heaven—proof of pre-existence. But that is not what John means by "from above."

John has already defined this word. In John 3:3–7, Jesus tells Nicodemus you must be born *anōthen*—from above, of the Spirit. "That which is born of the flesh is flesh; and that which is born of the Spirit is spirit." Being "from above" means being born of the Spirit. It means entering the Father's Kingdom—the new creation. John 3:31 confirms it: "He that cometh from above is above all: he that is of the earth is earthly." Two orders. One from the Spirit, one from the flesh.

Jesus is "from above" because he is fully born of the Spirit, the firstborn of the new creation, walking entirely in the Father's Kingdom. Every believer who is born from above enters that same realm. This is not a travel narrative about descending from heaven. It is a statement about which kingdom you belong to. The *kosmos* is "beneath." The Father's Kingdom is "above." And being "from above" is about spiritual birth, not geography.

The rest of John's two-camp language confirms this. Light and darkness (John 1:5, 3:19–21, 1 John 1:5–7). Truth and lie (John 8:44, 1 John 2:21–22). Of God and of the world (1 John 4:5–6). Children of God and the whole world lying in the evil one (1 John 5:19). Two sides. No middle ground.

### 5.7 — Sent In, Not Of

Jesus uses the word "world" eighteen times in his prayer in John 17. He is drawing the sharpest possible line between his disciples and the system they live in.

He does not pray for the *kosmos* (John 17:9). He prays for those pulled out of it. He asks that they not be taken out of the world but kept from the evil one (John 17:15–16)—because they remain physically in the *kosmos* but are not "of" it. They are foreign agents in hostile territory. Then he sends them back in: "As thou hast sent me into the world, even so have I also sent them into the world" (John 17:18). Sent into, not belonging to. Representatives of another Kingdom, entering the system to pull people out.

This is the pattern: rescued from the *kosmos*, kept from the evil one, and sent back in as agents of the Father's Kingdom. Living in the world. Not of it. Hated for it.

### 5.8 — Overcoming the World

**νικάω**

***Nikaō***

*To conquer, to overcome. John uses this word for the believer's relationship to the kosmos: not escape, but victory.*

In 1 John 5:4–5, whatever is born of God overcomes the world, and the victory is faith—specifically, believing that Jesus is the Son of God. The Greek word *nikaō* means to conquer, to prevail. This is military language. The *kosmos* is not something you avoid. It is something you defeat.

Jesus says in John 16:33 that he has already overcome the world. The outcome is decided. In 1 John 4:4, the one in you—the Spirit of God—is greater than the one in the *kosmos*. This is not about feeling strong. It is about the hierarchy of authority. The Father's authority exceeds the authority of the world and its ruler.

## Part Six — The Restoration

### 6.1 — What the Church Built on the Wrong Definition

When the church reads *kosmos* as "the planet" or "the physical universe," it forces meanings onto verses that the text was never making. Several of John's most important passages get hijacked for the Trinity doctrine. In every case, the misreading depends on *kosmos* meaning something John never intended it to mean.

**John 1:10** — "He was in the world, and the world was made by him, and the world knew him not." Trinitarians read this as proof that Jesus created the physical universe. But John 1:6–11 is talking about the Father. Verses 6–8 tell us John the Baptist was sent to bear witness of the Light. And who is the Light? First John 1:5 answers plainly: "God is light, and in him is no darkness at all." The Father is the Light. John the Baptist was preparing the way for the God of Israel—bearing witness that the Father was about to communicate with His people in a new way (Hebrews 1:2). The *kosmos*—the fallen human order—came about through the Father's purpose. And "the world knew him not" is confirmed by Jesus himself: "Righteous Father, the world has not known you" (John 17:25). Yes, Jesus calls himself the light of the world in John 8:12—but believers are also called the light of the world in Matthew 5:14, and that does not make us God. We carry the Father's light because He lives in us. The Messiah carried the Father's light fully because the Father's Spirit was on him without measure. But the Light itself—in John 1:6–11—is the Father. This passage is about the God of Israel coming to His people. Not about Jesus creating the universe.

**John 17:5** — "Glorify thou me with thine own self with the glory which I had with thee before the world was." Trinitarians read "before the world was" as "before the Genesis creation," claiming it proves Jesus existed in heaven as God. But if *kosmos* means the fallen human order, then "before the world was" means before this rebellion existed—the glory the Messiah had in the Father's plan and foreknowledge (1 Peter 1:20). Foreknown, not pre-existent.

**John 8:23** — "I am from above." Trinitarians claim this proves Jesus descended from heaven. But John defines *anōthen* ("from above") in chapter 3 as being born of the Spirit—entering the Father's Kingdom. Jesus is "from above" because he walks entirely in the new creation. Every believer born from above shares the same origin. Kingdom language, not a pre-existence proof.

In every case, the trinitarian argument depends on *kosmos* meaning "the physical planet" or "the created universe." Once you let John define his own word—and the evidence across his entire Gospel and epistles is overwhelming—those arguments lose their foundation.

### 6.2 — The Silence in Revelation

If *kosmos* means the sin-ruled order, then you would expect the word to vanish when sin is removed. And that is exactly what happens.

Earlier in Revelation, *kosmos* still appears—"the kingdoms of this world" (11:15), "from the foundation of the world" (13:8, 17:8). The word is active while the rebellion is still in play. But when John reaches Revelation 21 and 22—the vision of the final state—*kosmos* disappears.

John describes the new heaven and new earth using *gē* (earth), not *kosmos*. He uses *polis* (city) for the New Jerusalem. He describes Yahuah dwelling directly with His people (Revelation 21:3). No more death, no sorrow, no pain—the former things have passed away (Revelation 21:4). No curse, no night, His servants see His face and His name is on their foreheads (Revelation 22:3–5).

But *kosmos*? Gone. Not reformed. Not renewed. Not mentioned. It is absent because it no longer exists. The planet continues as the new *gē*. The people continue, dwelling with God. But the *kosmos*—the ordered system of sin and rebellion—is finished. The word vanishes because the reality it described has been replaced by the uncontested Kingdom of Yahuah.

That silence is one of the strongest confirmations that *kosmos* in John was never just "the planet." If it meant the planet, the word would still be there. The planet is still there. But the *kosmos* is not—because it was never the planet. It was the rebellion. And the rebellion is over.

### 6.3 — The Word They Never Let You Define

You have probably read the word "world" in your Bible a thousand times and never once been told what the writer actually meant by it. That is not an accident. When a word stays vague, it can be made to mean whatever the teacher needs it to mean. "The world" becomes a blank check—cash it for the Trinity in John 1:10, cash it for pre-existence in John 17:5, cash it for universal love in John 3:16, cash it for moral scolding in 1 John 2:15. Same word, four different sermons, none of them driven by what John intended.

That is how false doctrine survives. Not by lying outright, but by keeping the definitions soft. As long as *kosmos* stays blurry—sometimes the planet, sometimes people, sometimes creation, sometimes "worldliness"—you can steer it wherever you want. But the moment you let John define his own term, the steering stops. The word snaps into focus. And what you see is not what the church has been telling you.

John's *kosmos* is the fallen human order—people under sin's authority, governed by a spiritual rebel, hostile to the Father, and destined for replacement. It is not the planet. It is not the physical creation. It is the kingdom that opposes Yahuah's Kingdom. And the reason John uses this one word over a hundred times is because he wants you to see the line—the line between the old order and the new creation, between death and life, between the realm of sin and the realm of the Spirit.

The church taught you to read past that line. To treat *kosmos* as background scenery rather than the main subject. To let translators and theologians decide what "world" means before you ever get to the text. And the result is a generation of believers who can quote John 3:16 from memory but cannot tell you what "the world" in that verse actually refers to.

Go back to the text. Let the writer speak. When John says *kosmos*, he is pointing at the enemy's kingdom and telling you it has been judged, it is passing away, and you do not belong to it anymore. That is not vague. That is not soft. And it does not need a theologian to explain it away. It needs a believer willing to read what is actually on the page.
