## What Yahuah Is Doing

Lamentations is a funeral for a city. Jerusalem fell to Babylon in 586 BC, and Yahuah kept the word He had given through Moses: the curse for refusing His commandments (Deuteronomy 28:15) and the promise to turn the captivity of those who return to Him (Deuteronomy 30:1-3). The book mourns the way Yahuah’s people mourned, and so it opens a window onto the funeral as Scripture shows it.

This study walks through five parts of a funeral: the song, the mourners, the body, the Law’s limits, and the message about the dead. Each part is shown twice, first as Scripture shows it and then as it is commonly done today. No claim is made that today’s funeral was copied from anything. The two are simply set side by side.

## One: The Song

**In Scripture.** The dead were sung over with a *qinah*, a funeral song. It had a sound everyone knew. Each line split into two halves, and the second half was shorter than the first, so the line fell short like a mourner’s step. Lamentations opens that way: “How doth the city sit solitary,” a full half, then “that was full of people,” a short one (1:1). The song usually began with the cry “How!” and told the story of a fall. David sang one over Saul and Jonathan: “How are the mighty fallen!” (2 Samuel 1:19).

The funeral song told the truth. It named the loss, and in Lamentations it named the sin: “We have transgressed and have rebelled” (3:42). The prophets even sang it over the living as a warning to turn back while there was time (Amos 5:1-2).

**Today.** The center of the service is the eulogy, a Greek word meaning good words, a speech of praise for the one who died. The service is often called a celebration of life. It speaks of the good the person did and rarely speaks of sin, judgment, or warning.

## Two: The Mourners

**In Scripture.** Grief was seen and heard. Lamentations shows it in one verse: the elders “sit upon the ground, and keep silence: they have cast up dust upon their heads; they have girded themselves with sackcloth” (2:10).

- Garments were torn (Genesis 37:34; 2 Samuel 1:11).
- Sackcloth was worn and dust put on the head (Joshua 7:6).
- Friends sat on the ground in silence, as Job’s did for seven days (Job 2:13).
- People wept out loud, and mourning women were called in to lead the wailing (Jeremiah 9:17-18; Matthew 9:23).
- There were set days of mourning: seven for Saul, thirty for Aaron and for Moses (1 Samuel 31:13; Numbers 20:29; Deuteronomy 34:8).

**Today.** The mourners wear their best clothes, sit in rows, and keep composed. Grief is covered over rather than let out. Tears are dabbed away, the family is praised for “holding up well,” and what the heart is actually saying stays hidden behind a brave face. Mourning usually ends when the service and the meal are over.

## Three: The Body

**In Scripture.** The body was washed (Acts 9:37), wound in linen with spices, “as the manner of the Jews is to bury” (John 19:40), and buried quickly, often the same day (Deuteronomy 21:23). Abraham bought a cave to bury Sarah (Genesis 23:19-20), and Yahuah Himself buried Moses (Deuteronomy 34:6). The only two men embalmed in Scripture were Jacob and Joseph, both in Egypt and by Egyptian physicians (Genesis 50:2-3, 26). Embalming was Egypt’s custom, not Israel’s.

**Today.** The body is commonly embalmed and set out for viewing days later. Embalming became common in America during the Civil War, when soldiers’ bodies were preserved to be shipped home. A growing share of the dead are now cremated.

**What Scripture says about cremation.** Scripture gives no command against burning the dead and never calls it a sin. Its pattern is burial. Burning a body shows up mostly as judgment, as with Achan (Joshua 7:25), and once as a crime when Moab burned the bones of the king of Edom (Amos 2:1). The men of Jabesh-gilead burned the bodies of Saul and his sons and then buried their bones, and David blessed them for it (1 Samuel 31:12-13; 2 Samuel 2:5). Burial carries a picture: the body is sown like a seed and raised (1 Corinthians 15:42-44). The resurrection does not depend on how a body was laid down, since the sea gives up the dead that are in it (Revelation 20:13). Burial is the pattern Scripture shows; cremation is not named as sin.

## Four: The Law’s Limits

**In Scripture.** The Law set boundaries around death. Whoever touched a dead body was unclean seven days and had to be cleansed (Numbers 19:11-12), because death was the enemy, not a natural part of life to be made beautiful. Israel was also forbidden the mourning rites of the nations: “Ye shall not make any cuttings in your flesh for the dead, nor print any marks upon you” (Leviticus 19:28; Deuteronomy 14:1).

**Today.** Memorial tattoos, the name or face of the dead marked on the skin, have become a common way to grieve. Leviticus 19:28 sets marks printed on the body in the same breath as cuttings for the dead, both mourning rites of the nations that Israel was told to leave alone.

## Five: The Message About the Dead

**In Scripture.** The dead are asleep. They “know not any thing” (Ecclesiastes 9:5), and they “sleep in the dust of the earth” until they awake (Daniel 12:2). Yahushua called death sleep (John 11:11-14). Peter said on Shavuot that even David “is not ascended into the heavens” (Acts 2:34). The hope is the resurrection: the dead in Messiah rise at His coming (1 Thessalonians 4:13-16), and the trumpet raises them incorruptible (1 Corinthians 15:52).

**Today.** The common word at a funeral is that the loved one is in a better place, already in heaven, looking down, or with the angels. The hope is placed at the moment of death.

This is the biggest difference between the two funerals. Today’s service places the hope at death. Scripture places it at the resurrection.

## Bringing It Home

Laid side by side, the two funerals run in opposite directions at every step. Scripture lets grief out; today’s funeral covers it up. Scripture names the loss and the sin; today’s funeral praises the person. Scripture buries the body quickly as a seed; today’s funeral preserves and displays it, as Egypt did. Scripture forbade marks for the dead; today they are common. Scripture says the dead sleep until the resurrection; today’s funeral says they are already in heaven.

The funeral in Scripture let grief be grief. It sat on the ground, wept out loud, told the truth about why death came, refused the rites of the nations, buried the body as a seed, and waited for Yahuah to raise the dead. Lamentations shows all of this at the hardest funeral Israel ever held, and it still ends in prayer, not despair: “Turn thou us unto thee, O Yahuah… renew our days as of old” (5:21).

## Where It Lands

Revelation shows mourners one last time, over Babylon. Kings, merchants, and sailors weep and wail, stand far off, and throw dust on their heads, crying, “Alas, alas, that great city” (Revelation 18:9-10, 19). It is a funeral scene in everything but name, and the city that burned Jerusalem gets the mourning she once caused.

Then the mourning ends for good. Death and the grave are cast into the lake of fire (Revelation 20:14), and Yahuah wipes away all tears: “there shall be no more death, neither sorrow, nor crying” (Revelation 21:4).
