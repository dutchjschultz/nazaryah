## What Yahuah Is Doing

Yahuah means to be known. Not guessed at and not argued over, but known, by every nation on the earth.

His way of doing that has never changed. He puts somebody in place to carry it — somebody who lives it out in the open where the rest of the world can watch.

Isaiah is the record of who was holding that assignment. It changes hands twice before the book is finished, and the whole book is readable once a person knows which hand it is in.

## When This Is Happening

Chapter 42 sits in the second of Isaiah's four stretches of time — chapters 40 through 48, the Babylon captivity and the road home, roughly 605 to 538 BC.

That matters for one reason. The servant who gets introduced at the top of this chapter is introduced in the middle of the Babylon material, hundreds of years before the chapters where He does the work. Isaiah shows Him early and explains Him later.

## Reading the Chapter Whole

Chapter 42 is usually read in pieces. The front of it gets quoted at Christmas services and the back of it gets skipped.

Read straight through, it holds two different servants about eighteen verses apart, and the difference between them is the point.

## Behold My Servant

> “Behold my servant, whom I uphold; mine elect, in whom my soul delighteth; I have put my spirit upon him.”
>
> — Isaiah 42:1

Nothing about this one is failing. He is upheld. He is chosen. Yahuah says His own soul delights in him.

One chapter earlier the servant was a worm. This is a different kind of description entirely.

## He Does Not Shout

> “He shall not cry, nor lift up, nor cause his voice to be heard in the street.”
>
> — Isaiah 42:2

A man sent to set the whole earth right does not raise his voice in public. No shouting. No crowd work.

That one verse rules out most of what people picture when they imagine a deliverer showing up.

## The Bent Reed and the Dying Wick

> “A bruised reed shall he not break, and the smoking flax shall he not quench.”
>
> — Isaiah 42:3

Both of these are everyday objects, and the verse opens up once a person knows what they were.

A reed is a hollow stalk that grows by the water. People cut them for walking sticks and measuring rods. Once a reed is cracked it will not hold weight, so the normal thing is to snap it off and cut a fresh one.

Flax is the wick in an oil lamp. When a wick burns down and starts smoking instead of giving light, it is finished. You pinch it out and put in a new one.

Both are things a person throws away without thinking twice. He does neither. He will not finish off what is already cracked, and He will not pinch out what is barely still burning.

## What He Is Sent to Do

> “I Yahuah have called thee in righteousness … and will give thee for a covenant of the people, for a light of the Gentiles; To open the blind eyes, to bring out the prisoners from the prison.”
>
> — Isaiah 42:6–7

He is sent to open blind eyes. That becomes important twelve verses later.

The far-off nations are also waiting on him for something specific.

> “He shall not fail nor be discouraged, till he have set judgment in the earth: and the isles shall wait for his law.”
>
> — Isaiah 42:4

The isles are the distant countries. What they are waiting for is His law. Not a replacement for it, and not a release from it. They are waiting to finally receive it.

## The Light Was Handed to Him

Verse 6 is worded carefully. Yahuah says, I will give thee for a light. The light is handed over. It is not something the servant arrived holding.

## A Verse People Fight Over

> “I am Yahuah: that is my name: and my glory will I not give to another, neither my praise to graven images.”
>
> — Isaiah 42:8

This gets used to argue that Yahuah never lets anybody else have any glory at all. The verse answers that itself in its second half.

Hebrew writing says a thing twice. The first line gives the thought and the second tells what it meant. My glory, to another. My praise, to graven images. The other party is named out loud, and it is a carved idol.

Chapter 48 settles it. A few verses ahead of time Yahuah says the problem is people claiming their idol did it — mine idol hath done them, my graven image commanded it (48:5). Then comes the same sentence: I will not give my glory unto another (48:11). The one trying to take credit is a block of wood.

So the verse was never a rule about whether Yahuah can honor a person. Isaiah says the opposite later on: arise, shine, for the glory of Yahuah is risen upon thee (60:1).

## Now the Other Servant

Twelve verses after the one who opens blind eyes, Yahuah turns and looks at the nation He named in chapter 41.

> “Hear, ye deaf; and look, ye blind, that ye may see. Who is blind, but my servant? or deaf, as my messenger that I sent?”
>
> — Isaiah 42:18–19

The blindest man in the room is His servant. The deafest man in the room is His messenger.

The next verse puts it plainer: seeing many things, but you do not notice; the ears are open, but he does not hear (42:20).

This is not about the pagan nations. This is the people He picked, in the chapter right after He picked them.

## Why Isaiah Set Them Together

A servant who cannot see cannot show anybody anything. A messenger who cannot hear has no message to carry.

Yahuah never cancels the assignment. He does not say the plan is off, or that He changed His mind about having a servant in the world.

The assignment stands. The one holding it cannot do it. And the only other person in the chapter is the one sent to open blind eyes.

The problem and the answer are eighteen verses apart, in one chapter.

## Where the Blindness Goes

The Scriptures do not drop the word. At the end of the last book the final assembly on the list is told the same thing.

> “anoint thine eyes with eyesalve, that thou mayest see.”
>
> — Revelation 3:18

A few chapters later the servants are no longer blind.

> “and his servants shall serve him: And they shall see his face; and his name shall be in their foreheads.”
>
> — Revelation 22:3–4

A blind servant in Isaiah 42, servants who see His face on the last page. The next four parts walk that road.

## In One Sentence

Chapter 42 holds two servants: one who was chosen and went blind, and one who was sent to open blind eyes — quietly, without breaking anything already bent.
