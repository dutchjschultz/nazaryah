## What Came West

The hamsa is a palm-shaped charm, usually with the fingers held together and often with an eye set in the center of the palm. It hangs on necklaces, keychains, car mirrors, and walls, and it is now common in American boutiques and home décor stores.

It is the one charm that both Islam and Judaism claim. The name comes from the Arabic and Hebrew word for "five," after the five fingers. In Muslim lands it is called the Hand of Fatima, after Fatima, daughter of Muhammad. In Jewish communities it is called the Hand of Miriam, after the sister of Moses, and the five fingers are said to stand for the five books of Moses. The charm itself is older than both religions; open-hand amulets were used in the ancient Near East and North Africa long before either claimed it.

## What It Is For

The hamsa has one job: to ward off harm, especially the "evil eye," the envious glance believed to bring sickness and bad luck. The eye in the palm is meant to stare that glance back. It is sold as protection, and it is hung where protection is wanted — over a door, over a baby's crib, in a car.

## What Scripture Says

Isaiah listed the jewelry of Jerusalem's women that Yahuah would strip away in judgment, and among the "tinkling ornaments" were "the earrings" (Isaiah 3:18-20). The Hebrew word there, *lechashim*, means charms or whispered spells — amulets worn for protection. Yahuah counted protective charms as part of Jerusalem's pride.

The Law also closes the door on every kind of charm-work: there was not to be found in Israel "an enchanter, or a witch, Or a charmer" (Deuteronomy 18:10-11). The believer's protection is not on his chain or his mirror. "My help cometh from Yahuah, which made heaven and earth" (Psalm 121:2).

Islam itself has teachers who condemn the hamsa, since its own tradition forbids trusting amulets over Allah. The charm crossed into both religions from the outside, and neither has been able to put it out.

## The Verdict

The hamsa is an amulet, and an amulet is trust placed in an object. Naming it after Fatima or Miriam, or counting its fingers as the five books of Moses, does not make it lawful. The Torah is not a charm hung on a wall; it is a law kept by the hand. The hamsa belongs under the oak.

In Revelation the one who is kept from harm is kept by Yahuah's seal, not by a charm: the servants of God are "sealed ... in their foreheads," and the locusts may hurt "only those men which have not the seal of God in their foreheads" (Revelation 7:3; 9:4).
