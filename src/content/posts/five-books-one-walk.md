## Introduction

Torah means instruction. The word comes from the Hebrew *yarah*, to aim or to point the way, as an archer aims an arrow or a guide points a traveler down a road. The Torah is not five separate subjects bound under one cover. It is one road, walked by one people, from the day Yahuah called a man out of his father's country to the day that man's descendants stood at the Jordan.

That road was written down for more than the people who walked it. The writer of Hebrews calls the Law "a shadow of good things to come" (Hebrews 10:1). Paul says the appointed times and sabbaths are "a shadow of things to come; but the body is of Messiah" (Colossians 2:17), and he says the wilderness events "happened unto them for ensamples" and were "written for our admonition" (1 Corinthians 10:11). Yahushua Himself, on the road to Emmaus, began "at Moses" when He laid out the things written concerning Him (Luke 24:27), and He told His critics that Moses "wrote of me" (John 5:46).

A shadow carries the shape of the thing that casts it. Laid side by side, the five books trace the same steps every believer takes: called out, delivered by blood, taught to walk, counted and tested in the wilderness, and made ready in heart to enter. The order is not loose. Each book stands on the one before it, and none of the five steps can be skipped.

The order also settles a question that divides modern teaching. Deliverance comes first, and the Law follows it. Israel did not keep the commandments in order to leave Egypt; Israel left Egypt and then received the commandments. Yahuah opens the Ten Words by saying so: "I am Yahuah thy Elohim, which have brought thee out of the land of Egypt" (Exodus 20:2). The blood opens the door, and the Law is how the delivered walk once they are through it.

## Genesis — The Calling

Genesis is how the nation was built. It opens with Yahuah speaking light into darkness and dividing the two (Genesis 1:3–4), and it narrows from the whole world down to one family. Yahuah tells Abram, "Get thee out of thy country" (Genesis 12:1), and Abram goes without knowing where. He believes Yahuah, and that belief is counted to him for righteousness (Genesis 15:6). The Law at Sinai came four hundred thirty years after that promise (Galatians 3:17).

The rest of the book builds the family: Isaac the son of promise, Jacob renamed Israel, twelve sons, and Joseph carried into Egypt ahead of them all. Genesis closes with the family living in a foreign land and Joseph laid in a coffin (Genesis 50:26), but not without a promise. Joseph's last words are "Elohim will surely visit you, and bring you out of this land" (Genesis 50:24). The rest of the Torah is that sentence coming true.

The believer's walk begins the same way. Yahuah speaks first, and the one He calls is brought out of darkness "into his marvellous light" (1 Peter 2:9). Paul calls the result a new creation, where "all things are become new" (2 Corinthians 5:17). The calling comes before anything else, and it rests on belief.

## Exodus — The Deliverance

In Exodus the family has become a nation of slaves, and Yahuah keeps Joseph's promise. He names Himself as the one who acts: "I will bring you out" and "I will redeem you with a stretched out arm" (Exodus 6:6). The deliverance turns on a lamb. Each house chose a lamb without blemish on the tenth day of the first month and killed it on the fourteenth (Exodus 12:3, 6). Its blood went on the doorposts, and Yahuah said, "when I see the blood, I will pass over you" (Exodus 12:13). Israel walked out on the fifteenth (Numbers 33:3), and the sea closed behind them.

The shadow is plain. Yahushua said that whoever commits sin is the servant of sin (John 8:34), and Egypt is that bondage. Paul writes that "Messiah our Pesach is sacrificed for us" (1 Corinthians 5:7). The lamb was Yahuah's own provision, as Abraham said it would be: "Elohim will provide himself a lamb" (Genesis 22:8). Yahuah is the one who saves, and His Son is the way He provided. Paul even reads the sea crossing as an immersion, saying the fathers were all "baptized unto Moses in the cloud and in the sea" (1 Corinthians 10:2).

Exodus does not end at the sea. It goes on to Sinai, where the covenant is given, and it closes with the tabernacle raised and the glory of Yahuah filling it (Exodus 40:34). Yahuah delivered His people so that He could live among them: "let them make me a sanctuary; that I may dwell among them" (Exodus 25:8). For the believer, that dwelling is the body itself, the temple of the spirit (1 Corinthians 6:19).

## Leviticus — The Walk

Leviticus opens with Yahuah calling to Moses "out of the tabernacle" (Leviticus 1:1), from inside the tent He had just moved into. Its whole subject is how a delivered people lives near a holy Elohim: the offerings, the priesthood, clean and unclean, the appointed times of Leviticus 23, and the charge that sums it all up: "Ye shall be holy: for I Yahuah your Elohim am holy" (Leviticus 19:2).

Not one word of Leviticus was given to slaves still making bricks. It was given to people already brought out by the blood. That is the walk after the blood. Peter lifts Leviticus 19:2 straight into his letter to believers: "Be ye holy; for I am holy" (1 Peter 1:16). Paul takes the promise of Leviticus 26:12, "I will walk among you," and applies it to the assembly (2 Corinthians 6:16). The priesthood carries forward as well. Believers are "a royal priesthood" (1 Peter 2:9), made priests unto Elohim (Revelation 1:6).

Paul sets the two steps side by side. Believers are justified "by his blood," and, being reconciled, they "shall be saved by his life" (Romans 5:9–10). The blood opens the way, and the life is walked out. Exodus is the entry, and Leviticus is the walk that follows it.

## Numbers — The Numbering

Numbers opens a little more than a year after the night of Pesach, on the first day of the second month of the second year (Numbers 1:1). Yahuah orders a count of every man twenty years and older, "with the number of their names" (Numbers 1:2), each one declaring his family line "by the house of their fathers" (Numbers 1:18). The surface reason is in the text: these are the men "able to go forth to war" (Numbers 1:3). Each tribe then takes its place in the camp around the tabernacle, with Yahuah's dwelling at the center of the host (Numbers 2).

The deeper reason is in the word. The verb for the count is *paqad*, and its first meaning is to visit: to attend to someone and take note of them. It is the word of Joseph's promise, "Elohim will surely visit you" (Genesis 50:24), and the word of the day Israel believed Moses, when they heard "that Yahuah had visited the children of Israel" (Exodus 4:31). The census is that visit carried through. Yahuah had brought them out, and now He stood among them and counted them one by one, by name and by family.

The same word also judges. When the first generation refused to enter the land after the report of the spies, Yahuah said that "all that were numbered of you" from twenty years and upward would fall in the wilderness (Numbers 14:29). Thirty-eight years later a second count was taken, and not one man from the first count was left except Caleb and Joshua (Numbers 26:64–65; Deuteronomy 2:14). The first generation was delivered by the blood and still did not enter. "They could not enter in because of unbelief" (Hebrews 3:19).

That is the believer's present ground. The wilderness lies between deliverance and inheritance, and it is a place of testing. Paul says the fathers all passed through the sea, yet with many of them Elohim "was not well pleased: for they were overthrown in the wilderness" (1 Corinthians 10:5). Hebrews draws the same line and then gives the hope: "There remaineth therefore a rest to the people of Elohim" (Hebrews 4:9), and the charge is to labour to enter it (Hebrews 4:11). Paul also takes a line from Numbers to assure the believer that he is known: "Yahuah knoweth them that are his" (2 Timothy 2:19), the words Moses spoke during the rebellion of Korah (Numbers 16:5). The one who is numbered is called by name (John 10:3).

## Deuteronomy — The Heart

Deuteronomy is Moses' farewell to the second generation, spoken on the plains of Moab in the fortieth year, on the first day of the eleventh month (Deuteronomy 1:3), weeks before the crossing. It is the recap: the journey retold, and the Law given again to people who were children or not yet born at Sinai.

This time the Law is aimed at the heart. "These words… shall be in thine heart" (Deuteronomy 6:6). Moses commands, "Circumcise therefore the foreskin of your heart" (Deuteronomy 10:16), and then promises that Yahuah will do it Himself, so that Israel will love Him "with all thine heart" (Deuteronomy 30:6). The word is "very nigh unto thee, in thy mouth, and in thy heart, that thou mayest do it" (Deuteronomy 30:14). Paul quotes that same passage to describe the word of belief he preached (Romans 10:6–8), and the new covenant of Jeremiah is the same Law written "in their hearts" (Jeremiah 31:33; Hebrews 8:10).

Moses himself does not cross. He dies on Mount Nebo in sight of the land (Deuteronomy 34:1–5), and Joshua leads Israel in. Joshua received that name from Moses in Numbers, when Hoshea the son of Nun was renamed Yehoshua (Numbers 13:16), the same name the Son carries. The writer of Hebrews uses one Greek name, *Iesous*, for both men (Hebrews 4:8). Moses brings the people to the border with the Law in their hearts, and the one who bears the name Yahushua brings them in.

## Where the Pulpit Breaks the Order

Modern teaching keeps the first step and the last and cuts out the middle. It preaches the calling and the Pesach lamb, then jumps straight to the promised land as heaven. Leviticus and Numbers drop out, and with them the walk and the testing.

- **Leviticus cut out.** The Law is called a burden that ended at the cross, though Peter and Paul both use Leviticus as instruction for believers (1 Peter 1:16; 2 Corinthians 6:16).

- **Numbers cut out.** "Once saved, always saved" teaches that whoever passes under the blood is certain to enter, though the first count passed under the blood and fell in the wilderness, and Paul says it was written as a warning (1 Corinthians 10:5, 11).

- **Deuteronomy flattened.** The new covenant is taught as a new law replacing the old, though Deuteronomy, Jeremiah, and Hebrews all describe the same Law moved into the heart (Deuteronomy 30:6; Jeremiah 31:33; Hebrews 8:10).

Put back in order, the five books answer all three. The blood comes first, the walk follows it, the wilderness tests it, and the heart is made ready to enter.

## The Walk Finished in Revelation

Revelation carries all five steps to their end. Those with the Lamb are "called, and chosen, and faithful" (Revelation 17:14). They were washed from their sins "in his own blood" (Revelation 1:5). They "keep the commandments of Elohim, and have the testimony of Yahushua Messiah" (Revelation 12:17). They are sealed and numbered by tribe, twelve thousand from each, as the tribes were counted at Sinai (Revelation 7:4–8). The bride "hath made herself ready" (Revelation 19:7), and the tabernacle of Elohim is with men at last (Revelation 21:3). Standing by the sea of glass, they sing "the song of Moses the servant of Elohim, and the song of the Lamb" (Revelation 15:3): the song of the Torah and the song of the blood, sung as one.
