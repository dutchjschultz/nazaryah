At Wat Tham Suea, the Tiger Cave Temple in Krabi, Thailand, a golden serpent with five heads stands at the foot of the temple approach. Its Thai name is Phaya Nak. *Nak* is the Thai form of *naga*, the Sanskrit word for snake, and *Phaya* is a title meaning lord. The name says what the statue is: the serpent lord.

Anyone who has read Job 41 and Isaiah 27 in the King James will feel he has met this creature before. This study sets the two side by side. No historical record ties them together, and the study says so plainly below. The comparison is one of picture, not proof. Even so, the picture is worth a long look.

## Who the Phaya Nak Is

The naga began in ancient India and belongs to both Hindu and Buddhist tradition. In Thailand it came in with those religions and blended with older local serpent reverence. Thailand today is mainly Theravada Buddhist, so the Phaya Nak is met mostly at Buddhist temples.

Nagas are lords of the water, said to live beneath rivers and seas. They are often shown with an odd number of heads: three, five, seven, or nine. The best-known Buddhist story tells of Mucalinda, a naga king who wrapped himself seven times around the Buddha during a storm and spread his hood over the Buddha’s head. The oldest known carving of him, from the second century BC in India, gives him five heads. Later art usually gives him seven.

Naga bodies form the railings of temple stairs and walkways because the naga is believed to protect Buddhism. A man preparing to become a monk is even called a *nak*, after a story of a naga who took human form to be ordained. Naga statues receive offerings. On November 1, 2022, the Thai Cabinet approved the naga as the nation’s official mythological creature.

## The King James Portrait, Mark by Mark

**A serpent and a dragon.** Isaiah gives leviathan both names in one verse: “the piercing serpent,” “that crooked serpent,” and “the dragon that is in the sea” (Isaiah 27:1). The Phaya Nak is the same blend, a snake’s long body under a dragon’s face.

**Many heads.** Psalm 74 speaks of “the heads of leviathan” (Psalm 74:14), plural, though it gives no number. Revelation’s dragon has “seven heads” (Revelation 12:3). The statue has five.

**A king.** Leviathan is “a king over all the children of pride” (Job 41:34), and Revelation’s dragon wears “seven crowns” (Revelation 12:3). The Phaya Nak carries a lord’s title, and a tall golden crest rises from every head like a crown.

**Terrible teeth.** “Who can open the doors of his face? his teeth are terrible round about” (Job 41:14). All five mouths stand open, ringed with white fangs on every side.

**Sealed scales.** “His scales are his pride, shut up together as with a close seal” (Job 41:15). The body running up the walkway is covered in tight, overlapping scales.

**A strong neck.** “In his neck remaineth strength” (Job 41:22). The five necks rise out of one massive column.

**Raised up.** “When he raiseth up himself, the mighty are afraid” (Job 41:25). The statue is shown in exactly that moment, reared up at the gate.

**A creature of the water.** Leviathan is in the sea (Isaiah 27:1; Psalm 104:26), and “he maketh the deep to boil like a pot” (Job 41:31). The naga is a lord of the waters, and this statue rises from a base carved in curling, wave-like swirls.

**Fire.** “A flame goeth out of his mouth” (Job 41:21). The statue itself shows no fire. Yet each year, around the end of Buddhist Lent, crowds gather on the Mekong River to watch glowing balls of light said to rise from the water, and people in Thailand and Laos credit them to the Phaya Nak. These are called naga fireballs. Their cause is disputed, and some critics call them man-made.

## What the Record Cannot Show

No record shows the Thai naga came from Job, or that Thai artists knew the Bible. The naga arrived by way of India, and its story runs on its own path.

Part of the likeness also comes from the artist. Older Indian carvings often show a plain hooded cobra. The fierce fangs and tall crests are a style of the region, and this statue is a modern work.

The many-headed serpent is not only found in Thailand. Tablets from Ugarit in Syria, dated about 1350 to 1185 BC, speak of Litan, “the wriggling serpent” and a seven-headed one, in wording very close to Isaiah 27:1. Greece told of Herakles and the many-headed Hydra. Some scholars hold that Israel borrowed the image. This study reads the record the other way. The serpent came first, in the garden (Genesis 3:1), and the nations copied what they remembered, each in its own dress. Canaan gave him seven heads, Greece gave him to Herakles, and India and Thailand brought him to the temple door. Many copies point back to one original. The tablets are older than Isaiah, so what was copied was not Isaiah’s book but the older truth behind it. History alone cannot prove which way the copying ran, but Scripture names the serpent outright.

In the end, the likeness cannot be proved to be anything more than likeness. It can only be observed. That nine marks of the King James portrait can be laid over one figure, eight on the statue and the ninth in the belief around it, is a fact the reader may weigh for himself.

## Where the Two Part

The likeness ends at the temple door. The Phaya Nak is honored as a protector, and Mucalinda spreads his hood over the Buddha. Scripture gives the serpent no such place. The believer shelters under “the most High” and is told that “the young lion and the dragon shalt thou trample under feet” (Psalm 91:1, 13). The Law forbids bowing to the likeness of anything “in the water under the earth” (Exodus 20:4-5), the very home the naga is given.

This paper does not mock the people who honor the Phaya Nak. They see a guardian. Revelation calls the great dragon “that old serpent” who “deceiveth the whole world” (Revelation 12:9), and his end is the lake of fire (Revelation 20:10).
