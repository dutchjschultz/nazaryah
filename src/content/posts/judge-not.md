## The Verse Quoted by Men Who Quote Nothing Else

"Judge not, that ye be not judged" (Matthew 7:1). It is the best known sentence in the sermon and the least read, because almost nobody who uses it continues to the end of the paragraph. Six verses later the same speaker orders His hearers to identify dogs and swine. Something in the standard reading is wrong.

## A Measure, Not a Ban

The next verse gives the rule: "For with what judgment ye judge, ye shall be judged: and with what measure ye mete, it shall be measured to you again" (Matthew 7:2). That is weights-and-measures language, and it comes straight out of the Law. "Ye shall do no unrighteousness in judgment, in meteyard, in weight, or in measure. Just balances, just weights... shall ye have" (Leviticus 19:35-36). "Thou shalt not have in thy bag divers weights, a great and a small... a perfect and just weight shalt thou have" (Deuteronomy 25:13-15).

The warning is about the scale a man judges with, not about whether he may weigh anything at all. A man who uses a heavy weight for his brother and a light one for himself will be handed his own weights at his own hearing.

## The Sentence Ends With You Removing It

"Thou hypocrite, first cast out the beam out of thine own eye; and then shalt thou see clearly to cast out the mote out of thy brother's eye" (Matthew 7:5). The paragraph closes with the reader taking something out of his brother's eye. A prohibition on judging cannot end with instructions for judging.

The word rendered hypocrite is hypokrites, a stage actor, a man performing a part he is not living. The disqualification is the beam, not the looking. Deal with the beam and the sentence hands the task back to you, sight restored.

The picture is deliberately absurd. A man with a roof beam in his own eye is squinting at a splinter in someone else's. The absurdity is not that he noticed the splinter, which is really there. It is the order he chose. Deal with the beam first and the mote is still on the list, waiting.

## And Then, Dogs and Swine

"Give not that which is holy unto the dogs, neither cast ye your pearls before swine" (Matthew 7:6). Obeying that command requires a judgment about persons, made before anything is handed over. It sits five verses after judge not. Whatever verse 1 forbids, it plainly does not forbid this.

The apostolic writings read it the same way. Paul asks the assembly at Corinth plainly, "do not ye judge them that are within?" and then tells them to put away the wicked man from among themselves (1 Corinthians 5:12-13). He goes further and rebukes them for having no man among them able to judge between his brethren (1 Corinthians 6:5). No one writing in the first century understood Matthew 7:1 as a ban.

## The Law Commands Judgment

The Torah does not treat judgment as optional:

"Ye shall do no unrighteousness in judgment... but in righteousness shalt thou judge thy neighbour" (Leviticus 19:15).

"Thou shalt in any wise rebuke thy neighbour, and not suffer sin upon him" (Leviticus 19:17).

"Hear the causes between your brethren, and judge righteously... ye shall not respect persons in judgment" (Deuteronomy 1:16-17).

"Judges and officers shalt thou make thee in all thy gates... and they shall judge the people with just judgment" (Deuteronomy 16:18).

And the same speaker settled the matter elsewhere in one line: "Judge not according to the appearance, but judge righteous judgment" (John 7:24). Matthew 7:1 and John 7:24 are not in tension. The second is the first, stated positively.

## Where the Pulpit Buried It

Judge not has become the one commandment the modern assembly enforces. It is produced whenever conduct is named, and it functions as a gag rather than a scale. The result is a congregation that cannot correct anything, which is the opposite of what Leviticus 19:17 requires of a brother.

The sequence is worth tracing, because it did not happen by accident. Remove the Law and the standard for judgment is gone. Remove judgment and no one is left who can say a standard was broken. What remains is a room full of people who cannot name a single thing as wrong, holding the one verse that makes it feel like obedience.
