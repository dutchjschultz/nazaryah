## What Came West

Stretch bracelets of wooden or stone beads, often with a small Buddha head, a lotus, or a tassel, are sold in malls, gas stations, and online shops across America. They are marketed for "balance," "calm," "protection," or "good luck." Most who wear them see a fashion piece.

The bracelet is a *mala*, a string of prayer beads used in both Hinduism and Buddhism. A full mala has 108 beads; wrist malas are shorter, often 18, 21, or 27 beads, which divide into 108. The beads are used to count chants. A Hindu devotee passes one bead through his fingers for each repetition of a god's name or a mantra; a Tibetan Buddhist counts repetitions of *Om mani padme hum*. Some malas are made of rudraksha seeds, which Hindu tradition calls the tears of the god Shiva.

## What the Beads Are For

A mala exists to count repetition. The more times the name or mantra is said, the more merit is gained. That is its purpose, whether the wearer knows it or not.

## What Scripture Says

Yahushua spoke directly to the practice: "use not vain repetitions, as the heathen do: for they think that they shall be heard for their much speaking" (Matthew 6:7). The prophets of Baal on Mount Carmel did the same thing, calling on Baal "from morning even until noon" with no answer (1 Kings 18:26). Prayer in Scripture is heard because of the One who hears it, not because of the count.

Yahuah also chose what goes on His people's hand. Of His commandments Moses said, "thou shalt bind them for a sign upon thine hand, and they shall be as frontlets between thine eyes" (Deuteronomy 6:8). The sign on the believer's hand is the Law of Yahuah, kept by his hands.

## Jacob's Answer

When Jacob brought his household back to Bethel, he told them to "Put away the strange gods that are among you." They handed over their idols "and all their earrings which were in their ears," and Jacob buried them "under the oak which was by Shechem" (Genesis 35:2-4). The jewelry went into the ground with the gods it belonged to. Jacob did not keep it as decoration.

## The Verdict

A mala is a counting tool for vain repetition, and a Buddha on the wrist is an image of another religion's founder worn on the body. Calling it fashion does not change what it counts. It belongs under the oak.

Revelation shows that the hand and the forehead are claimed by one side or the other. The beast gives a mark "in their right hand, or in their foreheads" (Revelation 13:16); the Lamb's company has "his Father's name written in their foreheads" (Revelation 14:1).
