Still the same night, the twenty-fourth day of the eleventh month in the second year of Darius (1:7), early in 519 BC. Every vision so far has been about one house: the house Yahuah promised to build in Jerusalem (1:16). The horns that scattered His people were cast out. The city was measured, with Yahuah as its wall of fire. The high priest was stripped of his filthy garments. The flying scroll went out against the thief and the liar. Now the seventh vision shows where the wickedness goes when it is removed. It does not simply vanish. It is carried off to build a house of its own.

## The Vision

The angel tells Zechariah to look at what is going out. It is an ephah, a measuring basket, and the angel says, “This is their resemblance through all the earth.” A round lid of lead is lifted, and a woman is sitting inside the basket. The angel says, “This is wickedness,” throws her back down into it, and slams the lead weight over its mouth. Then two women come out with the wind in their wings, wings like a stork’s, and they lift the basket up between the earth and the sky. Zechariah asks where they are taking it. The answer: “To build it an house in the land of Shinar: and it shall be established, and set there upon her own base” (5:11).

## The Ephah

An ephah was the standard measure for grain, a basket or bin used every day in the market. Every sale of barley or wheat passed through one. That is why the Law guarded it so closely: “Just balances, just weights, a just ephah, and a just hin, shall ye have” (Leviticus 19:36). Moses warned, “Thou shalt not have in thine house divers measures, a great and a small. But thou shalt have a perfect and just weight, a perfect and just measure shalt thou have” (Deuteronomy 25:14-15).

The prophets caught Israel cheating with it anyway. Amos heard the merchants waiting impatiently for the Sabbath to end so they could get back to “making the ephah small, and the shekel great, and falsifying the balances by deceit” (Amos 8:5). A small ephah meant the buyer got less grain. A heavy shekel meant he paid more silver. Micah asked, “Are there yet the treasures of wickedness in the house of the wicked, and the scant measure that is abominable?” (Micah 6:10). Wickedness had a favorite hiding place, and it was the measuring basket.

So when the angel says the ephah is “their resemblance through all the earth” (5:6), he is saying this is what they look like. Strip away the religion, and the people are a crooked measure. The basket is the picture of a whole way of life built on buying and selling, where the scale is tilted and nobody admits it.

## The Woman Called Wickedness

Inside the basket sits a woman, and the angel names her plainly: “This is wickedness” (5:8). Scripture often pictures a whole way of life as a woman. Proverbs sets two women side by side calling to the simple, Wisdom and the foolish woman (Proverbs 9:1-6, 13-18). The prophets call faithless Jerusalem a harlot (Ezekiel 16:15). Here the woman is the sum of everything the scroll in the last vision cursed: theft, false oaths, cheating, all sitting together in one basket.

Notice what she does when the lid comes up. The angel has to throw her back in. Wickedness does not want to be contained. Given the chance, it climbs out and goes back to work.

## The Talent of Lead

The lid is “a talent of lead” (5:7), and the angel casts “the weight of lead upon the mouth thereof” (5:8). A talent was the heaviest weight Israel used, around seventy-five pounds. Lead is the dead weight of Scripture. When Pharaoh’s army went into the sea, “they sank as lead in the mighty waters” (Exodus 15:10). The weight that sealed the basket is a weight of judgment. Wickedness is not reformed or let go. It is shut in and carried away.

## Wings Like a Stork

Two women come to carry the basket, “and the wind was in their wings; for they had wings like the wings of a stork” (5:9). A stork has a huge wingspan and carries a heavy load over long distances, which suits a journey from Judah to Babylon. But it is also one of the birds the Law named unclean: “the stork, the heron after her kind” (Leviticus 11:19). An unclean bird is the right carrier for an unclean cargo. Wickedness is not sent off by angels in glory. It is carried home by its own kind.

And the wind is in their wings. The Hebrew word for wind is also the word for spirit, and it is the word Zechariah uses for the four spirits of the heavens that drive the chariots in the last vision (6:5). Even the removal of wickedness is under Yahuah’s direction. He is clearing His land, and He decides where the basket goes.

## The Land of Shinar

The basket is going to Shinar. The first time Scripture names Shinar, it is the land where Nimrod’s kingdom began, “Babel, and Erech, and Accad, and Calneh, in the land of Shinar” (Genesis 10:10). It is where men said, “let us build us a city and a tower … and let us make us a name” (Genesis 11:2-4). Shinar is Babylon’s home ground.

It is also where the temple vessels went. When Nebuchadnezzar took Jerusalem, he carried the vessels of Yahuah’s house “into the land of Shinar to the house of his god” (Daniel 1:2). Babylon stole the furniture of Yahuah’s house and put it in a house of its own. Now, in Zechariah’s vision, the traffic is reversed. Yahuah’s house is being rebuilt in Jerusalem, and wickedness is being shipped back to the land that taught it.

## A House on Its Own Base

The angel says the basket is going to Shinar “to build it an house … and it shall be established, and set there upon her own base” (5:11). That is the language of a temple. A house built for her, set on a pedestal like an idol on its stand. Wickedness gets a house and a base of its own.

That is the heart of the vision, and it ties the whole night together. There are two houses. In Jerusalem, Yahuah is building His house, with a cleansed priest and a city measured for His glory. In Shinar, wickedness is getting a house too, a counterfeit temple with its own base. The two cannot stand in the same place. Before Yahuah’s house could be finished, the woman in the basket had to leave the land.

## Why This Vision, and Why Now

The men rebuilding the temple had a real fear. They had come home, but the old sins had come home with them. The land was full of people who had cheated, sworn falsely, and lived by a crooked measure. How could Yahuah dwell among people like that? Visions four, six, and seven are the answer, in order. First the priest is cleansed, and Yahuah promises to remove the iniquity of the land “in one day” (3:9). Then the curse goes out against the thief and the liar (5:3-4). Now wickedness itself is boxed up and carried out of the country. Yahuah does not build His house in a land still full of the old life. He clears the ground first.

## Bringing It Home

Babylon still has a house, and its base is still commerce. It is the world where everything has a price and the scale is always tilted a little toward the seller. And the pulpit has not escaped it. Churches sell blessings by the “seed offering,” preachers promise wealth as proof of faith, and holy days have been turned into the biggest shopping seasons of the year. Protestant and Catholic alike have built a house where religion and money sit in the same basket.

The vision asks every believer two plain questions. First, is there an ephah in your own house that is a little too small? The Law never stopped caring about honest measures, and a believer who cheats in business is building wickedness a house in his own home. Second, which house are you living in? Yahuah is building His house in His people (1 Corinthians 3:16), and wickedness is building its house in Babylon. You cannot keep a room in both.

## Where It Lands

Revelation finishes the vision. The woman comes out of the basket and is seen sitting on a scarlet beast, dressed in purple and scarlet and gold, with a name on her forehead: “MYSTERY, BABYLON THE GREAT” (Revelation 17:3-5). The merchants of the earth have grown rich through her (18:3), and their cargo runs from gold and precious stones down to “slaves, and souls of men” (18:11-13). The voice from heaven calls, “Come out of her, my people, that ye be not partakers of her sins” (18:4). And then her house falls: “Thus with violence shall that great city Babylon be thrown down, and shall be found no more at all” (18:21). The house in Shinar was set on its own base, but it was never permanent. The Nazaryah study [*Two Babylons*](/blog/two-babylons) traces the ancient city and the last one side by side.
