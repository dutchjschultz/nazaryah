## The Word Underneath

Lamentations is the funeral of Jerusalem, burned by Babylon in 586 BC, and through it Yahuah keeps the word He gave in the Law: the curse for refusing it and the road home for turning back (Deuteronomy 28:15; 30:1-3). The name of the book comes from a Hebrew word that carries far more than its English translation does.

The word is *qinah*. In English it comes out as “lamentation,” which sounds like any sad speech. In Hebrew it is something specific: a funeral song, a dirge sung over the dead. The verb that goes with it means to chant that song. When David mourned Saul, the Hebrew says he *dirged this dirge* (2 Samuel 1:17). The English says he “lamented with this lamentation,” and the music drops out.

A *qinah* had a sound people knew on hearing it. Each line splits into two halves, and the second half is shorter than the first, usually three beats and then two. Lamentations opens that way: “How doth the city sit solitary,” a full half, then “that was full of people,” a short one (1:1). Some call this the limping rhythm, because the line walks like a man with a limp: a full step, then a short one, like a mourner whose step catches. A *qinah* also usually opens with a cry of “How!”, in Hebrew *eikh* or *eikhah*. And it tells the story of a fall: what once was, and what it has become.

Once a reader knows those three marks, the long-then-short line, the cry of “How!”, and the story of a fall, funeral songs start turning up in places where nobody expects them.

## Where the Word Shows Up

The noun *qinah* appears about eighteen times in the Old Testament, and more than half of them are in Ezekiel. Each place below is a funeral song, whether the English makes that plain or not.

- **Ezekiel** — the scroll written with lamentations (2:10); the princes of Judah as a lioness and a vine (19:1, 14); the city of Tyre (26:17); Tyre as a sinking ship (27:2, 32); the king of Tyre (28:12); Pharaoh king of Egypt (32:2, 16).
- **David** over Saul and Jonathan (2 Samuel 1:17).
- **Jeremiah’s** laments over King Josiah (2 Chronicles 35:25).
- **Jeremiah** over the land and its people (Jeremiah 7:29; 9:10, 20).
- **Amos** over Israel (Amos 5:1), and the warning that their songs would turn into lamentation (8:10).

The Ezekiel passages read differently once each one is known to be a funeral song over a living king or nation.

## The Cry Everybody Knows

The best-known funeral song in the Bible is David’s, over Saul and Jonathan: “How are the mighty fallen!” (2 Samuel 1:19). It repeats three times (1:19, 25, 27). Most people know the line without ever hearing that it is a dirge, the opening cry and the fall of the mighty together in four words.

The book of Lamentations opens the same way: “How doth the city sit solitary” (1:1). Chapters 2 and 4 open on that same word (2:1; 4:1). Before the first sentence ends, every Hebrew listener knew they were at a funeral.

## A Funeral at the Birthday Party

The prophets took this form and did something startling with it. They sang the funeral song over people who were still alive.

Israel in the days of Amos was rich, busy, and sure of itself. Amos stood up and announced, “Hear ye this word which I take up against you, even a lamentation” (Amos 5:1), and then sang, “The virgin of Israel is fallen; she shall no more rise” (5:2). Israel was not fallen. Israel was having a fine time. It was like standing up at a man’s birthday party and reading his obituary.

That was the point. A funeral song over the living is a last warning. The nation heard its own dirge while there was still time to turn.

## The Obituary Read as a Biography

Ezekiel is told, “Son of man, take up a lamentation upon the [king of Tyrus](/blog/thou-art-a-man-and-not-el)” (Ezekiel 28:12). The word is *qinah*. What follows is a funeral song over the king of a real city on the coast north of Israel, a man made rich by trade (28:4-5, 16).

Read in English, the chapter is preached as the life story of Satan: a covering cherub in Eden, perfect until iniquity was found in him (28:13-15). Read as what it is, it is an obituary. It sings of a man’s glory and his fall in the high, picture-filled language a funeral song uses, the same way David sang of Saul as swifter than eagles and stronger than lions (2 Samuel 1:23).

The chapter says plainly who it is about. “Thou art a man, and not El” (28:2). “Thou shalt die the deaths of the uncircumcised by the hand of strangers” (28:10). Angels are not circumcised or uncircumcised, and they are not killed by foreign armies. Dirges are sung over the dead, and Ezekiel’s song is sung over a dead king.

## The Cousin in Isaiah

[Isaiah 14](/blog/is-this-the-man) is the other passage turned into the devil’s life story, and it uses a cousin word. Israel is told to “take up this proverb against the king of Babylon” (Isaiah 14:4). The word is *mashal*, a saying, a parable, a taunt. It is not a funeral song for a friend. It is a mocking song over an enemy.

Still, it borrows the funeral song’s clothes. It opens with the dirge cry: “How hath the oppressor ceased!” (14:4). Then comes the most famous line of all: “How art thou fallen from heaven, O Lucifer, son of the morning!” (14:12).

In Hebrew, “How art thou fallen” is *eikh naphalta*. David’s line, “How are the mighty fallen,” is *eikh naphlu*. It is the same cry and the same verb. The verse the church made into an angel’s fall from heaven opens exactly like David’s funeral song over a dead king. Isaiah’s song even follows the king into the grave, where worms cover him and the onlookers ask, “Is this the man that made the earth to tremble?” (14:11, 16).

## What the English Hides

An English reader meets the word “lamentation” and takes it for a sad passage. A Hebrew listener heard the opening “How!” and the long-then-short line, and knew at once: this is a funeral, and somebody is being buried. That one difference decides how two of the most misused chapters in the Bible are read. Ezekiel 28 and Isaiah 14 are funeral songs over kings, the kind of song David sang over Saul, and not the history of a fallen angel.

## Where It Lands

Revelation 18 never uses a word for funeral song, but it is written like one. The kings, merchants, and sailors who grew rich from Babylon weep and wail over her, stand far off, and throw dust on their heads (Revelation 18:9, 15, 19), the same things mourners did at a funeral. Their cry, “Alas, alas, that great city” (18:10, 16, 19), is in Greek *ouai, ouai*, a cry of woe. And their question, “What city is like unto this great city?” (18:18), is taken from Ezekiel’s lamentation over Tyre: “What city is like Tyrus?” (Ezekiel 27:32). John took Ezekiel’s funeral song and sang it over Babylon.

After that, the funeral songs stop. Yahuah wipes away all tears, “and there shall be no more death, neither sorrow, nor crying” (Revelation 21:4). The *qinah* is retired for good.
