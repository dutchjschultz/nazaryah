## The Birth of Zionism

*Where It Started, and the Paths Between Germany and Palestine*

This is a short, fast walk through where the Zionist movement began, who led it, and the people and ideas that moved back and forth between Germany and Palestine in the years before the Second World War. The record is laid out in order. The reader can weigh it.

### How It Started

- *1881–1882.* Violent attacks on Jews break out across the Russian Empire. Groups called the "Lovers of Zion" form and begin sending settlers to Palestine, which was then ruled by the Ottoman Turks.
- *1890.* A writer in Vienna named Nathan Birnbaum coins the word "Zionism."
- *1896.* Theodor Herzl, a newspaper writer living in Vienna, publishes a short book called *The Jewish State*. He argues that Jews will never be accepted in Europe and need a land of their own.
- *1897.* Herzl holds the First Zionist Congress in Basel, Switzerland. Its goal is a homeland for the Jewish people in Palestine. The early meetings were held mostly in German.

### The Grün Family

Avigdor Grün was a leader of the Lovers of Zion in Płońsk, a town in Poland under Russian rule. His son David Grün was born there in 1886 and grew up in the movement. David moved to Palestine in 1906 and changed his name to the Hebrew name Ben-Gurion. In 1948 he became the first Prime Minister of Israel. The name Grün is German in form, as many Jewish family names in Eastern Europe are.

### Weizmann, Balfour, Rothschild, and Churchill

Chaim Weizmann was a Jewish chemist working in England. During the First World War he found a new way to make acetone, a chemical Britain badly needed to make gunpowder for the war. His work put him in close contact with Britain’s top leaders. On November 2, 1917, Britain’s Foreign Secretary, Arthur Balfour, wrote a letter promising British support for "a national home for the Jewish people" in Palestine. The letter was addressed to Lord Walter Rothschild, of the Rothschild banking family. It is known as the Balfour Declaration.

Winston Churchill was a strong supporter of the Zionist cause and helped shape Britain’s policy in Palestine in the early 1920s. Weizmann later became the first President of Israel in 1949.

### Germans in the Holy Land

Long before any of this, Germans were already living in Palestine. In 1861 a German preacher named Christoph Hoffmann started a revival group called the Templers. They believed God’s people should gather in the Holy Land to get ready for Messiah’s return. Starting in 1869 they built colonies at Haifa, Jaffa, Sarona, and Jerusalem, and became some of the best farmers and builders in the land.

When Hitler came to power in 1933, the Nazi Party set up branches among the Templers. A man named Karl Ruf of Haifa started the first chapters at Sarona and Jaffa in March 1933. By 1938 about one Templer in six was a party member, and swastika flags flew over German villages a short walk from Tel Aviv. When the war began, the British locked the Templers up. Hundreds were shipped to Australia, and about a thousand were traded back to Germany in exchange for about 550 Jews held under German rule. Some Templers joined the German army.

### The Mufti of Jerusalem

Haj Amin al-Husseini was the top Muslim leader in Jerusalem. He led the Arab fight against Jewish settlement and the Arab revolt of 1936 to 1939. Forced out by the British, he made his way to Berlin and met Hitler on November 28, 1941. He spent the war working for the Nazis, making radio broadcasts to the Arab world and recruiting Muslim soldiers for the SS.

### Back and Forth: Zionists and Nazis

- *Spring 1933.* A Nazi SS officer, Leopold von Mildenstein, spends six months touring Palestine together with a German Zionist leader, Kurt Tuchler, and their wives. In 1934 Joseph Goebbels’s Nazi newspaper prints Mildenstein’s twelve-part report, "A Nazi Travels to Palestine," praising the Jewish settlements. The paper makes a medal to mark it, with a swastika on one side and a Star of David on the other.
- *August 1933.* German Zionists and the Nazi government sign the Haavara, or "Transfer," Agreement. German Jews moving to Palestine can take part of their money with them, in the form of German goods sold in Palestine. About 60,000 German Jews leave this way before 1939. The deal also breaks the worldwide Jewish boycott of German goods.
- *Mid-1930s.* Mildenstein runs the SS office on Jewish affairs. One of the men under him is Adolf Eichmann. Eichmann later said he read Herzl’s *The Jewish State* there and studied Zionist writings closely.
- *1937.* Feivel Polkes, an agent of the Haganah, the Jewish underground army in Palestine, travels to Berlin and meets Eichmann. That fall, Eichmann and another SS man sail to Palestine. The British turn them away at Haifa, and they meet Polkes in Cairo instead.
- *January 1941.* A small Jewish fighting group in Palestine called Lehi, led by Avraham Stern, sends a man to a German official in Beirut. He offers to fight alongside Germany against Britain in return for letting Jews leave Europe. Germany never answers.

In those years both sides wanted the same thing to happen. The Nazis wanted Jews out of Germany. The Zionists wanted Jews to move to Palestine. In 1941 the Nazi plan changed from driving Jews out to killing them, and about six million Jews were murdered in the years that followed.

### Kennedy and Dimona

In 1963 President Kennedy sent Ben-Gurion a series of strong letters demanding that Israel open its new nuclear plant at Dimona to regular American inspection. Ben-Gurion resigned as Prime Minister that June. Kennedy was shot in Dallas that November.

### What Scripture Says About the Land

Yahuah’s promises were made "to Abraham and his seed," and Paul says that seed is one person — Messiah (Galatians 3:16). Everyone who belongs to Messiah is Abraham’s seed and an heir of the promise (Galatians 3:29). Abraham himself was not looking for a nation drawn up by congresses and letters. He "looked for a city which hath foundations, whose builder and maker is" Yahuah (Hebrews 11:10). John saw that city, the new Jerusalem, coming down out of heaven (Revelation 21:2).

## Three Wars and the Land

*How Palestine Changed Hands, One War at a Time*

For four hundred years, one small strip of land changed very little. Then, in about thirty years, it changed completely. Each big change came during or right after a war. This paper lays out what happened, in order, with the dates. The reader can line them up and decide what they show.

### Before the Wars

From 1517 on, Palestine was ruled by the Ottoman Turks, a Muslim empire based in Istanbul. Most of the people living there were Arab farmers and townspeople, Muslim and Christian, with a small Jewish community. In the 1880s the first Zionist settlers began to arrive. In 1897 Theodor Herzl held the first Zionist meeting in Switzerland and set the goal: a homeland for the Jewish people in Palestine. There was one problem. The land belonged to the Ottoman Empire, and the Ottomans would not give it up.

### The First War: The Land Is Opened

- *June 1914.* An heir to the Austrian throne is shot in Sarajevo. Within weeks, the great powers of Europe are at war.
- *November 1914.* The Ottoman Empire joins the war on Germany’s side. Now Britain has a reason to go after the lands the Ottomans hold, including Palestine.
- *1915–1916.* Britain makes a promise to the Arabs. In letters to Sharif Hussein of Mecca, Britain says that if the Arabs rise up against the Turks, they will get their own independent lands. The Arabs rise up in June 1916.
- *May 1916.* At the same time, Britain and France secretly sign the Sykes-Picot Agreement. They draw lines on a map and split the Ottoman Middle East between themselves. The Arabs are not told.
- *November 2, 1917.* Britain makes a third promise. The Balfour Declaration, addressed to Lord Rothschild, promises "a national home for the Jewish people" in Palestine.
- *December 11, 1917.* British General Allenby walks into Jerusalem. After four hundred years, Turkish rule over the Holy Land is over.
- *1920–1922.* After the war, the world’s new League of Nations hands Palestine to Britain to govern. The Balfour promise is written right into the papers that give Britain control.

**Three promises were made about the same land: one to the Arabs, one to France, and one to the Zionists.**

### Why Britain Wanted the Land

Theodor Herzl was not British. He was from Vienna, and he died in 1904. Yet it was Britain that opened the door. Britain had four reasons:

- *The canal.* The Suez Canal was Britain’s road to India, its richest colony. Palestine sits right next to it.
- *Keeping France out.* Britain did not want France as a neighbor to the canal.
- *Oil.* The British Navy had just switched from coal to oil, and the oil was in Iraq. Haifa, in Palestine, later became the end of Britain’s oil pipeline.
- *Winning the war.* British leaders hoped the Balfour promise would win Jewish support in America and Russia.

There was one more reason, and it came from church. Prime Minister Lloyd George and Lord Balfour were both raised on a teaching that the Jews must return to Palestine before Messiah comes. Lloyd George once said he knew the towns of Palestine better than the battles of the war. British preachers had spread that teaching all through the 1800s, and it is still preached from many American pulpits today.

### Between the Wars: The Land Is Filled

With Britain in charge, the door to Palestine opened. In 1922 there were about 84,000 Jews in the land, a little over one person in ten. By 1939 there were around 450,000, nearly one in three.

Land was bought as well. The Jewish National Fund, set up in 1901, bought large pieces of land, often from rich landlords who lived far away in Beirut or Damascus. The Arab families who had farmed that land for generations were often made to leave, because they rented it and did not own it.

- *1933.* Hitler comes to power. The Transfer Agreement between German Zionists and the Nazi government helps about 60,000 German Jews move to Palestine over the next six years.
- *1936–1939.* The Arabs of Palestine rise up against British rule and Jewish immigration. Britain crushes the revolt.
- *1937.* A British commission, the Peel Commission, says for the first time that the land should be cut in two: one part Jewish, one part Arab.
- *1939.* Just before the Second World War, Britain tries to slow things down and limits Jewish immigration to 75,000 over five years.

### The Second War: The State Is Born

The Second World War began in September 1939. By its end in 1945, about six million Jews had been murdered in Europe. Hundreds of thousands of Jewish survivors were left in camps across Europe with no home to go back to. Ships packed with refugees tried to reach Palestine. The most famous, the *Exodus*, was turned back by the British in July 1947 while the world watched.

- *February 1947.* Britain, worn out by the war, hands the Palestine question to the new United Nations.
- *November 29, 1947.* The UN votes to split Palestine in two. The Jewish state is given about 56 percent of the land. At that time Jews were about one-third of the people and owned roughly 7 percent of the land.
- *May 14, 1948.* David Ben-Gurion declares the State of Israel. President Truman recognizes it eleven minutes later. The next day, the armies of the neighboring Arab countries attack.
- *1948–1949.* By the end of the fighting, about 700,000 Palestinian Arabs have fled or been driven from their homes, and more than 400 Arab villages are left empty. The Palestinians call it the *Nakba*, "the catastrophe." They are not allowed to return.

**What the First War opened and the years between filled, the Second War turned into a state.**

### The Wars of Today

The fighting did not end in 1948. In the Six-Day War of 1967, Israel took the rest of the land: the West Bank, Gaza, and East Jerusalem, along with the Golan Heights from Syria and the Sinai from Egypt. Settlements were built on the newly taken land, and they have kept growing ever since.

After the Hamas attack on October 7, 2023, Israel went to war in Gaza, and the war spread to Lebanon, Syria, Yemen, and finally Iran, with the United States joining the strikes on Iran. Many people around the world now call it the beginning of a Third World War.

### Lining Up the Dates

- Before the First World War, the land was locked under Ottoman rule.
- The First World War ended Ottoman rule and put Palestine in British hands, with the Balfour promise attached.
- Between the wars, the land was bought and settled under British protection.
- The Second World War drove the Jews of Europe out and led straight to the UN vote and the new state.
- The wars of today are being fought over the same land and its neighbors.

Each war moved the land one more step in the same direction. Whether that was planned, or whether powerful men simply used each war as it came, is for the reader to judge.

### What Scripture Says

Yahuah gave the promise of the land to Abraham and his seed, and Paul says that seed is one person — Messiah (Galatians 3:16). Those who belong to Messiah are Abraham’s seed and heirs of the promise (Galatians 3:29). Abraham never got his inheritance by treaty or by war. He lived in the land as a stranger, living in tents, because he was looking for "a city which hath foundations, whose builder and maker is" Yahuah (Hebrews 11:9-10).

John saw that city at the end. It does not come from a vote in New York or a letter from London. It comes "down from Yahuah out of heaven" (Revelation 21:2).
