## The Book in a Nutshell

In 586 BC, Babylon burned Jerusalem and the temple. Lamentations was written in the ashes. The book names no writer.

It is five chapters long, and each chapter is a poem spoken by a different voice. Each voice stands for someone else in the Bible, and each one explains why the city fell in the language of the Law.

Moses had warned Israel long before. He set two roads in front of them, blessing for keeping Yahuah’s commandments and curses for breaking them (Deuteronomy 28), and told them to choose life (Deuteronomy 30:19). They chose the other road. Lamentations is what that road looked like at the end. It also holds on to the other half of Moses’ words: when the people turn back, Yahuah turns them home (Deuteronomy 30:1-3).

Here are the five voices:

- **Chapter 1** — the city, speaking as a wife who was sent away
- **Chapter 2** — a witness standing in the ruins
- **Chapter 3** — one man in a pit
- **Chapter 4** — someone comparing what was to what is
- **Chapter 5** — the whole people, praying together

## Voice One — Chapter 1: The Wife Sent Away

**Who is talking.** Jerusalem herself, saying “I.”

**Who she stands for.** She sits “as a widow” (1:1), but her husband is not dead. Yahuah was her husband (Isaiah 54:5), and He sent her away because she was unfaithful to Him (Jeremiah 3:8). She is the wife who left. Five times the chapter says she has no one to comfort her (1:2, 9, 16, 17, 21).

Isaiah gives the answer, said twice for emphasis: “Comfort ye, comfort ye my people” (Isaiah 40:1). That is the same chapter that announces John the Baptist, the voice in the wilderness (Isaiah 40:3; Matthew 3:3). When old Simeon held the baby Yahushua, he had been waiting his whole life for “the consolation of Israel,” and he said, “mine eyes have seen thy salvation” (Luke 2:25-30). The wife with no comforter was answered by Messiah.

**Why it happened, in Law terms.** She says it herself: “Yahuah is righteous; for I have rebelled against his commandment” (1:18). Then everything Moses warned about comes true:

- She finds no rest among the nations (1:3), as Moses said (Deuteronomy 28:65).
- Her enemies become the head and she the tail (1:5; Deuteronomy 28:44).
- A yoke is put on her neck (1:14; Deuteronomy 28:48).
- Her sons and daughters are taken captive (1:18; Deuteronomy 28:41).

## Voice Two — Chapter 2: The Witness

**Who is talking.** Someone standing in the burned streets, telling what he sees.

**Who he stands for.** He asks the city, “What thing shall I take to witness for thee?” (2:13). The word he uses means a witness in court. It is the same word Moses used when he called witnesses against Israel: “I call heaven and earth to record this day against you, that I have set before you life and death, blessing and cursing” (Deuteronomy 30:19). The witnesses Moses called have now spoken.

He stands for the true watchman, the one Yahuah sets to warn His people (Ezekiel 33:7). Jerusalem’s own prophets told her everything was fine and never named her sin (2:14). This voice names it. Yahushua stood over the rebuilt temple centuries later and gave the same warning: “There shall not be left here one stone upon another” (Matthew 24:2).

**Why it happened, in Law terms.** The witness says it plainly: Yahuah “hath fulfilled his word that he had commanded in the days of old” (2:17). Babylon was only the tool. The sentence came from the Law.

- The feasts and Sabbaths are forgotten, and “the law is no more” (2:6, 9).
- People passing by clap and shake their heads at her (2:15). Moses said Israel would become a byword among the nations (Deuteronomy 28:37).
- In the siege, mothers eat their own children (2:20). Moses wrote that warning first (Deuteronomy 28:53).

## Voice Three — Chapter 3: The Man in the Pit

**Who is talking.** One man: “I am the man that hath seen affliction” (3:1).

**Who he stands for.** The Hebrew word for “man” here is *geber*, a strong man. Yahuah uses the same word in Zechariah: “smite the shepherd,” the man (*geber*) “that is my fellow” (Zechariah 13:7). Yahushua said that verse was about Himself the night He was arrested (Matthew 26:31).

The man in Lamentations is put in a dark place like the dead (3:6), shut in a pit, with a stone laid over him (3:53). He calls out, and Yahuah hears him, comes near, and says, “Fear not” (3:55-57). Messiah was laid in a tomb cut in rock, with a stone rolled over the door (Matthew 27:60), and He cried out to the One who could save Him from death, “and was heard” (Hebrews 5:7). The man in the pit is a picture of Messiah.

**Why it happened, in Law terms.** In the middle of the pit the man turns: “It is of Yahuah’s mercies that we are not consumed… They are new every morning: great is thy faithfulness” (3:22-23). Then he tells the people to do what the Law asked: “Let us search and try our ways, and turn again to Yahuah” (3:40). That is the turning Moses promised Yahuah would answer (Leviticus 26:40-42; Deuteronomy 30:1-3).

## Voice Four — Chapter 4: Then and Now

**Who is talking.** Someone looking at the ruins and remembering how things used to be. Gold has gone dull (4:1). Children beg for bread (4:4).

**Who he stands for.** His saddest picture is the Nazarites. They were once “purer than snow,” and now they are “blacker than a coal,” so dark that no one recognizes them in the street (4:7-8). A Nazarite was someone who made a vow “to separate themselves unto Yahuah” (Numbers 6:2). The most famous Nazarite, Samson, broke his vow and lost his strength, and “he wist not that Yahuah was departed from him” (Judges 16:20). He didn’t even notice.

That is what happened to the whole people. The ones who were supposed to be set apart stopped being set apart, and the light left without them noticing. The call to Messiah’s people today is the same: “come out from among them, and be ye separate” (2 Corinthians 6:17).

**Why it happened, in Law terms.** The voice blames the leaders: “the sins of her prophets, and the iniquities of her priests, that have shed the blood of the just” (4:13). Yahushua made the same charge against the leaders of His day (Matthew 23:35). And again, Moses had written it first:

- An enemy that would not respect the old or pity the young (4:16; Deuteronomy 28:50).
- An enemy swift as an eagle (4:19; Deuteronomy 28:49).
- People wandering like the blind (4:14; Deuteronomy 28:29).
- Their king carried off to a foreign nation (4:20; Deuteronomy 28:36).

The chapter ends with good news: “The punishment of thine iniquity is accomplished” (4:22). The sentence is served.

## Voice Five — Chapter 5: The People Praying

**Who is talking.** Everyone together, saying “we”: “Remember, O Yahuah, what is come upon us” (5:1).

**Who they stand for.** The assembly, Yahuah’s people praying as one, the way the disciples later “continued with one accord in prayer” (Acts 1:14). Their prayer ends: “Turn thou us unto thee, O Yahuah, and we shall be turned; renew our days” (5:21).

Those exact words show up in Jeremiah, where Ephraim prays, “turn thou me, and I shall be turned” (Jeremiah 31:18). A few verses later, Yahuah answers with the new covenant: “I will put my law in their inward parts, and write it in their hearts” (Jeremiah 31:31-33). The people asked to be made new, and Yahuah’s answer was His Law written on their hearts.

Earlier in the book their prayers could not get through (3:44). Now they go through Messiah, the “one mediator between Elohim and men” (1 Timothy 2:5).

**Why it happened, in Law terms.** They own it: “woe unto us, that we have sinned!” (5:16). And they live what Moses described:

- Their homes belong to strangers (5:2; Deuteronomy 28:30).
- Servants rule over them (5:8; Deuteronomy 28:43).
- Their elders are not honored (5:12; Deuteronomy 28:50).

## The Whole Book in Five Sentences

The wife who left Yahuah sits alone with no one to comfort her, and admits she broke His commandment.

A witness says everything Moses warned about has come true.

A man in a pit, a picture of Messiah, calls on Yahuah and is heard.

The ones who were set apart have gone dark, and the sentence is declared served.

The whole people pray to be turned back and made new, and Yahuah answers with His Law written on their hearts.

## Where It Lands

Lamentations opens with Jerusalem sitting “as a widow” (1:1). In Revelation, Babylon, the city that burned Jerusalem, brags, “I sit a queen, and am no widow” (Revelation 18:7), and falls in one hour.

And the wife who was sent away comes home. The holy city comes down “prepared as a bride adorned for her husband” (Revelation 21:2), and the one who had no comforter is comforted by Elohim Himself, who “shall wipe away all tears from their eyes” (Revelation 21:4).
