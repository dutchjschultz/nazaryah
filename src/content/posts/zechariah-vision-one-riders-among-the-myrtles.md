The night of the eight visions has a date: the twenty-fourth day of the eleventh month, Sebat, in the second year of Darius (1:7), early in 519 BC. The captives have been home from Babylon for almost twenty years. The altar is standing. The temple is not. Five months earlier Haggai had stood up and asked the people a hard question: “Is it time for you, O ye, to dwell in your cieled houses, and this house lie waste?” (Haggai 1:4). The first vision is Yahuah’s answer to that same problem.

## The Vision

Zechariah sees a man riding a red horse, standing among myrtle trees in a low hollow, with red, speckled, and white horses behind him. The riders report to the angel of Yahuah that they have walked through the whole earth, and all of it sits still and at rest. The angel of Yahuah then asks Yahuah of hosts how long He will withhold mercy from Jerusalem after seventy years. Yahuah answers with good and comforting words. He is jealous for Jerusalem and angry with the nations at ease, He has returned to Jerusalem with mercy, His house will be built in it, and He will yet comfort Zion.

## The Rider on the Red Horse

Zechariah sees a man riding a red horse. The man “stood among the myrtle trees that were in the bottom” (1:8). He is not sitting in a tree. He stands, mounted, down among the low myrtles in a hollow, and the same verse that reports his place calls him the angel of Yahuah (1:11).

A red horse appears for the first time in Scripture right here. The color itself has a long history before it. Red, *adom*, first meets the reader in the red stew Esau traded his birthright for, and the name it gave him, Edom (Genesis 25:30). Isaiah later sees One coming up from Edom with red garments and asks why. The answer: He has trodden the winepress alone, and their blood is sprinkled on His clothing, “for the day of vengeance is in mine heart” (Isaiah 63:1-4). In Scripture, red is the color of blood and of judgment.

Revelation shows what a red horse does. “There went out another horse that was red: and power was given to him that sat thereon to take peace from the earth” (Revelation 6:4). Hold that beside this vision. The report Zechariah is about to hear is that the earth is at peace. The rider leading the patrol sits on the horse whose work is to take that peace away.

## The Horses Behind Him

Behind the leader are red horses, speckled, and white (1:8). Zechariah does not stop to explain each color, and the study should not pretend he does. The word behind “speckled” is uncertain. It sits close to *soreq*, the “choicest vine” Yahuah planted in His vineyard (Isaiah 5:2), so most read it as a reddish brown, a color between red and white. Scripture does fill in the two ends. Red is blood and the sword. White is righteousness and triumph: sins made “white as snow” (Isaiah 1:18), the white horse that goes out conquering (Revelation 6:2), and the white horse of the One called Faithful and True (Revelation 19:11).

What the horses do is plain. They were sent “to walk to and fro through the earth” (1:10). That phrase belongs to Yahuah’s own eyes. “The eyes of Yahuah run to and fro throughout the whole earth, to shew himself strong in the behalf of them whose heart is perfect toward him” (2 Chronicles 16:9). Zechariah repeats it three chapters later (4:10). The nations trusted their horses (Psalm 20:7). Yahuah has horses of His own, and they see everything.

## The Myrtles in the Hollow

The myrtle, *hadas* in Hebrew, is a low, sweet-smelling shrub. It is not the cedar of kings. Esther’s Hebrew name, Hadassah, comes from it (Esther 2:7). The hollow it grows in, *metsulah*, comes from the same family of words as the deep where Pharaoh’s army sank (Exodus 15:5) and where Jonah was thrown (Jonah 2:3). Yahuah’s people are down in the low place, few and small. His rider stands there with them.

Myrtle is also a Sukkot plant. When the Law was read to the returned captives, they found it written that they should dwell in booths, and they went out and brought “myrtle branches” to build them (Nehemiah 8:14-15). Sukkot remembers Israel living in booths in the wilderness when Yahuah brought them out (Leviticus 23:42-43). It is the feast of Yahuah dwelling with His people in a temporary house.

That ties the vision to the problem in front of Zechariah. When the captives first came home, they built the altar and kept Sukkot, “but the foundation of the temple of Yahuah was not yet laid” (Ezra 3:4-6). They had the feast of booths and no house. Haggai’s great promise came on the twenty-first day of the seventh month, the last day of Sukkot: “The glory of this latter house shall be greater than of the former” (Haggai 2:1, 9). And Zechariah’s book ends with the nations going up year after year to keep Sukkot (14:16). The rider stands among the booth branches because the question of the whole vision is where Yahuah will dwell.

## The Report: The Earth at Rest

The riders come back and report: “all the earth sitteth still, and is at rest” (1:11). That sounds like good news. It is not. The nations are comfortable, and Yahuah’s house is a ruin. Haggai had already named the same disease among the people themselves: “because of mine house that is waste, and ye run every man unto his own house” (Haggai 1:9). Amos had a word for it long before: “Woe to them that are at ease in Zion” (Amos 6:1).

## Who Is Asking Whom

The angel of Yahuah asks how long Yahuah of hosts will withhold mercy from Jerusalem, “against which thou hast had indignation these threescore and ten years” (1:12), Jeremiah’s seventy years (Jeremiah 25:11; 29:10). The angel of Yahuah speaks to Yahuah of hosts and asks Him for mercy. Teachers who make this angel a second person of a godhead leave him praying to himself. The text keeps them two: the one who is sent and asks, and the One who answers.

## The Answer: Build the House

Yahuah answers with “good words and comfortable words” (1:13). He is jealous for Jerusalem and “very sore displeased with the heathen that are at ease” (1:15). Then the heart of the vision: “I am returned to Jerusalem with mercies: my house shall be built in it,” and a measuring line will be stretched over the city (1:16). He kept His word. The house was finished in the sixth year of Darius (Ezra 6:15), about seventy years after Babylon burned it.

## We Are the House Now

The building Zechariah watched go up was a pattern. Paul tells believers, “ye are the temple of Elohim” (1 Corinthians 3:16), and “your body is the temple” of the spirit within you (1 Corinthians 6:19). Hebrews says plainly, “whose house are we” (Hebrews 3:6). Peter calls believers “lively stones” being “built up a spiritual house” (1 Peter 2:5).

Entry into that house comes through the blood of the Messiah. Nothing is added to that. But a house that has been bought still has to be built, and it is built the way every house of Yahuah was built: to His pattern. That pattern is His commandments, now written on the heart (Hebrews 8:10).

So the first vision still asks Haggai’s question. A believer can live a quiet, comfortable life, his own house paneled and in good order, while the temple Yahuah actually cares about, his own body and walk, lies half built. The world will call that peace. Laodicea called it being “rich, and increased with goods,” in need of nothing (Revelation 3:17). Yahuah calls it the earth at rest, and He is not pleased with it. The riders are still going to and fro, and the eyes are still looking for hearts that are perfect toward Him.

## Where It Lands

In Revelation the measuring line comes back. John is told to “measure the temple of Elohim, and the altar, and them that worship therein” (Revelation 11:1), and the temple being measured is the worshipers. Then Sukkot reaches its end: “Behold, the tabernacle of Elohim is with men, and he will dwell with them” (Revelation 21:3). The booth becomes a city, and the house is finally finished.
