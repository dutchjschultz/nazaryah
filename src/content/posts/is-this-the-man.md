## The Claim

Ask most churchgoers where the Bible tells how Satan fell, and they will point to Isaiah 14. “How art thou fallen from heaven, O Lucifer, son of the morning!” (14:12). The story they were taught goes like this: Lucifer was heaven’s brightest angel, he said in his heart “I will be like the most High,” and he was thrown out of heaven before the world began.

That story depends on one thing: reading verse 12 by itself. Put it back in its chapter, and Isaiah tells you exactly who he is talking about.

## When and Where

This section of Isaiah opens with a heading: “The burden of Babylon, which Isaiah the son of Amoz did see” (13:1). Isaiah lived around 700 BC, when Assyria was the great power and Babylon had not yet risen. He looks ahead to Babylon’s empire and to its fall, and he even names who will bring it down: “Behold, I will stir up the Medes against them” (13:17). The Medes and Persians took Babylon in 539 BC, the same night Belshazzar saw the handwriting on the wall (Daniel 5:28-31).

Chapter 14 then sets the scene for the song. Yahuah will have mercy on Jacob and bring Israel home, and “in the day that Yahuah shall give thee rest from thy sorrow, and from thy fear, and from the hard bondage wherein thou wast made to serve” (14:3), the freed captives will sing it. The song belongs to the day Israel walks out of Babylon.

## Isaiah Names Him

Verse 4 settles the question before verse 12 is ever reached: “thou shalt take up this proverb against the king of Babylon, and say, How hath the oppressor ceased! the golden city ceased!”

A proverb, *mashal*, is a taunt song, a mocking poem sung over a fallen enemy. The subject is named: the king of Babylon. The setting is named: the golden city has stopped. Everything after verse 4 is the song the freed captives sing over him.

## A Parable, Not a Biography

Verse 4 also tells the reader what kind of writing this is. The word translated proverb, *mashal*, is the same word Scripture uses for a parable. Ezekiel is told to “speak a parable unto the house of Israel” about two eagles and a cedar (Ezekiel 17:2), and the psalmist says, “I will open my mouth in a parable” (Psalm 78:2). Both times the word is *mashal*. A *mashal* paints in pictures. Nobody reads Ezekiel’s eagles as real birds, or the talking trees of Jotham’s parable as real trees (Judges 9:8-15).

This is the key most Christians are never handed. In this song the trees sing, and the dead kings sit up on their thrones and talk. No one takes those lines literally. The heaven, the stars, the holy mountain, and the morning star belong to the same song and are the same kind of picture. The king is real, and his fall was real. The pictures are how the song tells it. The pulpit does the reverse: it reads the pictures as history and forgets the man.

## The Song, Verse by Verse

## The earth breathes again

“The whole earth is at rest, and is quiet: they break forth into singing” (14:7). Even the trees join in: since you fell, “no feller is come up against us” (14:8). Babylon’s kings cut the cedars of Lebanon for their building projects. The forests are glad he is gone.

## The dead kings greet him

Then the song goes down into the grave. *Sheol*, the grave, “is moved for thee to meet thee at thy coming,” and the dead kings of the nations rise from their thrones to say, “Art thou also become weak as we? art thou become like unto us?” (14:9-10). He has joined the other dead kings. “Thy pomp is brought down to the grave … the worm is spread under thee, and the worms cover thee” (14:11). Worms do not eat spirits. This is a body in a tomb.

## How art thou fallen

Now verse 12: “How art thou fallen from heaven, O Lucifer, son of the morning! how art thou cut down to the ground, which didst weaken the nations!” The Hebrew word behind “Lucifer” is *helel*, “shining one,” and “son of the morning” is *ben shachar*, son of the dawn. It is the morning star, the brightest light in the sky before sunrise, which disappears the moment the sun comes up. The king who outshone every other king is gone like the morning star at dawn.

Notice the last line of the verse: he is the one “which didst weaken the nations.” That is a conqueror’s record, not an angel’s. Where the name “Lucifer” came from is the subject of study three, [*The Lucifer Deception*](/blog/the-lucifer-deception).

## I will ascend

Then the song quotes his boast: “I will ascend into heaven, I will exalt my throne above the stars of El: I will sit also upon the mount of the congregation, in the sides of the north … I will be like the most High” (14:13-14).

This is how Babylon’s kings talked. Nebuchadnezzar walked on his palace roof and said, “Is not this great Babylon, that I have built … by the might of my power, and for the honour of my majesty?” (Daniel 4:30). Belshazzar lifted himself up against heaven and drank wine from the vessels of Yahuah’s house (Daniel 5:23). And look at the mountain he wanted. “The mount of the congregation” is *har mo’ed*, the mountain of the appointed meeting, and “the sides of the north” is where the psalm places Zion: “mount Zion, on the sides of the north, the city of the great King” (Psalm 48:2). Babylon’s king wanted Yahuah’s own mountain and Yahuah’s own seat.

## Is this the man?

The answer comes in verses 15-17: “Yet thou shalt be brought down to hell, to the sides of the pit. They that see thee shall narrowly look upon thee, and consider thee, saying, Is this the man that made the earth to tremble, that did shake kingdoms; That made the world as a wilderness, and destroyed the cities thereof; that opened not the house of his prisoners?”

Is this the *man*. The text says it in plain words. He shook kingdoms, emptied cities, and never released his prisoners. That is Babylon, the empire that held Judah captive for seventy years. Then he is denied even a royal burial: “thou art cast out of thy grave like an abominable branch” (14:19), and Yahuah will “cut off from Babylon the name, and remnant” (14:22).

## Falling From Heaven

The one phrase that seems to point beyond a man is “fallen from heaven.” But Scripture uses that picture for proud kings and cities again and again. Yahuah “cast down from heaven unto the earth the beauty of Israel” when Jerusalem fell (Lamentations 2:1). He told Edom, “though thou set thy nest among the stars, thence will I bring thee down” (Obadiah 4). Yahushua told the town of Capernaum, “which art exalted unto heaven, shalt be brought down to hell” (Matthew 11:23). Heaven is the height of a king’s pride. The fall is his ruin.

Some point to Yahushua’s words, “I beheld Satan as lightning fall from heaven” (Luke 10:18). Read the verse before it. The seventy had just come back saying, “even the devils are subject unto us through thy name” (Luke 10:17). Yahushua was speaking about what was happening in His own ministry, not quoting Isaiah 14, and not describing something before the world was made.

## Bringing It Home

Turn this chapter into an angel’s history, and it becomes a curiosity that warns nobody. Read it as Isaiah wrote it, and it is a mirror. The king of Babylon said in his heart, “I will be like the most High.” Every proud ruler, every proud preacher, and every proud heart has said some version of that sentence. The taunt song is what Yahuah sings over all of them in the end: “Is this the man?”

It is also a promise to Yahuah’s people. The song was written for captives to sing on the day their bondage ended (14:3). The empire that seemed to own the world ends up in a grave with worms for a blanket, and the people it held captive walk home singing.

## Where It Lands

Revelation sings the same song over the last Babylon. “Babylon the great is fallen, is fallen” (Revelation 18:2), the same cry Isaiah raised a few chapters later (Isaiah 21:9). The kings and merchants who grew rich from her stand at a distance and mourn (Revelation 18:9-11), and Yahuah’s people are called out of her first: “Come out of her, my people” (Revelation 18:4). The king of Babylon was never a fallen angel. He was the first picture of the city that falls at the end.
