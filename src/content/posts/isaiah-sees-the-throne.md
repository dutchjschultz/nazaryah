## What Yahuah Is Doing

Yahuah means to be known. Not guessed at and not argued over, but known, by every nation on the earth.

His way of doing that has never changed. He puts somebody in place to carry it, and He sends men to say so out loud.

Isaiah 6 is the day one of those men got the job, and it is the one scene in the whole book that Isaiah says he watched happen.

## When This Is Happening

This is the first of Isaiah's four stretches of time — chapters 1 through 39, roughly 740 to 690 BC. The nation is still standing. There are kings on thrones, a temple in use, and Assyria coming up the road.

Chapter 6 carries the only hard date in that whole stretch: the year King Uzziah died, about 740 BC. Every claim about when Isaiah lived and preached hangs on that one line.

The book opens with the charges against the nation first and only then shows where Isaiah got his orders. The charges are what matters; the call explains who is bringing them.

## The Only Vision in the Book

Isaiah 1:1 calls the whole book a vision, and the word means something seen. But this is the only place where Isaiah describes an actual scene in front of him.

There are no night visions here like Zechariah's eight. There is no chariot like Ezekiel's. One throne room, one time, and the rest of the book is words.

## What He Saw

> “I saw also the Master sitting upon a throne, high and lifted up, and his train filled the temple.”
>
> — Isaiah 6:1

The train is the hem of the robe, the part that drags on the floor. Just the hem filled the building.

One detail worth having if anyone ever argues this chapter: verse 1 uses a title meaning Master, and the Name itself shows up two verses later, at 6:3, and again at 6:5.

Above the throne are seraphim with six wings each. Two cover the face, two cover the feet, and two are for flying (6:2). Four of the six wings are for covering up. Only two are for moving.

## Holy, Holy, Holy

> “And one cried unto another, and said, Holy, holy, holy, is Yahuah of hosts: the whole earth is full of his glory.”
>
> — Isaiah 6:3

This gets preached as three persons, one holy each. That is not how Hebrew works.

Hebrew has no word for very. It says a thing twice to make it strong and three times to say the most it can say. Jeremiah writes O earth, earth, earth (22:29), and there is one planet. Ezekiel writes I will overturn, overturn, overturn (21:27), and it is one act.

If saying it three times counted persons, Jeremiah just handed the world three earths.

The doorposts shook and the house filled with smoke (6:4).

## What It Did to Him

> “Then said I, Woe is me! for I am undone; because I am a man of unclean lips.”
>
> — Isaiah 6:5

Not his hands. His lips. The man whose whole job is going to be talking says his mouth is the problem.

The fix comes off the altar. A seraph takes a live coal with tongs from the altar, touches his mouth with it, and says his sin is purged (6:6–7). The altar is the place where sin was dealt with, and that is where the cure came from.

## Then He Is Sent

> “Whom shall I send, and who will go for us? Then said I, Here am I; send me.”
>
> — Isaiah 6:8

The word us gets used to argue for a conversation inside one being. The chapter already said who else is in the room.

Isaiah has just described seraphim standing above the throne. It is a throne room with attendants in it, and Micaiah describes the same setting the same way: I saw Yahuah sitting on his throne, and all the host of heaven standing by him on his right hand and on his left (1 Kings 22:19).

A king in a full court says who will go for us. That is not a hard sentence.

## The Part Nobody Preaches

> “Hear ye indeed, but understand not … Make the heart of this people fat, and make their ears heavy, and shut their eyes.”
>
> — Isaiah 6:9–10

Here am I, send me gets quoted at missions meetings. The next verse never does.

He is sent to speak to people who will not hear him, and he is told so before he starts.

That verse gets picked up three separate times later on. Yahushua quotes it about the crowds (Matthew 13:14–15). John quotes it (12:40). Paul quotes it in the last chapter of Acts, as the last thing he says to the men who came to hear him (Acts 28:26–27).

Three men, hundreds of years apart, reaching for the same verse about people who had the word in their hands and would not take it. It is the same condition the nation is in later, in chapter 42, when Yahuah calls His own servant blind.

## How Long

> “Then said I, Master, how long? And he answered, Until the cities be wasted without inhabitant … But yet in it shall be a tenth … the holy seed shall be the substance thereof.”
>
> — Isaiah 6:11, 13

Isaiah asks how long he has to do this. The answer is: until there is nothing left standing.

Then the last line of the chapter: a tenth remains, like a tree cut down to a stump, and there is seed in the stump. The whole rest of the book grows out of that.

## A Verse People Bring to This Chapter

John 12:41 says that Isaiah said these things when he saw his glory, and spake of him. It gets used to argue that the one Isaiah saw on the throne was the Son.

Two things keep that from settling anything. John quotes Isaiah 6:10 in the verse right before it, so the these things he means is the hearing-but-not-understanding. And the glory under discussion all through John 12 is the glory of the Son being lifted up, which John has been talking about since verse 23.

The verse can be read more than one way, and a sentence that can be read more than one way cannot carry a doctrine by itself.

## Where It Lands

The scene shows up again at the other end of the Scriptures, and it is recognizable.

> “And the four beasts had each of them six wings about him … and they rest not day and night, saying, Holy, holy, holy.”
>
> — Revelation 4:8

Six wings and the same three words. One throne, one seated figure, and creatures who have not stopped saying it since Isaiah walked in on them.

## In One Sentence

Isaiah saw one throne, admitted his mouth was the problem, got it cleaned off the altar, and was sent to people who were not going to listen.
