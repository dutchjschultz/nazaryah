In the early days of human civilization, when people huddled around fires and marveled at lightning from a safe distance, there lived a humble man named Barney. He was known throughout his village as one who spoke truthfully and lived righteously, though he possessed no special education and no worldly wisdom.

One evening, as Barney sat under the stars, the Founder of all things came to him — the One who had made the lightning and set its laws in place long before any man had a name for it. And in that meeting the Founder revealed to Barney the complete mysteries of electricity: knowledge no human mind could have conceived, discovered, or stumbled into by experiment.

“Write down what I show you,” the Founder said, “for this is the foundation your descendants will build on. Nothing true will ever be built apart from it.”

For forty days and forty nights Barney received revelation after revelation. He learned the nature of current, the principles of conductivity and resistance, the workings of generators and motors, the properties of semiconductors, and a hundred other things his age had no words for. Barney himself barely understood what he was writing. But his hand moved faithfully across the parchment, recording every detail exactly as it was given.

These notebooks became the treasure of Barney's people. Every word was studied and preserved. The knowledge in them ran so far ahead of the understanding of that time that many dismissed it as fantasy. Yet those who believed it and acted on it began to see things happen that no one could explain.

Decades passed, then centuries. Slowly, human understanding caught up to what had been written. Inventors and engineers found that when they followed the principles in those old notebooks, they could harness invisible forces that transformed their world. Great cities rose, lit and powered by knowledge first given to a man who had no education at all. Every breakthrough traced back to the same foundation.

Human ingenuity expanded on it. Engineers developed applications the notebooks never described — improvements, refinements, new machines. These were useful and good. But everyone understood the difference: the foundation was revealed, and everything after it was built. The building never corrected the foundation.

---

Among the great electrical companies that emerged, one stood above all others: Electric World. And Electric World had an owner — not a distant investor, but the Founder himself, the same One who had come to Barney under the stars. The company was his. The knowledge was his. The men who ran the floor were stewards, nothing more, and every one of them knew it.

In time the Founder sent his Son into the company.

He did not send him with a title, an office, or a corner desk. The Son came dressed as a worker, took a place on the floor, ate what the workers ate, and carried what they carried. The men simply called him the Guide.

The Guide understood electricity in a way that seemed to reach past even the detail in Barney's notebooks. He could explain the deepest mysteries with a length of wire and a nail. He could solve problems that had defeated the best minds in the industry, and do it without raising his voice. But he never once claimed the knowledge as his own.

“I say nothing the Founder has not given me,” he told them. “The company is his. The notebooks are his. The power in these walls is his. I am here because he sent me.”

Working alongside him, day after day and year after year, was a devoted employee named Clyde. Clyde watched every demonstration, heard every explanation, and had a hand in every project. He saw firsthand how the Guide took the ancient principles from Barney's notebooks and applied them in ways no one had imagined — never against the notebooks, always out of them.

The Guide would gather the men around him at break and open things up. “You see how current flows only through a complete circuit?” he would say. “It is the same with the Founder's power. It flows only through those who stay connected to the source.” His teaching was plain, practical, and it changed the way men looked at their own hands.

---

Meanwhile, the board of directors had other plans for Barney's revelations. They saw in those principles the opportunity to build systems of control — grids that would make people dependent, devices that would monitor and restrict, applications that would gather all the power into a few hands. And they grew increasingly irritated with the Guide, whose teaching kept showing the workers how to use electricity for freedom, comfort, and peace.

Then the directors learned who the Guide actually was. They learned that the quiet man in work clothes was the Founder's Son, and that the company they had been running was never theirs to keep.

They did not submit. They calculated.

This is the heir, they said among themselves. Kill him, and the company is ours.

So they accused him of undermining profits and spreading dangerous ideas among the workers, and they had him killed — certain that with the Guide gone they could bend Barney's revelations to their own purposes and turn electricity into an instrument of bondage.

The workers were devastated. They had lost not only their teacher and friend, but the one man who knew how to apply the foundation for the good of everyone. The board began implementing their restrictive policies before the week was out.

But something they had not calculated began to happen.

---

Requests came pouring in to Clyde. People throughout the industry had heard what had been done at Electric World, and they wanted to understand the principles behind it. So Clyde sat down to write.

And as he wrote, he found the Guide had not left him.

Though the Guide was gone from the floor, Clyde sensed his presence more powerfully than when they had stood side by side. Conversations he had half-forgotten came back whole. Explanations he had never fully grasped opened up in front of him. Insights arrived that he knew were not his own. It was not a new man in the room. It was the Guide's own mind and the Founder's own power, still working — reaching Clyde in a way that no longer required the Guide to be standing next to him.

The book Clyde wrote was unlike anything he could have produced on his own. It was built entirely on what he had learned from the Guide, yet it carried understanding beyond what he had consciously observed. And it honored two things at once: Barney's original revelations, and the Guide's faithful demonstration of them.

Clyde's book spread across the world. Engineers read it hungrily. Where Barney's notebooks held the foundational truths, Clyde's book showed how those truths were meant to be lived out on the floor — exactly as the Guide had taught, now available to anyone who wanted it.

But Clyde was careful, and he said so in his own preface:

> *I have felt the Guide's presence with me in the writing of this book, quickening my memory and opening my understanding. Let no man mistake what that means. It is not a third man on the payroll. It is the Founder's own power, carrying the mind of the Son he sent. This book is valuable not because I received new revelation, but because I worked beside the One who perfectly embodied the revelation already given.*

---

As the book spread, people came to understand the distinction.

Barney's notebooks contained direct revelation from the Founder — truths no human mind could have discovered or invented. They carried absolute authority because they came from the One who made electricity in the first place. Every principle in them was foundational, and none of it changed.

Clyde's book carried a real authority, but a different one. It was the eyewitness account of a man who had worked beside the Son — the One who perfectly understood and demonstrated the Founder's original revelations — written under the continuing influence of that same Son.

Both were precious to those who worked with electricity. The notebooks gave the foundation, without which nothing else was possible. The book gave the faithful witness of how the foundation was meant to be walked out.

And the wise recognized the order. Clyde's insights were invaluable and carried the weight of the Guide's own authority — and they were always to be measured against Barney's notebooks. Any teaching, any practice, any clever new application that contradicted the foundation was to be rejected, no matter how appealing it looked, no matter who taught it, no matter how many followed it.

Clyde would have been the first to say so. His whole book existed to show men how to live inside the notebooks. Never how to live past them.

Years went by. Both writings kept instructing new generations. The notebooks remained the unchanging foundation — the direct word of the Founder about the nature of electrical reality. Clyde's book remained the faithful witness to how the Son had demonstrated those eternal principles.

And the board of directors discovered that killing the Guide had not given them the company after all. His influence grew stronger after his death than it had been before it, spreading through Clyde's writings and the testimony of the men who had known him. The liberating applications he had taught kept spreading, and every effort to suppress them only carried them further.

Together, Barney's notebooks and Clyde's book formed one complete library — enough to let a man not only understand electricity, but live and work in harmony with it as the Founder had intended: for freedom, comfort, and peace, rather than control and oppression.

---

And in the end, both Barney and Clyde pointed past themselves and past their own writings to the same place.

Not to each other. Not to the Guide as the source.

To the Founder. The One who first revealed the mysteries. The One who owned the company. The One who sent his Son to demonstrate them, and whose power continues to teach every man who will stay connected to it.

The Guide never asked to be the source. He spent his whole life on that floor pointing at somebody else.

The workers who saw that understood everything.
