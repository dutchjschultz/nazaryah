## The Verse

“Think not that I am come to destroy the law, or the prophets: I am not come to destroy, but to fulfil” (Matthew 5:17). The sentence is built on a contrast — not this, but that — and the two verbs are meant to be opposites. The common pulpit reading makes *fulfil* mean *end*, which collapses the contrast into nonsense: “I did not come to end the Law, but to end it.” Heard with Hebrew ears, the two words carry a wordplay that settles the matter.

## The Two Words

The Greek *kataluō*, “destroy,” means at root to loose down or unharness — the traveler halting and laying his load down. More to the point, the Greek translators of the prophets used it for the Hebrew *shabath*, “to cause to cease,” the very root of the word Sabbath:

- **Jeremiah 7:34**: “Then will I cause to cease… the voice of mirth, and the voice of gladness, the voice of the bridegroom, and the voice of the bride.”
- **Jeremiah 16:9**: “I will cause to cease out of this place… the voice of the bridegroom, and the voice of the bride.”

- **Ezekiel 26:13**: “I will cause the noise of thy songs to cease; and the sound of thy harps shall be no more heard.”

Each line is *shabath* in Hebrew and *kataluō* in Greek. On the other side, *plēroō*, “fulfil,” stands for the Hebrew *male* — to fill, to carry out, as when Yahuah “spake with his mouth… and hath with his hand fulfilled it” (1 Kings 8:15). That is the whole of the proof the wordplay needs: *kataluō* can carry *shabath*, and *plēroō* carries *male*.

## Cease Against Fill

Put back into Hebrew, the sentence sets the Sabbath word against the work word. *Shabath* is what Yahuah did when He “rested on the seventh day from all his work” (Genesis 2:2) — the work laid down. *Male* is the hand filled with work, the word carried out to its end. Six days the hand is full; on the seventh it ceases (Exodus 20:9–10). Yahushua took that pattern, written into the Law itself, and turned it on the Law: I did not come to give the Law its Sabbath. I came to fill it — to work it, to carry it out.

The irony is sharp. The one speaking is the Son of man who is “Master even of the sabbath day” (Matthew 12:8). The one who holds authority over the ceasing is the one who declared He would not cause the Law to cease. If anyone could have put the Law to rest, it was He, and He said in plain words that He did not come to do it.

The doubling adds weight in the Hebrew manner. *Destroy* is said twice — “think not that I am come to destroy… I am not come to destroy” — and *fulfil* once, as the answer. The denial is pressed home before the purpose is stated, and the next breath seals it: not one jot or tittle passes “till heaven and earth pass” (Matthew 5:18).

## The Bridegroom’s Voice

The Jeremiah verses carry the wordplay further. What Yahuah caused to cease — *shabath*, *kataluō* — was a sound: the voice of joy, the voice of the bridegroom and of the bride. Judgment on Jerusalem was a wedding gone silent. Jeremiah also recorded the promise that the silence would break: “The voice of joy, and the voice of gladness, the voice of the bridegroom, and the voice of the bride” would be heard again (Jeremiah 33:10–11).

Yahushua named Himself that Bridegroom (Matthew 9:15). John the Immerser, the friend of the bridegroom, described the moment Jeremiah promised: he “standeth and heareth him, rejoiceth greatly because of the bridegroom’s voice: this my joy therefore is fulfilled” (John 3:29). The verb is *plēroō*. The two words of Matthew 5:17 meet over the same subject. In the prophets, *kataluō* silenced the bridegroom’s voice. In the Gospel, the bridegroom’s voice returns and joy is *plēroō*.

So when the Bridegroom stood on the mountain and said, “I am not come to *kataluō*, but to *plēroō*,” the words carried their own echo. He did not come to bring the silence of Jeremiah 7 and 16. He came to fill. And He tied the filling to the commandments Himself: “If ye keep my commandments, ye shall abide in my love; even as I have kept my Father’s commandments… that your joy might be full” (John 15:10–11) — *plēroō* again. The Law kept is the joy filled.

## Conclusion

Matthew 5:17 is a Hebrew wordplay carried in Greek. *Kataluō* stands for *shabath*, “to cause to cease”; *plēroō* stands for *male*, “to fill.” Yahushua set the Sabbath word against the work word and declared that the Law would not be put to rest but carried out. Behind it runs the prophets’ own pairing: *shabath* silencing the bridegroom’s voice, and the promised day when that voice would return and joy would be full.

Revelation finishes the picture with the same two outcomes. Over Babylon the prophets’ ceasing falls for good: the sound of harpers “shall be heard no more at all in thee… and the voice of the bridegroom and of the bride shall be heard no more at all in thee” (Revelation 18:22–23) — Jeremiah 7:34 and Ezekiel 26:13 spoken over the great city. Over the saints, “that keep the commandments of God” (Revelation 14:12), the voice returns: “the marriage of the Lamb is come, and his wife hath made herself ready” (Revelation 19:7). Babylon receives the ceasing. The Bride receives the filling.
