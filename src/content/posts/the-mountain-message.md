## The Claim

The Sermon on the Mount is the most quoted passage in the modern pulpit and one of the least examined. It is preached as the founding charter of a new religion, the place where a softer covenant begins and the old one is left behind. This investigation makes a single claim against that reading: Matthew 5 through 7 is Torah exposition from beginning to end.

Yahushua is not replacing the Law on that mountain. He is taking it back from men who had trimmed it, padded it, and moved it outdoors, and He says so in plain words at both ends of the sermon. Ten studies carry the evidence, each one taking a block of the sermon and setting it beside the Scripture it was drawn from. The hub states the claim. The studies prove it.

## Two Mountains

The sermon opens on two lines that are almost never preached: "And seeing the multitudes, he went up into a mountain: and when he was set, his disciples came unto him" (Matthew 5:1). A man goes up a mountain, and the Law comes down from it. That had happened before.

At Sinai the people were fenced off. The mountain burned, the trumpet sounded, and the nation begged that Yahuah speak to them no more directly (Exodus 20:18-19). Moses went up and came down carrying the words. On this mountain nobody is fenced. The disciples come near, and He sits down, which is the posture of a teacher rather than a lawgiver.

Moses had promised the office: "Yahuah thy God will raise up unto thee a Prophet from the midst of thee, of thy brethren, like unto me" (Deuteronomy 18:15). The job was defined narrowly three verses later: "I will put my words in his mouth; and he shall speak unto them all that I shall command him" (Deuteronomy 18:18). A prophet like Moses does not deliver a different law. He carries words that are not his own, and Yahushua said exactly that about His own teaching: "My doctrine is not mine, but his that sent me" (John 7:16).

So the mountain is not a second Sinai issuing a second law. It is the promised prophet delivering the words of Yahuah to a people who had been handed a hedged version of them.

## The Two Walls

The sermon is bracketed, and both walls name the same thing. At the front: "Think not that I am come to destroy the law, or the prophets: I am not come to destroy, but to fulfil" (Matthew 5:17). Near the close, the same pair again, named as the point of everything said in between: "for this is the law and the prophets" (Matthew 7:12).

Eleven verses after that second wall, the sermon hands down its verdict, and the charge is built out of the word for law: "depart from me, ye that work iniquity" (Matthew 7:23), anomia, lawlessness. The sermon opens by refusing to abolish the Law, closes by naming the Law as its summary, and ends by sentencing men for the lack of it.

## The Ten Studies

Each title is a phrase spoken on the mountain. The passage follows it, and the one line under it is what the study proves.

Blessed Are (5:3-12). Eight blessings, eight passages already written by the prophets and the psalms.

A City Set on a Hill (5:13-16). Salt of the covenant, a lamp that makes no light of its own, and the good works Deuteronomy 4:6 already named.

Not One Jot (5:17-20). Fulfil means fill full, and the clock He set was heaven and earth, not the tree.

Ye Have Heard (5:21-48). Six quotations of what men said, one of which Yahuah never said at all.

This Is the Law and the Prophets (5:39-42; 7:7-12). The cheek, the cloke, the second mile, the asking, and the golden rule sealed by naming the Law.

Enter Into Thy Closet (6:1-18). Alms, prayer, and fasting all kept; only the audience is corrected.

Ye Cannot Serve (6:19-34). The evil eye is a Hebrew money idiom, and the manna was a test of walking in the Law.

Judge Not (7:1-6). A measure, not a ban, and the paragraph ends with the reader removing the mote.

Strait Is the Gate (7:13-27). Deuteronomy 30 and the Torah test for a false prophet.

I Never Knew You (7:21-23). The verdict is lawlessness, and every man rejected is inside the assembly.

## How This Investigation Reads

Every study opens on the Scripture that had already said it and closes under one heading, Where the Pulpit Buried It. That is the shape of the whole project: what was written, then what was put in its place.

The order of the doctrine is not reversed anywhere in these studies. Entry is through the blood. A man is brought near by the offering Yahuah provided, never by his record, and nothing in the sermon on the mountain is offered as the means of coming in. Matthew 5 through 7 is addressed to disciples who are already following. It describes the walk of a delivered man, and it describes it out of the Law.

Read straight through with the sources in the margin, the Sermon on the Mount stops being the charter of a new religion. It becomes what the text says it is twice, at both ends, in words that need no interpreting: the law and the prophets.
