*Witness Two in the investigation [**The Sealed Count**](/investigations/the-sealed-count) — back to the case.*

## The Question the Ground Can Answer

The count of twenty-two books leans on the count of twenty-two letters. If the letters were a late invention, or if the alphabet in Israel ran to some other number, the whole link would be a story told after the fact. So the question is fair. Can it be shown that Israel's alphabet had twenty-two letters, in a fixed order, long before anyone counted books?

It can. The answer does not come from a later writer's claim. It comes out of the ground. Archaeologists have found the Hebrew alphabet written out in order, letter after letter, on clay, stone, and plaster at sites across the land. These are called abecedaries, alphabet lists, the kind of thing a student writes while learning to write. Three of them stand out.

## Izbet Sartah

In 1976 excavators working at Izbet Sartah, a small hill village west of Shiloh near modern Rosh HaAyin, found a broken piece of pottery with writing scratched into it. A piece of pottery used this way is called an ostracon. It dates to roughly 1200 to 1000 BC, the time of the judges.

The sherd carries five lines of letters. Most are practice strokes. But the bottom line is the alphabet itself, written out from beginning to end by someone learning the letters. It runs to twenty-two letters. Some scholars connect the site with Ebenezer, where Israel camped before the battle in which the ark was taken (1 Samuel 4:1). Whether or not that identification holds, the sherd shows a learner in the hill country of Israel copying out the twenty-two letters in the days when Samuel was a boy.

One detail matters for the next witness. On the Izbet Sartah sherd, the letter *pe* comes before the letter *ayin*. In the order printed in Hebrew Bibles today, *ayin* comes first.

## Tel Zayit

In 2005 a team excavating Tel Zayit, in the lowland hills of Judah, found a limestone boulder of about thirty-eight pounds built into the wall of a building. Carved across its face was the alphabet, letter by letter. The stone dates to the tenth century BC, the age of the early kings of Israel.

The Tel Zayit alphabet runs in nearly the standard order, with a few neighboring letters trading places, which is common in a student's copy. What matters most is not the small swaps. It is that a full alphabet was being carved in Judah in the generation of David and Solomon, and that it was the same alphabet of twenty-two letters.

## Kuntillet Ajrud

Far to the south, in the northeastern Sinai, stand the ruins of Kuntillet Ajrud, a way station on the desert road, dated to around 800 BC. When it was excavated in 1975 and 1976, the walls and storage jars were found covered with writing and drawings. Among them are several alphabet lists, written out more than once by different hands. Here again, *pe* comes before *ayin*.

Kuntillet Ajrud is a hostile witness, and that makes it more useful. The same site carries inscriptions that join the Name of Yahuah to Asherah, the very mixing of worship that the prophets condemned and that brought down the northern kingdom (2 Kings 17:16). The scribes who worked there were not keepers of a pure tradition. Yet even in a compromised place, the alphabet they learned and copied was the same twenty-two letters. The count did not depend on faithful scribes. It was simply the alphabet of the land.

## Pe Before Ayin

The order of the letters connects the ground to the text. Several chapters of Scripture are written as alphabet poems, each verse or group of verses beginning with the next letter. Lamentations has four of them. In Lamentations 1 the order is the familiar one, with *ayin* before *pe*. In Lamentations 2, 3, and 4, the order is reversed, and *pe* comes before *ayin*.

For a long time that reversal was treated as an oddity, perhaps a scribe's slip. The dig sites settle it. Izbet Sartah and Kuntillet Ajrud both show that *pe* before *ayin* was a real, living order used in the land of Israel. The writer of Lamentations was not making a mistake. He was writing an alphabet that people in Israel actually used. The text and the ground agree.

## The Old Letters

None of these alphabets look like the square Hebrew letters printed in a modern Hebrew Bible. They are written in the old Hebrew script, sometimes called paleo-Hebrew, which is closer in shape to the letters the Phoenicians used along the coast. The square script came into common use after the exile in Babylon, borrowed from the Aramaic that the empire spoke.

The shapes changed. The number did not. The same twenty-two letters carry over from the old script into the square one, in the same basic order. Among the scrolls found in the caves near the Dead Sea, some scribes who wrote everything else in the square letters still wrote the Name of Yahuah in the old script, as though the oldest letters belonged to it in a special way. The alphabet was older than the empire that changed its handwriting.

## What the Stones Prove

These finds do not list the books of Scripture. That is not their job. Their job is to establish the letters. And on that question they speak clearly. From the time of the judges, through the early kings, and on into the divided kingdom, people in the land of Israel were learning, copying, and carving an alphabet of twenty-two letters in a fixed order.

That means that when later writers counted the books as twenty-two and said the count answered to the letters, they were tying the Scriptures to something every Israelite child learned before he could read a scroll. The number was not a scholar's invention. It was the first lesson in every schoolroom of the land.

## Where the Pulpit Buried It

The pulpit treats the Hebrew alphabet as trivia, a curiosity for a sermon illustration, the little letter names printed over the sections of Psalm 119 that most readers skip. The idea that the alphabet might carry weight in how Scripture was kept never comes up.

The ground says otherwise. The twenty-two letters were written on pottery in Samuel's time, carved in stone in David's time, and painted on walls in the time of the divided kingdom. A count of books tied to those letters was tied to the foundation of written Hebrew itself, not to the decision of a council in North Africa.

Revelation, the prophecy Yahuah gave to John, still speaks in the language of letters. In Revelation 1:8 the Almighty calls Himself the Alpha and the Omega, the beginning and the ending, which in a Hebrew mouth would be the *aleph* and the *tav*, the first and last of the twenty-two. It is the same claim Yahuah made through Isaiah: "I am the first, and I am the last; and beside me there is no God" (Isaiah 44:6).
