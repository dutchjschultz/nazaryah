***nasa / salach** — to lift the debt off and bear it away*

## Part One — The Surface

### 1.1 — What People Think Forgiveness Means

Ask most Christians what forgiveness means and you will get a one-sentence answer: letting go of anger, choosing not to hold a grudge, or moving on. The word has become soft. It sounds like therapy. But the Hebrew Scriptures never treat forgiveness as a feeling. They treat it as a **transaction** — a series of deliberate, costly, covenantal actions that Yahuah (God) modelled first and then commanded His people to follow.

### 1.2 — One English Word, Six Hebrew Actions

English has one word for forgiveness. Hebrew has at least six. Each one describes a different action. A pardon is not the same as a release. A release is not the same as an erasure. An erasure is not the same as a covering. And none of them are the same as lifting a burden or passing over a charge. When you flatten all six into one English word, you lose the weight of every one of them.

What follows is not a vocabulary lesson. It is a guided walk through the stories, laws, and prophetic promises where these six words appear — and where the Jubilee cycle ties them all together into a single picture of what Yahuah does when He forgives.

## Part Two — The Root

### 2.1 — Sālaḥ: The Pardon Only God Can Give

The Hebrew word ***sālaḥ*** (סלח) is the closest equivalent to our English word "forgive." But there is something about this word that most people never notice. In almost every Old Testament occurrence, **the only one who does it is Yahuah.** Humans do not *sālaḥ* each other. This is a divine prerogative — a judicial pardon, a real clearing of guilt from the record.

> **Numbers 14:19–20**
>
> *"Pardon, I beseech thee, the iniquity of this people according unto the greatness of thy mercy... And Yahuah said, I have pardoned according to thy word."*

Moses asked. Yahuah pardoned. This was not therapy. This was a sovereign act of the only One with the authority to erase the charge. And notice what triggered it — not Israel's goodness but the *greatness of Yahuah's mercy.* The ground of pardon is always the character of the pardoner, not the merit of the guilty.

> **Nehemiah 9:17**
>
> *"But thou art a God ready to pardon, gracious and merciful, slow to anger, and of great kindness."*

Nehemiah lists *sālaḥ* alongside the Exodus 34:6–7 character declaration: gracious, merciful, slow to anger, great in *chesed* (covenant loyalty). Pardoning is not something Yahuah does reluctantly. It is who He is.

Here is where this matters for the New Testament. When Yahushua (Jesus) forgave sins during His ministry, the religious leaders were outraged.

> **Mark 2:7**
>
> *"Why doth this man thus speak blasphemies? Who can forgive sins but God only?"*

They said that because they understood *sālaḥ.* They knew this kind of pardon belonged to Yahuah alone. What they missed is that Yahuah had given that authority to His appointed agent — the Son He sent to act in His name.

### 2.2 — Nāśāʼ: Lifting the Burden

The word ***nāśāʼ*** (נשא) literally means *to lift, to carry, to bear away.* When Scripture uses it for sin, the picture is physical. Guilt is a weight. Someone has to carry it. Either the sinner staggers under it, or someone else lifts it off their back. This is the word that tells you **forgiveness costs someone something.**

> **Genesis 50:17**
>
> *"So shall ye say unto Joseph, Forgive, I pray thee now, the trespass of thy brethren, and their sin; for they did unto thee evil."*

The word here is *nāśāʼ.* Joseph's brothers are not asking him to feel differently. They are asking him to *lift the weight* — to stop carrying the debt they owe. Joseph does. He absorbs the cost of what they did without passing it back. He bears it.

The Day of Atonement is where this word comes alive in the most dramatic picture in all of Scripture.

> **Leviticus 16:21–22**
>
> *"And Aaron shall lay both his hands upon the head of the live goat, and confess over him all the iniquities of the children of Israel... and the goat shall bear upon him all their iniquities unto a land not inhabited."*

The Hebrew says the goat *nāśāʼ* — carries, lifts, bears away — all their iniquities. The sins are transferred from the people to the animal, and the animal carries them into the wilderness where they can never return. John the Baptist knew exactly what he was saying when he pointed at Yahushua:

> **John 1:29**
>
> *"Behold the Lamb of God, which taketh away the sin of the world."*

The Lamb lifts. The Lamb carries. The Lamb bears away. That is *nāśāʼ.*

### 2.3 — Māḥāh: Wiping the Record Clean

The Hebrew word ***māḥāh*** (מחה) means *to blot out, to wipe away, to erase.* This is record-book language. If your offence was written in a ledger, *māḥāh* is someone deleting the entry completely.

> **Isaiah 43:25**
>
> *"I, even I, am he that blotteth out thy transgressions for mine own sake, and will not remember thy sins."*

Two things to notice. First, He blots out for *His own sake* — because this is who He is. Second, He *will not remember.* In Hebrew, "remember" is not a brain function. It is a covenant action. When Yahuah "remembers" a sin, He brings it forward as an active charge. He holds it against you. He treats you according to it. When He says He will not remember, He means: **this charge will never be reopened.**

> **Psalm 51:1–9**
>
> *"Have mercy upon me, O God, according to thy lovingkindness: according unto the multitude of thy tender mercies blot out my transgressions. Wash me throughly from mine iniquity, and cleanse me from my sin."*

David uses *māḥāh* alongside washing and cleansing. The language is not abstract. It is a man begging for the record to be erased and the stain to be scrubbed away. Notice that David appeals to Yahuah's *chesed* (lovingkindness) and *rachamīm* (tender mercies) — the same Exodus 34 character traits.

> **Colossians 2:14**
>
> *"Blotting out the handwriting of ordinances that was against us, which was contrary to us, and took it out of the way, nailing it to his cross."*

Paul uses the same image. The written record of debt — nailed to the cross. Not filed. Not suspended. Destroyed.

### 2.4 — ʿĀvar: Passing Over

The word ***ʿāvar*** (עבר) means *to pass over, to pass by, to let pass.* When used with sin, Yahuah sees the offence, has every right to act, and chooses to withhold judgment — not because the sin does not matter, but because He is making room for repentance.

> **Micah 7:18–19**
>
> *"Who is a God like unto thee, that pardoneth iniquity, and passeth by the transgression of the remnant of his heritage? He retaineth not his anger for ever, because he delighteth in mercy. He will turn again, he will have compassion upon us; he will subdue our iniquities; and thou wilt cast all their sins into the depths of the sea."*

Look at how many forgiveness words Micah stacks in two verses: pardoning (*sālaḥ*), passing by (*ʿāvar*), subduing iniquities, and casting sins into the sea. The prophets did not flatten forgiveness into one idea. They painted it with every colour they had.

Paul picks up the *ʿāvar* concept directly:

> **Romans 3:25**
>
> *"Whom God hath set forth to be a propitiation through faith in his blood, to declare his righteousness for the remission of sins that are past, through the forbearance of God."*

The word "forbearance" here is the Greek *anochē* — patient restraint. Paul's argument is that Yahuah had been *passing over* sins for centuries, not because He was careless with justice, but because the cross was coming. The Messiah's blood would settle the account that patience had been holding open.

### 2.5 — Kāphar: Covering the Damage

The word ***kāphar*** (כפר) is usually translated "atone" or "make atonement." It is not strictly a forgiveness word. It is the *mechanism* that makes forgiveness righteous. Forgiveness releases the debt. Atonement deals with the damage the debt caused.

> **Leviticus 17:11**
>
> *"For the life of the flesh is in the blood: and I have given it to you upon the altar to make an atonement for your souls: for it is the blood that maketh an atonement for the soul."*

*Kāphar* shares the same root as *kopher* (the ransom price) and *kapporet* (the mercy seat — the lid of the Ark where blood was applied). The price, the process, and the place all come from the same three Hebrew letters: *kaf-peh-resh.* Forgiveness does not float in the air. It has an address.

> **Hebrews 9:22**
>
> *"And almost all things are by the law purged with blood; and without shedding of blood is no remission."*

Notice the word "remission" — that is *aphesis* in the Greek, the release word. Even the release of sin requires blood. Forgiveness and atonement are partners. You cannot have the full picture without both.

### 2.6 — Shāmaṭ and Derōr: The Forgiveness Calendar

The Hebrew word ***shāmaṭ*** (שמט) means *to release, to let drop, to remit.* It is the word behind the Shemitah — the seventh-year release when all debts were cancelled, slaves were freed, and the land rested.

> **Deuteronomy 15:1–2**
>
> *"At the end of every seven years thou shalt make a release. And this is the manner of the release: Every creditor that lendeth ought unto his neighbour shall release it; he shall not exact it of his neighbour, or of his brother; because it is called Yahuah's release."*

Then there is ***derōr*** (דרור), which means *liberty, freedom, release.* This is the Jubilee word.

> **Leviticus 25:10**
>
> *"And ye shall hallow the fiftieth year, and proclaim liberty throughout all the land unto all the inhabitants thereof: it shall be a jubile unto you; and ye shall return every man unto his possession, and ye shall return every man unto his family."*

The word "liberty" in that verse is *derōr.* Hold that in your mind, because that exact word appears again in Isaiah:

> **Isaiah 61:1–2**
>
> *"The Spirit of the Lord GOD is upon me; because Yahuah hath anointed me to preach good tidings unto the meek; he hath sent me to bind up the brokenhearted, to proclaim liberty to the captives, and the opening of the prison to them that are bound; to proclaim the acceptable year of Yahuah."*

The word translated "liberty" in Isaiah 61:1 is the same Hebrew word: **derōr.** And "the acceptable year of Yahuah" is *shĕnat rātsōn* — literally the year of favour, the year of acceptance. Isaiah is using the language of Leviticus 25 — the Jubilee — and applying it to the coming Messianic mission. This is not an inference. It is the same vocabulary.

Now watch what Yahushua does with it:

> **Luke 4:18–21**
>
> *"The Spirit of the Lord is upon me, because he hath anointed me to preach the gospel to the poor; he hath sent me to heal the brokenhearted, to preach deliverance to the captives, and recovering of sight to the blind, to set at liberty them that are bruised, to preach the acceptable year of the Lord. And he closed the book... And he began to say unto them, This day is this scripture fulfilled in your ears."*

Yahushua opens the Isaiah scroll, reads the Jubilee passage, closes it, and says: **this is fulfilled right now.** He is not announcing a metaphor. He is announcing that He is the walking, breathing Jubilee. The *derōr* of Leviticus 25 and the *derōr* of Isaiah 61 have arrived in a person.

## Part Three — The Ancient Ground

### 3.1 — The Day That Held All Six Words

Yahuah did not leave forgiveness to human willpower. He built it into the calendar. Not as one event using one word, but as an annual cycle where all six acts of forgiveness happened together on a single day.

The Day of Atonement — Yom Kippur — was the day Israel's entire forgiveness vocabulary came to life.

> **Leviticus 16:30**
>
> *"For on that day shall the priest make an atonement for you, to cleanse you, that ye may be clean from all your sins before Yahuah."*

Walk through what happened on that single day and watch the six words appear.

***Kāphar*** — the high priest entered the Most Holy Place and sprinkled blood on the *kapporet* (the mercy seat). This was atonement: the covering, the cleansing, the repair of what sin had damaged in the relationship between Yahuah and His people (Leviticus 16:14–16).

***Nāśāʼ*** — the high priest laid his hands on the live goat and transferred the iniquities of Israel onto it. The goat was sent into the wilderness, *bearing* all their sins away to a place of no return (Leviticus 16:21–22).

***Sālaḥ*** — the result of the entire ceremony was Yahuah's pardon. The divine judge declared the case cleared. Israel stood forgiven — not by their own merit, but by the sovereign decision of the One who accepted the blood.

***Māḥāh*** — the sins were not filed for future reference. They were carried into the wilderness, never to return. The record was blotted out. As Micah would later write: "Thou wilt cast all their sins into the depths of the sea" (Micah 7:19).

***ʿĀvar*** — the fact that Israel needed this ceremony every year tells you that Yahuah had been passing over sins all year long, withholding full judgment, holding the account in patience until the appointed day of settlement.

***Shāmaṭ / Derōr*** — the Jubilee trumpet sounded on the Day of Atonement itself. Not on a random Tuesday. The release of debts, the freeing of slaves, and the restoration of land all began on Yom Kippur.

> **Leviticus 25:9**
>
> *"Then shalt thou cause the trumpet of the jubile to sound on the tenth day of the seventh month, in the day of atonement shall ye make the trumpet sound throughout all your land."*

Read that again. **The Jubilee began on the Day of Atonement.** Atonement and release were not separate ideas happening in separate seasons. They were the same event. The blood was applied first. Then the trumpet sounded. First the covering, then the liberty. First the price paid, then the captives set free.

Forgiveness was not a suggestion. It was a scheduled event — and the schedule placed all six Hebrew words on the same day, in the same ceremony, pointing to the same future reality: a Messiah who would fulfil every one of them at once.

### 3.2 — Ruth: The Jubilee on a Threshing Floor

The book of Ruth is not a love story. It is a Jubilee story wearing the clothes of a romance.

Naomi has lost everything. Her husband Elimelech is dead. Her two sons are dead. She is a widow in Moab with no income, no heir, and no way to recover the family land back in Bethlehem. Under Israelite law, her husband's property should return to the family in the Jubilee year. But Naomi cannot wait for the Jubilee. She is destitute now.

> **Ruth 4:3–4**
>
> *"And he said unto the kinsman, Naomi, that is come again out of the country of Moab, selleth a parcel of land, which was our brother Elimelech's: And I thought to advertise thee, saying, Buy it before the inhabitants..."*

Enter Boaz. He is a *go'el* — a kinsman redeemer — a near relative with the legal right and the financial ability to recover what the family had lost. The *go'el* must be a family member, must have the means to pay, and must be willing. Boaz is all three.

Watch what Boaz does. He pays the price to recover the land (*kāphar / kopher* — the cost of restoration). He lifts the burden of poverty and childlessness from Naomi and Ruth (*nāśāʼ*). He restores the family name and property to its rightful line (*derōr* — Jubilee restoration, everything returning to its original owner). And Ruth, the Moabite widow who had no legal standing, is brought fully into the covenant family.

Boaz did not wait for the Jubilee calendar to arrive. He *became* the Jubilee. He enacted the release personally. And from his line came David. And from David's line came Yahushua — the ultimate Go'el who would do for all of Israel what Boaz did for one family on a threshing floor in Bethlehem.

### 3.3 — Joseph: Forgiveness Before There Was a Law

Long before Sinai, long before the Day of Atonement or the Shemitah laws existed, Joseph demonstrated what forgiveness looks like when all six acts come together in one man.

His brothers sold him into slavery. He spent years in a foreign prison. Then Yahuah raised him to the second-highest seat in Egypt. When the famine drove his brothers to Egypt for grain, they stood before the brother they had betrayed — and they did not recognise him.

Joseph could have destroyed them. He had the power. He had the authority. He had the legitimate grievance. Instead, look at what he did.

> **Genesis 50:19–21**
>
> *"And Joseph said unto them, Fear not: for am I in the place of God? But as for you, ye thought evil against me; but God meant it unto good, to bring to pass, as it is this day, to save much people alive. Now therefore fear ye not: I will nourish you, and your little ones."*

Joseph passed over the full judgment he could have imposed (*ʿāvar*). He lifted the burden of guilt from his brothers by telling them not to be afraid (*nāśāʼ*). He released the debt entirely — not only refusing to demand repayment but promising to *nourish* them going forward (*shāmaṭ*). He did not bring the old charge forward; he reframed the entire history as Yahuah's provision (*māḥāh*). He absorbed the cost of their sin personally — years of suffering that he bore without passing it back (*kāphar* in principle). And he spoke with the authority of the one in power: "Am I in the place of God?" — leaving final judgment to Yahuah while extending human pardon from his own position (*sālaḥ* in shadow form).

Joseph is one of the clearest pictures of the Messiah in the entire Old Testament — betrayed by his own brothers, raised to a throne, and then forgiving the very people who put him in the pit.

### 3.4 — The Seventy Weeks: A National Mercy Window

Now we come to one of the most misunderstood teachings Yahushua ever gave.

> **Matthew 18:21–22**
>
> *"Then came Peter to him, and said, Lord, how oft shall my brother sin against me, and I forgive him? till seven times? Yahushua saith unto him, I say not unto thee, Until seven times: but, Until seventy times seven."*

Most sermons say this just means "a lot." But Yahushua chose His numbers carefully, and His listeners would have heard the echo of Daniel immediately.

> **Daniel 9:24**
>
> *"Seventy weeks are determined upon thy people and upon thy holy city, to finish the transgression, and to make an end of sins, and to make reconciliation for iniquity, and to bring in everlasting righteousness."*

Daniel's seventy weeks were a probationary mercy period — a national *ʿāvar*, a passing over — given to Judah and Jerusalem. The purpose was specific: *to finish transgression, to end sins, to make reconciliation for iniquity.* In other words, Yahuah was giving the nation a fixed window of time to repent. This was not unlimited patience with no deadline. It was measured mercy with an appointed end.

When Yahushua said "seventy times seven," He was not giving Peter a random large number. He was speaking in the language of Daniel's prophecy. And what He did next proves it — He told a parable about a king and two servants.

### 3.5 — The Parable of the Two Servants

> **Matthew 18:23–27**
>
> *"Therefore is the kingdom of heaven likened unto a certain king, which would take account of his servants. And when he had begun to reckon, one was brought unto him, which owed him ten thousand talents... The lord of that servant was moved with compassion, and loosed him, and forgave him the debt."*

The king cancels an unpayable debt. Ten thousand talents — a sum so absurd it could only represent the accumulated sin-debt of a nation before Yahuah. The word "forgave" is *aphēken* — from *aphiēmi*, the release word. And the word "loosed" is *apelusen*, also a release. The entire description is Jubilee language: the debt is dropped, the servant is freed.

> **Matthew 18:28–30**
>
> *"But the same servant went out, and found one of his fellowservants, which owed him an hundred pence: and he laid hands on him, and took him by the throat, saying, Pay me that thou owest. And his fellowservant fell down at his feet, and besought him... And he would not: but went and cast him into prison, till he should pay the debt."*

Many of Yahushua's parables deal with the two houses of Israel — Judah and Ephraim. Consider this one through that lens. Judah was the servant who received enormous mercy from the King. They returned from Babylon. They were given the temple back, the city back, the covenant back. They received the seventy-weeks mercy window Daniel prophesied. That is the ten-thousand-talent cancellation.

But Judah did not extend that mercy to their brother. Ephraim — the northern tribes scattered among the nations — was treated as lost, unclean, beyond recovery. The religious leadership of Judah built walls of exclusion rather than bridges of restoration. They received the release but refused to pass it on.

> **Matthew 18:32–35**
>
> *"Then his lord, after that he had called him, said unto him, O thou wicked servant, I forgave thee all that debt, because thou desiredst me: Shouldest not thou also have had compassion on thy fellowservant, even as I had pity on thee? And his lord was wroth, and delivered him to the tormentors, till he should pay all that was due unto him."*

The king's judgment is devastating. You received the Jubilee. You did not extend it. And now the mercy window closes. This connects directly to Daniel 9:24 — the seventy weeks were *determined*, meaning they had a deadline. The probationary passing over was not permanent. It was aimed at producing repentance and restoration. When the servant who received mercy refused to become a person of mercy, the king revoked the release.

Yahushua's "seventy times seven" is not a math problem about personal patience. It is a prophetic warning rooted in Daniel's timeline: you are living inside a mercy window. Yahuah has been passing over sins, holding judgment in trust, waiting for His people to become what they received. If you accept the release and refuse to release others, the window closes.

## Part Four — The Bridge

### 4.1 — Aphesis: The Sound of Chains Hitting the Floor

In the New Testament, the most common word for forgiveness is the Greek ***aphesis*** (ἄφεσις), and its verb form *aphiēmi* (ἀφίημι). These words literally mean *to send away, to release, to let go.* This is the same word used in the Greek Old Testament (the Septuagint) for freeing prisoners and cancelling debts.

> **Luke 4:18 (Septuagint of Isaiah 61:1)**
>
> *"...to preach aphesis to the captives..."*

When the New Testament says "forgiveness of sins," it is saying: **release from sin's claim.** The debt is cancelled. The chain is unlocked. The captive walks out. The NT writers did not invent a new spiritual word. They reached for the most concrete, transactional release word they could find — and it sounds exactly like Shemitah and Jubilee.

> **Ephesians 1:7**
>
> *"In whom we have redemption through his blood, the forgiveness of sins, according to the riches of his grace."*

Notice how Paul stacks the terms. Redemption (*apolutrōsis* — a buying-back, a ransom-release) through blood, resulting in forgiveness (*aphesis* — the sending-away of sins). The Go'el pays the price. The Jubilee trumpet sounds. The prisoner walks free. All of it in one verse.

## Part Five — The Discovery

### 5.1 — Three Things That Are Related but Not the Same

One of the biggest reasons forgiveness has lost its power in the church is that people have collapsed three separate ideas into one.

**Forgiveness (release / cancel)** — you drop the claim. You stop demanding payment. In Hebrew: *nāśāʼ* (lifting), *māḥāh* (erasing), *shāmaṭ* (releasing). In Greek: *aphesis* (sending away). This is a decision. It can happen immediately. It does not require the other person's cooperation.

**Atonement (cover / repair)** — the damage is dealt with. The stain is cleansed. The breach is repaired. In Hebrew: *kāphar.* Atonement is what makes pardon righteous — Yahuah's answer to the question, "How can guilt be erased without pretending the harm never happened?"

**Reconciliation (restored relationship)** — trust rebuilt, fellowship recovered, access renewed. Reconciliation often requires repentance, changed behaviour, and time. You can forgive someone fully and still maintain boundaries while reconciliation is in process. Releasing someone from the debt they owe you is not the same as handing them the keys to your house.

### 5.2 — Release Is Not Weakness

There is a common fear that if you truly release, you become defenceless. But the Hebrew picture is the opposite. In the Jubilee, who had the power to cancel debts? The creditor — the one who was owed. Releasing a debt is not the act of a weak person. It is the act of someone who has the power to collect and chooses not to. That is exactly what Yahuah does in *sālaḥ.*

> **Isaiah 55:7**
>
> *"Let the wicked forsake his way, and the unrighteous man his thoughts: and let him return unto Yahuah, and he will have mercy upon him; and to our God, for he will abundantly pardon."*

*Abundantly pardon* — the Hebrew is *yarbeh lis'lōḥ*, literally "he will multiply pardoning." The same God who has every right to judge chooses to pardon extravagantly. This is strength, not weakness. And it is the model His people are commanded to follow.

Forgiveness does not mean you lose wisdom. It does not mean you ignore patterns of harm. It means you stop collecting the debt through bitterness, gossip, cold shoulders, and silent scorekeeping. You release the claim. If trust needs to be rebuilt, that is reconciliation — which has its own conditions, timeline, and boundaries.

## Part Six — The Restoration

### 6.1 — What the Hebrew Actually Teaches

When you lay all six Hebrew words side by side, a picture emerges that is far bigger and far more demanding than anything the modern church teaches about forgiveness.

*Sālaḥ* teaches that true pardon is a sovereign act — a judicial clearing of guilt that belongs to Yahuah's authority (Numbers 14:19–20). *Nāśāʼ* teaches that forgiveness costs someone something — the weight gets transferred to the one who lifts (Leviticus 16:21–22). *Māḥāh* teaches that the record is erased, not filed away — and "remembering" sin means bringing the charge forward as an active accusation (Isaiah 43:25). *ʿĀvar* teaches that withheld judgment is purposeful mercy aimed at repentance (Micah 7:18). *Kāphar* teaches that forgiveness without dealing with the damage is incomplete — atonement repairs what the offence broke (Leviticus 17:11). *Shāmaṭ* and *derōr* teach that forgiveness was legislated into the calendar — and the Jubilee trumpet sounded on the Day of Atonement itself (Leviticus 25:9–10).

### 6.2 — What the Modern Church Needs to Hear

The modern church has weakened forgiveness by turning it into an emotion — a feeling, a phrase, a personal therapy strategy. But forgiveness in Scripture is a covenantal action modelled by Yahuah and commanded of His people. It has weight, cost, structure, and a calendar.

Yahuah's model is *māḥāh* — blot it out, erase the entry, never bring the charge forward again. His model is *nāśāʼ* — lift it, carry it away, let it go to a place of no return. His model is *derōr* — blow the trumpet and set the captive free. Biblical forgiveness is not the absence of consequences or boundaries. It is the **presence of release.**

### 6.3 — Conclusion

Yahuah did not leave forgiveness to human emotion. He built it into the Law, the calendar, the sacrificial system, and the very language His people spoke. He gave Israel six different words because one was never enough to capture the full weight of what it costs Him and what it gives us.

Then He sent His Son to embody all six. Yahushua is the One who lifts the burden (*nāśāʼ*), erases the record (*māḥāh*), absorbs the judgment (*ʿāvar*), makes the atonement (*kāphar*), and announces the permanent Jubilee (*derōr*). He is forgiveness with a face.

And those who follow Him are living inside the mercy window Daniel saw. The seventy-times-seven is not a number. It is a warning and an invitation. You have received a release so enormous you could never have earned it. Now become a person of release — or the window closes.

***Sh'ma Yisrael: Yahuah Eloheinu, Yahuah Echad.*** Hear, O Israel: Yahuah our God, Yahuah is one.
