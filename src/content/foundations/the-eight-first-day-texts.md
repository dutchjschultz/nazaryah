## The Claim

Most churches teach that the Sabbath was moved from the seventh day to the first. Yahushua rose on the first day of the week. The disciples met on the first day. Paul broke bread with the believers on the first day, and he told the assemblies to take up their offering on the first day. John was in the Spirit on the Lord’s day. From these passages comes the teaching that the first day became the Christian day of worship, and that Sunday is the Lord’s day in place of the old Sabbath.

It is the most widely practiced teaching in this whole series. Nearly every church building in the world opens its doors on the strength of it. It deserves to be weighed carefully, one text at a time.

## The Short Version

- The phrase “first day of the week” appears exactly eight times in the apostles’ writings.
- Five of the eight describe the same morning: the tomb found empty.
- None of the eight commands anything about the day. None blesses it, sets it apart, calls it holy, or calls it the Sabbath.
- The seventh day was instituted with a blessing, a sanctification, a commandment, and a sign. Nothing like that is ever said of the first.
- The change Scripture never made, history records being made, centuries later, by men.

## Eight Times, and Only Eight

The whole case for a new day rests on a short list. Here is every place the phrase appears:

- Matthew 28:1
- Mark 16:2
- Mark 16:9
- Luke 24:1
- John 20:1
- John 20:19
- Acts 20:7
- 1 Corinthians 16:2

That is the complete list. There is no ninth text hidden elsewhere. A reader can check all eight in a single sitting, and should.

## What the Eight Actually Describe

Sorted by what each one reports, the list is smaller than it sounds.

- Five tell of one morning. The women and the disciples came to the tomb at dawn and found it empty (Matthew 28:1; Mark 16:2, 9; Luke 24:1; John 20:1). They date a discovery. They are the same event told by four witnesses.
- One tells of that same evening. The disciples were gathered behind shut doors, and the verse gives the reason: “for fear of the Jews” (John 20:19).
- One tells of a single gathering in Troas, the night before Paul left on a journey (Acts 20:7).
- One tells believers to set money aside at home ahead of Paul’s arrival (1 Corinthians 16:2).

So the eight texts describe four occasions, and one of those occasions accounts for six of the eight. What each of the last three passages means is answered in its own testimony. The point here is the shape of the whole list.

## What None of the Eight Say

Read all eight together and look for what a new holy day would need. It is not there.

- Not one gives a command to keep the first day.
- Not one says Yahuah blessed it or sanctified it.
- Not one calls it holy.
- Not one calls it the Sabbath, or says the Sabbath moved to it.
- Not one calls it the Lord’s day.
- Not one records anyone resting on it.
- Not one says it remembers the resurrection.

Every one of those things would be needed to carry the modern teaching. All eight texts are silent on every one.

## How Yahuah Makes a Day Holy

Scripture shows exactly what it looks like when Yahuah sets a day apart. He did it once, at creation, and He did it in words that cannot be missed.

> And Elohim blessed the seventh day, and sanctified it: because that in it he had rested from all his work which Elohim created and made.
> 
> — **Genesis 2:3**

He blessed it. He sanctified it. He gave the reason. Then at Sinai He commanded it in the middle of the ten words, named which day it was, and told Israel to remember it (Exodus 20:8–11). Then He made it a sign:

> Wherefore the children of Israel shall keep the sabbath, to observe the sabbath throughout their generations, for a perpetual covenant.
> 
> — **Exodus 31:16**

And through Isaiah He called it by a name He gives no other day: “my holy day” (Isaiah 58:13).

A blessing, a sanctification, a reason, a commandment, a sign, and a name. That is how the seventh day became holy. If the holiness had been moved to another day, the move would need words at least as plain. Scripture does not contain them. A transfer that was never spoken is not a transfer.

## The Phrase Itself Points Back to the Sabbath

The Greek behind “first day of the week” is mia ton sabbaton. Mia means one. Sabbaton (G4521) is the word for Sabbath, and here it is plural. The word “day” is not in the Greek at all, which is why the King James translators set it in italics.

There are two honest ways to read the phrase.

- Sabbaton can mean a week, named after the Sabbath that closes it. The Pharisee says, “I fast twice in the week” (Luke 18:12), and the word for week there is sabbaton. On this reading, mia ton sabbaton means the first of the week.
- The plural can also be read as it stands: day one toward the Sabbaths. Torah has a count that begins on the morrow after the Sabbath, at the waving of the first sheaf, and runs seven Sabbaths toward Shavuot (Leviticus 23:11, 15–16). On this reading the phrase marks the first day of that count.

This foundation does not rest on either reading, because both lead to the same place. On the first, the week takes its very name from the Sabbath. On the second, the day is counted toward the Sabbaths. Neither reading makes the first day a Sabbath. In both, the Sabbath is still the fixed point everything else is measured from.

## The Apostles Kept the Seventh Day After the Resurrection

If the Sabbath had moved at the resurrection, the record would show the believers keeping the new day and leaving the old one. It shows the reverse.

The women who followed Yahushua rested on the Sabbath between His burial and the empty tomb, and Luke wrote it down without a word of correction, decades later:

> And they returned, and prepared spices and ointments; and rested the sabbath day according to the commandment.
> 
> — **Luke 23:56**

Years after the resurrection, Paul’s practice was the same, and Luke calls it his custom:

> And Paul, as his manner was, went in unto them, and three sabbath days reasoned with them out of the scriptures.
> 
> — **Acts 17:2**

In Corinth “he reasoned in the synagogue every sabbath, and persuaded the Jews and the Greeks” (Acts 18:4). In Antioch of Pisidia it was the people of the nations who asked to hear more “the next sabbath,” and on that Sabbath “came almost the whole city together to hear the word of Yahuah” (Acts 13:42, 44). Nobody told them to come back on the first day instead.

The book of Acts names the Sabbath again and again as the day the word was taught. It names the first day once.

## Where the Change Actually Came From

Scripture warned that a power would rise and “think to change times and laws” (Daniel 7:25). It never records the change being made by Yahuah, by Yahushua, or by any apostle.

History records it being made by men. The following facts are supporting evidence, not authority, but they are well documented.

- In the year 321, the Roman emperor Constantine ordered that courts and city trades rest on what his edict called the venerable day of the sun. It was a civil law of the empire, not a word from Scripture.
- Around the year 364, the Council of Laodicea ruled that Christians must not rest on the Sabbath like the Jews, but must work on that day and honor the first day instead.

A council does not forbid something nobody is doing. The ruling shows that believers were still keeping the seventh day three centuries after the resurrection, and that it took a church council to stop them.

So the question can be asked plainly. If the apostles moved the day, why did an emperor and a council need to move it again? The answer is that the apostles never moved it. Men did, and they did it in the open, in their own name.

## What Did Change

Saying exactly what the first day does carry keeps anyone from overstating the case.

- The resurrection is real, and the tomb was found empty on the first day. That is a true and wonderful fact of history, and nothing in this foundation takes it away.
- The risen Messiah is called “the firstfruits of them that slept” (1 Corinthians 15:20). The firstfruits sheaf was waved before Yahuah on a working day, not kept as a rest. It honored a harvest. It did not replace a Sabbath.
- Yahushua did leave His followers a remembrance, and He told them what it was: the bread and the cup, “in remembrance of me” (Luke 22:19). He gave a meal to remember Him by. He never gave a day.
- The seventh day did not change. No text moves it, and no apostle claims to.

## The Verdict

The case for a new Sabbath rests on eight verses. Five of them describe one morning, and none of them says a word about keeping a day. Against them stand a day Yahuah blessed, sanctified, commanded, called a perpetual sign, and named as His own.

Every one of the eight is answered in its own testimony. Taken together, they show what the modern teaching needs and does not have: a single sentence in which Yahuah moves His holy day. That sentence does not exist. The day was moved by an emperor and a council, three centuries after the last apostle wrote.

> Do we then make void the law through faith? God forbid: yea, we establish the law.
> 
> — **Romans 3:31**

The resurrection did not void the fourth commandment. It was the Sabbath-keeping women who first found the tomb empty.
