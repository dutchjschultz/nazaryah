## The Claim

Many teachers point out that Yahuah Himself turned against the feasts long before Messiah came. Through Isaiah He said He hated the new moons and appointed feasts. Through Amos He said He despised the feast days. Through Hosea He said He would cause the feast days and Sabbaths to cease. Through Jeremiah He said He did not command sacrifices when He brought Israel out of Egypt. Ezekiel even says Yahuah gave statutes that were not good. If Yahuah rejected these things through His own prophets, the church is only finishing what the prophets began.

These verses sound like Yahuah speaking against His own law. The question is what kind of speech they are.

## The Point in One Sentence

When a prophet says Yahuah rejects a people’s worship, he is passing judgment on the worshipper, not repealing the worship.

## The Short Version

- Scripture uses the same rejection language against prayer, and no one believes prayer was repealed.
- The same psalm that says Yahuah does not desire sacrifice says He will be pleased with it.
- Yahushua taught the same order: be reconciled first, then bring the gift.
- A threat to take something away proves it still mattered.
- The removal the prophets threatened really happened, and afterward the feasts and Sabbaths were restored.

## Rejection Language Is Used Against Prayer Too

Proverbs states the principle twice, once about offerings and once about prayer:

> The sacrifice of the wicked is an abomination to Yahuah: but the prayer of the upright is his delight.
> 
> — **Proverbs 15:8**

> He that turneth away his ear from hearing the law, even his prayer shall be abomination.
> 
> — **Proverbs 28:9**

Even his prayer shall be abomination. If rejection language repealed whatever it rejected, prayer would have been repealed by Proverbs 28:9. No one reads it that way. Everyone understands that the problem is the man praying, not prayer. The prophets’ words about feasts and offerings are the same kind of speech.

## The Same Psalm Says Both

David wrote, after his sin with Bathsheba:

> For thou desirest not sacrifice; else would I give it: thou delightest not in burnt offering. The sacrifices of Elohim are a broken spirit: a broken and a contrite heart, O Elohim, thou wilt not despise.
> 
> — **Psalm 51:16–17**

Taken alone, that sounds like the end of the offerings. But two verses later the same psalm says:

> Then shalt thou be pleased with the sacrifices of righteousness, with burnt offering and whole burnt offering: then shall they offer bullocks upon thine altar.
> 
> — **Psalm 51:19**

Not desired, and then pleasing, in one psalm. The difference is the heart of the one bringing it. “Thou desirest not” is about the offering of a man who has not repented. “Then shalt thou be pleased” is about the offering of a man who has.

## Malachi Shows the Same Turn

Malachi delivers the same kind of rejection: “I have no pleasure in you, saith Yahuah of hosts, neither will I accept an offering at your hand” (Malachi 1:10). Then, after the refining, the same book says: “Then shall the offering of Judah and Jerusalem be pleasant unto Yahuah, as in the days of old, and as in former years” (Malachi 3:4). Rejected, then pleasant again. What changed was the people, not the instruction.

## Yahushua Taught the Same Order

> Therefore if thou bring thy gift to the altar, and there rememberest that thy brother hath ought against thee; Leave there thy gift before the altar, and go thy way; first be reconciled to thy brother, and then come and offer thy gift.
> 
> — **Matthew 5:23–24**

Leave the gift, be reconciled, then come and offer. Yahushua did not say the gift was worthless. He said it comes second. This is the prophets’ point exactly: obedience and justice first, worship from clean hands after. “To obey is better than sacrifice” (1 Samuel 15:22) ranks the two. It does not delete either one.

## A Threat of Removal Proves the Thing Mattered

No one threatens to take away something worthless. A father who tells a disobedient son that the family table will be closed to him is not saying the table is bad. He is saying it is precious, and that the son has made himself unfit for it. When Yahuah threatens to make the feasts and Sabbaths cease, the threat only has force because they still belonged to Him and still mattered to His people.

## The Removal Came, and So Did the Restoration

The threat was carried out. After Jerusalem fell, Jeremiah wrote: “Yahuah hath caused the solemn feasts and sabbaths to be forgotten in Zion” (Lamentations 2:6). If the prophetic warnings had been a repeal, the story would end there.

It does not end there. When the exiles came home, the first thing they rebuilt was the altar, and then: “They kept also the feast of tabernacles, as it is written … and afterward offered the continual burnt offering, both of the new moons, and of all the set feasts of Yahuah that were consecrated” (Ezra 3:4–5). Under Nehemiah they kept Sukkot again with great joy (Nehemiah 8:14–17), and Nehemiah shut the city gates to protect the Sabbath (Nehemiah 13:19).

The feasts were taken away as judgment and given back as restoration. A repealed law is not restored by Yahuah after the punishment ends.

## What This Foundation Does Not Claim

- It does not claim that animal offerings continue today. What changed in the offering is set out in F-06. The offerings appear here only because the prophets’ logic about them is the same logic they use for the feasts.
- It does not settle the meaning of each indicting verse. Isaiah 1, Amos 5, Hosea 2, Jeremiah 7, Ezekiel 20, and Psalm 40 each have their own testimony.
- It claims this: prophetic rejection is judgment on the worshipper. Scripture shows it applied to prayer, reversed after repentance, and followed by restoration, none of which fits a repeal.

## The Verdict

The modern teaching hears Yahuah say “I hate your feasts” and concludes He was done with them. Scripture shows the same language used against prayer, the same psalm calling sacrifice both unwanted and pleasing, Malachi turning rejection into acceptance, Yahushua putting reconciliation before the gift, and the feasts removed in judgment and restored after the exile. Judgment on the worshipper is not the retirement of the worship. The passages that rest here are answered one at a time in their own testimonies.

> Do we then make void the law through faith? God forbid: yea, we establish the law.
> 
> — **Romans 3:31**

Yahuah rejected the hands, never the feast. When the hands were washed, the feast came back.
