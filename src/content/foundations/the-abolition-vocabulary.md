## The Claim

Most churches teach that the apostles said in plain words that the Law is gone. Paul says Messiah abolished in His flesh the law of commandments. He says the glory of Moses’ ministry was done away. He says Messiah is the end of the law. Hebrews says the priesthood was changed and with it the law. Paul says the handwriting of ordinances was blotted out and nailed to the cross. And Yahushua said, “It is finished.” Abolished, done away, end, changed, blotted out, finished. How many ways does Scripture have to say it?

The list is strong in English. The question is what the words say in the language the apostles wrote.

## The Short Version

- Five Greek words carry nearly the whole abolition case: katargeo, telos, pleroo, metathesis, and exaleipho.
- Each one is used elsewhere in Scripture, in places no one disputes. Those uses show what the word means.
- Not one of the five means repealed.
- There is one word in the debate that does mean set aside. Scripture names exactly what it sets aside, and it is not the Torah.

## Katargeo: Rendered Idle

Katargeo (G2673) is built from kata and argos, idle or not working. It means to make inoperative, to strip of power, to make something stop working on someone. It is the word behind “abolished” in Ephesians 2:15 and “done away” in 2 Corinthians 3. Look at how it is used where no one is arguing about the law:

- “Who hath abolished death” (2 Timothy 1:10). Believers still die. Death was not repealed. It was stripped of its power over them.
- “That through death he might destroy him that had the power of death, that is, the devil” (Hebrews 2:14). Yet the devil still “walketh about, seeking whom he may devour” (1 Peter 5:8). He was not erased. His hold was broken.
- “Cut it down; why cumbereth it the ground?” (Luke 13:7). The barren fig tree was making the ground idle. The ground still existed.

And the most important use of all is in the series’ own anchor verse. When Paul asks, “Do we then make void the law through faith?” the word for make void is katargeo. His answer: “God forbid.” Paul uses the abolition word to deny that the law has been abolished. Whatever katargeo does in Ephesians or Corinthians, it cannot be what Paul says belief never does to the law.

## Telos: The Goal

Telos (G5056) is the word behind “the end of the law” in Romans 10:4. It can mean a stopping point. It also means a goal, an aim, the place something is headed. Scripture uses it both ways, and the context decides.

- “Now the end of the commandment is charity out of a pure heart, and of a good conscience, and of faith unfeigned” (1 Timothy 1:5). The end of the commandment is its goal. No one reads this as the termination of the commandment.
- “Receiving the end of your faith, even the salvation of your souls” (1 Peter 1:9). The end of belief is its outcome, not its cancellation.
- “Of his kingdom there shall be no end” (Luke 1:33). Here telos does mean termination.

Honesty requires stating it plainly: telos can mean either one. The word alone does not prove the goal reading in Romans 10:4. What it does prove is that “end” in English does not settle the question. The Romans 10:4 testimony shows from the context which meaning Paul intended.

## Pleroo: Filled Full

Pleroo (G4137) means to fill, to fill up to the full, to bring to full measure. It is the word behind “fulfil” in Matthew 5:17 and in Paul’s statements that love fulfils the law. The modern teaching reads fulfil as finish, like a contract that is fulfilled and then closed. Scripture uses the word otherwise:

- “Suffer it to be so now: for thus it becometh us to fulfil all righteousness” (Matthew 3:15). Yahushua said this at His immersion. He fulfilled all righteousness. No one believes righteousness was cancelled that day.
- “Fill ye up then the measure of your fathers” (Matthew 23:32). The leaders filled up the measure of their fathers’ sin. Filling did not end it. It brought it to full measure.

To fulfil something is to fill it full, to give it its complete expression. A vessel filled to the brim is not destroyed. It is finally holding all it was made to hold.

## Metathesis: Transferred

Metathesis (G3331) is the word behind “change” in Hebrews 7:12: “the priesthood being changed, there is made of necessity a change also of the law.” It means a transfer, a moving from one place to another. The same writer uses it a few chapters later:

> By faith Enoch was translated that he should not see death; and was not found, because Yahuah had translated him: for before his translation he had this testimony, that he pleased Yahuah.
> 
> — **Hebrews 11:5**

Translation there is metathesis. Enoch was not abolished. He was moved. Honesty requires adding that the word can also describe the removal of something (Hebrews 12:27). In Hebrews 7, the context names what was moved: the priesthood, from the tribe of Levi to Messiah of the tribe of Judah (F-06). The Hebrews 7 testimony carries the full case.

## Exaleipho: Wiped Clean

Exaleipho (G1813) means to wipe out, to blot out, to wipe clean. It is the word behind “blotting out” in Colossians 2:14. Everywhere else it appears, look at what is wiped:

- “That your sins may be blotted out” (Acts 3:19). Sins.
- “I will not blot out his name out of the book of life” (Revelation 3:5). A name in a written record.
- “And Yahuah shall wipe away all tears from their eyes” (Revelation 21:4). Tears.

The word wipes away records of guilt, names from a book, and tears from a face. It is never used anywhere else for wiping out a commandment. What exactly was written in the record of Colossians 2:14 is answered in its own testimony.

## The One Word That Does Mean Set Aside

A study that claimed no word in this debate ever means set aside would not survive a careful reader. There is one. Hebrews 7:18 speaks of “a disannulling of the commandment going before.” The word is athetesis (G115), and it does mean annulment, a setting aside.

But Hebrews names the commandment it means. Two verses earlier it describes the old priests as made “after the law of a carnal commandment” (Hebrews 7:16), the commandment that tied the priesthood to physical descent from Levi. That is the commandment set aside: the rule about who may be priest. The same word appears once more in Hebrews, and again its object is named: Messiah appeared “to put away sin by the sacrifice of himself” (Hebrews 9:26). Sin is set aside. The priesthood rule is set aside. The Torah as a whole is never the object.

## A Sixth Word: Finished

“It is finished” (John 19:30) is one Greek word, tetelestai, from teleo (G5055): to complete, to accomplish, to carry through to the end. Yahushua had said what would be accomplished: “all things that are written by the prophets concerning the Son of man shall be accomplished” (Luke 18:31). Accomplished there is teleo. What was finished at the tree was the work written about Him. A finished assignment does not abolish the One who gave it, or the words He gave.

## The Verdict

In English, the abolition vocabulary sounds overwhelming. In Greek, the same words mean rendered powerless, goal, filled full, transferred, and wiped clean. Each is used elsewhere in Scripture in ways no one disputes, and not one of those uses means repealed. The one word that does mean set aside names its object, and the object is a priesthood rule and sin, not the Torah.

Every passage that rests on this foundation is answered one at a time in its own testimony, where the context shows how each word is working.

And the strongest evidence is the anchor verse itself. Paul uses katargeo, the word translated abolished, and answers with an oath:

> Do we then make void the law through faith? God forbid: yea, we establish the law.
> 
> — **Romans 3:31**

The apostle who wrote the abolition vocabulary used it to say the law was not abolished.
