## The Claim

Most churches teach that everything changed at the cross. Yahushua cried, “It is finished.” The veil of the temple tore from top to bottom. Hebrews says the priesthood was changed and that there is “of necessity a change also of the law.” Paul says the handwriting of ordinances was nailed to the cross. Put together, the cross was the end of the old order, and the Torah belonged to the old order.

The claim rests on something true. Real things did change at the tree, and Scripture says so plainly. The question this foundation answers is which things.

## The Short Version

- Scripture names what changed. It is a short list: the priest, the offering, the penalty, and access to Yahuah’s presence.
- Each change was foretold in Scripture written long before the tree.
- None of the changes touches the commandments for daily life: what is sin, what is food, which day is holy, how to treat a neighbor.
- The abolition case lives on the vague phrase “everything changed.” Once the list is named, nothing is left for that phrase to carry.

## Why the List Matters

A vague claim cannot be answered, only a specific one. “Everything changed” sounds like a fact, but it is never proved one item at a time. When Scripture is asked what changed, it gives a short and precise answer. Anything that is not on that list did not change, because no one has the authority to add to it (Deuteronomy 4:2; see F-09).

## Change One: The Priest

Under the Torah, the priesthood belonged to one family of one tribe: Aaron and his sons, of Levi (Exodus 28:1; Numbers 3:10). The Messiah came from Judah, the royal tribe (Genesis 49:10), a tribe the Torah never assigned to the altar.

This was not a surprise sprung at the tree. David announced it a thousand years earlier, while the Levitical priesthood was still serving:

> Yahuah hath sworn, and will not repent, Thou art a priest for ever after the order of Melchizedek.
> 
> — **Psalm 110:4**

A priest forever, of a different order, confirmed by oath. Hebrews builds its whole argument on this verse, and calls the shift of priesthood a change in the law that governs who may serve as priest (Hebrews 7:12). That verse is answered in its own testimony. The point here is the scope: what changed was the priest, the one who stands between Yahuah and the people. The Ten Commandments say nothing about which tribe serves at the altar, and they were not touched by the change.

## Change Two: The Offering

Under the Torah, sin offerings were animals brought to the altar again and again. Isaiah foresaw one offering of a different kind, a man:

> Yet it pleased Yahuah to bruise him; he hath put him to grief: when thou shalt make his soul an offering for sin, he shall see his seed.
> 
> — **Isaiah 53:10**

John the Immerser pointed at Him and said, “Behold the Lamb of Yahuah, which taketh away the sin of the world” (John 1:29). Peter wrote that He “hath once suffered for sins, the just for the unjust, that he might bring us to Yahuah” (1 Peter 3:18). Once.

Even before the tree, Scripture never placed the animal offerings at the center. “To obey is better than sacrifice” (1 Samuel 15:22). “For I desired mercy, and not sacrifice; and the knowledge of Elohim more than burnt offerings” (Hosea 6:6). The sin offering pointed to the One who would bear sin. When He came, the pointer had arrived. Obedience, which was always better than sacrifice, did not go anywhere.

## Change Three: The Penalty

The Torah carried a sentence of death for sin, and a curse on the man hanged on a tree: “for he that is hanged is accursed of Elohim” (Deuteronomy 21:23). At the tree, the sentence owed by the guilty was laid on the innocent.

> All we like sheep have gone astray; we have turned every one to his own way; and Yahuah hath laid on him the iniquity of us all.
> 
> — **Isaiah 53:6**

“Who his own self bare our sins in his own body on the tree, that we, being dead to sins, should live unto righteousness” (1 Peter 2:24). The penalty was borne, so the one who trusts Him no longer faces it. How the believer moves out from under the sentence is set out in full in F-02.

Notice again what was carried: the penalty. A penalty presupposes a law. What was taken away at the tree was the punishment for breaking the commandments, not the commandments themselves.

## Change Four: Access

At the moment of His death, the record says, “the veil of the temple was rent in twain from the top to the bottom” (Mark 15:38). From the top: torn from above, not by men below. Hebrews explains what it meant: believers have “boldness to enter into the holiest by the blood of Yahushua, by a new and living way, which he hath consecrated for us, through the veil, that is to say, his flesh” (Hebrews 10:19–20).

Honesty requires a note. The temple had more than one curtain, and which one tore is debated. The point here does not rest on that question. Whichever curtain it was, the meaning given in Scripture is access: the way into Yahuah’s presence was opened. A door opening is not a law being repealed.

## What Did Not Change

Set against that list, everything else stands where it stood.

- Yahuah did not change. “For I am Yahuah, I change not” (Malachi 3:6).
- The definition of sin did not change. “Sin is the transgression of the law” (1 John 3:4), written decades after the tree.
- The definition of food did not change (F-10).
- The holy day did not change (F-11).
- The command to love Yahuah and neighbor did not change. Both were quoted by Yahushua straight out of Torah (Deuteronomy 6:5; Leviticus 19:18).
- The standing of the Torah until heaven and earth pass did not change (F-13).

Every one of these was in force before the tree, and every one is still described as in force after it.

## The Verdict

The cross was the greatest event in history, and the modern teaching is right that it changed things. It is wrong about which things. Scripture names them: a new priest of a new order, one offering for all, a penalty carried by the innocent, and a way opened into the holiest. Each was promised long before it happened. None of them touches the commandments by which a man is told how to live.

The passages that rest on this foundation are answered one at a time in their own testimonies. Each can be met with one question: which of the named changes is this verse describing?

> Do we then make void the law through faith? God forbid: yea, we establish the law.
> 
> — **Romans 3:31**

The tree lifted the law’s penalty from those who trust Him. What it established is the law itself.
