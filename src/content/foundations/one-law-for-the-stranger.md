## The Claim

Most churches teach that the Torah was given to Israel and to Israel alone. The nations were never under it. When the gospel went out to the Gentiles, the apostles made that plain: they would not be made to carry a yoke the Jews themselves could not bear. Gentile believers received four simple rules and nothing more. Circumcision was nothing. The wall between Jew and Gentile came down, and with it the law that divided them. Torah keeping is for Jewish believers who choose it; a Gentile who takes it up is putting on clothes that were never cut for him.

The teaching rests on a picture of two peoples with two sets of rules. The question is whether Scripture ever drew that picture.

## The Point in One Sentence

The Torah never had a separate law for the outsider who joined Yahuah’s people; it gave the stranger the same law as the native born, and the nations brought near in Messiah are brought into that same household.

## The Short Version

- From the night Israel left Egypt, outsiders came with them.
- The Torah says, more than once, that there is one law for the native born and for the stranger.
- The prophets promise that strangers who join themselves to Yahuah and keep His Sabbath will be welcomed into His house.
- The apostles describe believers from the nations as fellow citizens, grafted into the same tree, and Abraham’s seed.
- Two peoples with two laws is a picture Scripture never paints.

## Outsiders Were There From the Start

Israel did not leave Egypt alone: “and a mixed multitude went up also with them” (Exodus 12:38). People of other nations walked out with Israel, stood at Sinai with Israel, and received the Torah with Israel. From the first day, the people of Yahuah included those born outside the family.

The Hebrew word for these people is ger (H1616): a stranger, a sojourner, a foreigner living among Israel. The Torah mentions the ger again and again, and it never treats him as someone living under a different rulebook.

## One Law, Said in So Many Words

The Torah states the principle directly, and it states it more than once:

> One law shall be to him that is homeborn, and unto the stranger that sojourneth among you.
> 
> — **Exodus 12:49**

> One ordinance shall be both for you of the congregation, and also for the stranger that sojourneth with you, an ordinance for ever in your generations: as ye are, so shall the stranger be before Yahuah. One law and one manner shall be for you, and for the stranger that sojourneth with you.
> 
> — **Numbers 15:15–16**

As ye are, so shall the stranger be. And it is “an ordinance for ever.”

### The Stranger Named in the Commandments

The stranger is not mentioned only in general statements. He is named inside specific commandments:

- The Sabbath: “nor thy stranger that is within thy gates” (Exodus 20:10).
- Yom Kippur: “do no work at all, whether it be one of your own country, or a stranger that sojourneth among you” (Leviticus 16:29).
- Blood: “whatsoever man there be of the house of Israel, or of the strangers that sojourn among you, that eateth any manner of blood” (Leviticus 17:10).
- Moral purity: “neither any of your own nation, nor any stranger that sojourneth among you” (Leviticus 18:26).

A Torah written only for ethnic Israel would have no reason to name the stranger in its commandments. This one names him over and over.

## The Prophets Promised the Stranger a Place

Isaiah addresses the outsider directly, and he describes exactly what the stranger who joins Yahuah does:

> Also the sons of the stranger, that join themselves to Yahuah, to serve him, and to love the name of Yahuah, to be his servants, every one that keepeth the sabbath from polluting it, and taketh hold of my covenant; Even them will I bring to my holy mountain, and make them joyful in my house of prayer … for mine house shall be called an house of prayer for all people.
> 
> — **Isaiah 56:6–7**

The stranger keeps the Sabbath and takes hold of the covenant, and Yahuah brings him into His house. Ezekiel goes further still. In the land to come, the strangers who sojourn among Israel “shall be unto you as born in the country among the children of Israel” and receive an inheritance among the tribes (Ezekiel 47:22). Ruth said it in a single line: “thy people shall be my people, and thy God my God” (Ruth 1:16).

## The Apostles Used the Same Picture

When the apostles describe believers from the nations, they never describe a second people. They describe outsiders brought into the one people that already existed.

> Now therefore ye are no more strangers and foreigners, but fellowcitizens with the saints, and of the household of Yahuah.
> 
> — **Ephesians 2:19**

- Fellow citizens of an existing commonwealth, and members of an existing household.
- Grafted in: “thou, being a wild olive tree, wert graffed in among them, and with them partakest of the root and fatness of the olive tree … thou bearest not the root, but the root thee” (Romans 11:17–18). One tree, one root.
- “And if ye be Messiah’s, then are ye Abraham’s seed, and heirs according to the promise” (Galatians 3:29).

A citizen lives by the laws of the commonwealth he joins. A branch draws from the root it is grafted into. An heir takes the name and the ways of the house. None of these pictures leaves room for a second law.

## What This Foundation Does Not Claim

- It does not claim that every stranger law applies in the same way today. Some, like those tied to the altar, belong to what changed (F-06).
- It does not settle the circumcision question. The Torah required a stranger to be circumcised to eat the Pesach (Exodus 12:48), and how that applies to the nations now is answered in the circumcision testimonies, including Acts 15.
- It claims this: Scripture never gives the nations a separate, lighter law. The stranger who joins Yahuah’s people lives under the one law given to all of them.

## The Verdict

The modern teaching divides Yahuah’s people into two peoples with two laws. Scripture never does. From the mixed multitude at the Exodus to the stranger in Isaiah who keeps the Sabbath, from “one law” in Exodus to “fellowcitizens” in Ephesians, there is one household, one root, and one law for the native born and the stranger alike. The passages that rest on this foundation, chiefly Acts 10, Acts 15, and Ephesians 2, are answered one at a time in their own testimonies.

> Do we then make void the law through faith? God forbid: yea, we establish the law.
> 
> — **Romans 3:31**

The wall came down so the stranger could come in, not so the house could lose its order.
