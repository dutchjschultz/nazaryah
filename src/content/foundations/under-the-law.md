## The Claim

Most churches teach that “under the law” describes a way of life. A person under the law is a person who keeps the Torah: rests on the Sabbath, keeps the feasts, avoids unclean food. Paul told believers, “ye are not under the law” (Romans 6:14). So a believer who keeps the Torah has climbed back under something Paul said he was freed from.

On this reading, every verse about being under the law becomes a warning against obedience. The more a believer keeps Yahuah’s commandments, the more he is under the law, and the further he has drifted from the favor of Messiah.

## The Short Version

- In Scripture, to be under something is to be under its power. It is a position, not a habit.
- A person under the law stands within its jurisdiction and answerable to its verdict. For a sinner, that verdict is death.
- The law holds two kinds of authority: to direct a man’s life and to condemn him for breaking it. Scripture shows the believer released from the second, never from the first.
- Messiah took that verdict. The believer is moved out from under the sentence.
- A pardon removes a man from his sentence. It does not repeal the statute he broke.

## The Whole Case in One Courtroom

This study follows a single picture from start to finish: a courtroom. Every point below is one step in that courtroom, taken in order.

- Step One: every person is brought into court.
- Step Two: the verdict is guilty, and the sentence is death.
- Step Three: “under the law” names where the guilty man stands, under that sentence.
- Step Four: Messiah carries the sentence in his place.
- Step Five: the pardoned man walks out, and the law stays on the books.
- Step Six: the pardoned man now lives inside the law as a free man.

Keep the courtroom in mind, and each step follows from the one before it.

## Step One: Every Person Is Brought Into Court

The first thing the law does is call the whole world to the bench.

> Now we know that what things soever the law saith, it saith to them who are under the law: that every mouth may be stopped, and all the world may become guilty before Yahuah.
> 
> — **Romans 3:19**

The word guilty is hupodikos (G5267), built from hupo, under, and dike, justice or judgment. It means answerable to the court, liable to its sentence. It is the position of a defendant. The law’s work in this verse is a courtroom work: it stops every mouth and leaves the whole world standing before the bench.

No one is exempt, because “all have sinned” (Romans 3:23).

## Step Two: The Sentence Is Death

A court that finds a man guilty pronounces a sentence. This one was pronounced long ago.

It was spoken in the garden: “in the day that thou eatest thereof thou shalt surely die” (Genesis 2:17). The prophet repeats it: “the soul that sinneth, it shall die” (Ezekiel 18:4). Paul states it plainly: “the wages of sin is death” (Romans 6:23). And Moses wrote the curse into the Torah itself: “Cursed be he that confirmeth not all the words of this law to do them” (Deuteronomy 27:26).

Yahushua spoke of that sentence as something already resting on a man until it is lifted:

> He that believeth on the Son hath everlasting life: and he that believeth not the Son shall not see life; but the wrath of Yahuah abideth on him.
> 
> — **John 3:36**

Abideth. The sentence is already in place, and it remains.

## Step Three: “Under the Law” Names Where the Guilty Man Stands

This is the step the modern teaching misses. “Under the law” is not a description of how a man lives. It is a description of where he stands in that courtroom.

The Greek word for under is hupo (G5259). It marks whose power a person is standing beneath. Paul uses the same pattern for several powers, and none of them describes a lifestyle:

- Under sin. “We have before proved both Jews and Gentiles, that they are all under sin” (Romans 3:9). “The scripture hath concluded all under sin” (Galatians 3:22).
- Sold under sin. “For we know that the law is spiritual: but I am carnal, sold under sin” (Romans 7:14).
- Under guardians. An heir “is under tutors and governors until the time appointed of the father” (Galatians 4:2).
- Under the elements. “We, when we were children, were in bondage under the elements of the world” (Galatians 4:3).

Being under sin does not mean practicing a religion called sin. It means sin holds the power. The phrase “under the law” works the same way. It means standing within the law’s jurisdiction, answerable to its verdict, with its sentence hanging over the head.

So yes, “under the law” does mean under the law’s authority. But the law holds two kinds of authority, and it matters which one is meant. It has authority to direct, to tell a man how to live. And it has authority to condemn, to pass sentence on the man who does not. A judge on the bench uses the second kind. The question every “under the law” verse raises is which kind of authority the believer has been taken out from under. The section after Step Six answers that from Scripture.

Romans 7:14 puts both in one sentence. The law is spiritual. The man is sold under sin. The problem is never placed in the law. It is placed in the man and the power holding him.

## Step Four: Messiah Carries the Sentence

The sentence was not waived. A just court cannot simply waive it. It was carried.

> But he was wounded for our transgressions, he was bruised for our iniquities: the chastisement of our peace was upon him; and with his stripes we are healed.
> 
> — **Isaiah 53:5**

Peter applies the same words to the Messiah: “Who his own self bare our sins in his own body on the tree, that we, being dead to sins, should live unto righteousness” (1 Peter 2:24). The sentence was carried away so that the man would stop doing what earned it.

Yahushua described the result in courtroom words:

> Verily, verily, I say unto you, He that heareth my word, and believeth on him that sent me, hath everlasting life, and shall not come into condemnation; but is passed from death unto life.
> 
> — **John 5:24**

Shall not come into condemnation. Passed from death unto life. The man who was facing a sentence no longer faces it. Notice also who does what. The Son speaks; the believer trusts the One who sent Him. Two are named, the Sender and the Sent, and the passage keeps them distinct.

## Step Five: The Pardoned Man Walks Out, and the Law Stays on the Books

Here the modern teaching draws its conclusion, and here it goes wrong.

Every courtroom knows the difference between a man’s sentence and the law he broke. A governor can pardon a thief. The pardon removes the sentence. It does not strike the law against theft from the books, and it does not give the pardoned man permission to steal again. If he steals the next week, he has broken the same law.

Paul faced the modern conclusion head-on and refused it:

> What shall we say then? Shall we continue in sin, that grace may abound? God forbid. How shall we, that are dead to sin, live any longer therein?
> 
> — **Romans 6:1–2**

And sin still has a definition: “sin is the transgression of the law” (1 John 3:4). If the law were gone, sin would have no measure, and Paul’s question would have no meaning. The question only makes sense because the law still defines what the pardoned man must not go back to.

## Step Six: The Pardoned Man Lives Inside the Law

Walking out from under the sentence does not leave a man outside every law. Paul says where he stands now:

> To them that are without law, as without law, (being not without law to Yahuah, but under the law to Messiah,) that I might gain them that are without law.
> 
> — **1 Corinthians 9:21**

The words “under the law to Messiah” are not the phrase hupo nomon at all. The Greek is ennomos (G1772): in-lawed, within the law. The King James renders two different Greek expressions with the same English words, and that has hidden the distinction for centuries. Paul was not under the law as a condemned man. He was inside it as a free one.

David described the same freedom a thousand years earlier: “And I will walk at liberty: for I seek thy precepts” (Psalm 119:45). Liberty and the precepts, in one line.

## Answering the Obvious Objection

A careful reader will push back here. “Under the law means under its authority. I am not under its authority at all, commandments included.” That objection deserves a real answer, and Scripture gives three.

### First: Paul Was Not Under the Law, and Not Without Law

Paul told believers they are “not under the law” (Romans 6:14). The same Paul said he was “not without law to Yahuah” but in-lawed to Messiah (1 Corinthians 9:21). Both statements are Paul’s, and both describe the believer. If “not under the law” meant free from every kind of the law’s authority, Paul would be without law, and he flatly denies that he is. So the authority he left cannot be the law’s authority to direct. It has to be something else.

### Second: Believers Can Still Sin

John writes to believers: “If we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves” (1 John 1:8). And he defines what sin is: “sin is the transgression of the law” (1 John 3:4). A man cannot transgress a law that has no authority over him. A citizen of one country cannot break the traffic laws of another country he has never entered. If believers can still sin, and they can, then the law still has authority to say what is right and wrong for them.

### Third: What the Believer Escapes Is Named

Scripture says exactly what the believer is taken out from under, and it is never the commandment. It is condemnation. “He that heareth my word, and believeth on him that sent me … shall not come into condemnation” (John 5:24). And when the believer does sin, John does not say the law no longer applies. He says the believer has a defender in court:

> My little children, these things write I unto you, that ye sin not. And if any man sin, we have an advocate with the Father, Yahushua Messiah the righteous.
> 
> — **1 John 2:1**

An advocate is a defense attorney. Nobody needs a defense attorney unless a law is still in force and a charge can still be brought. The believer is not outside the courtroom. He is in it, with a defender, and the sentence no longer falls on him. The Advocate stands with the Father, not as the Father, two persons and not one.

### Putting the Three Together

Paul is not without law. Believers can still sin against the law. And what they escape is named: condemnation, with an advocate standing for them. There is only one reading that fits all three. The believer is still under the law’s authority to direct. He has been taken out from under its authority to condemn. That is what “not under the law” means, and it is why this foundation calls it the sentence.

## What This Foundation Does Not Claim

Stating the limits keeps the case honest.

- It does not claim that “under the law” always means condemned. The phrase means standing within the law’s jurisdiction, answerable to its verdict. Messiah Himself was born within that jurisdiction, and He had no guilt of His own. For a sinner, standing there means facing the sentence.
- It does not claim that the believer is outside every law. Paul says the opposite.
- It claims this: being moved out from under the law’s sentence is not the same as being released from its commandments. The verses that say the first do not say the second.

## The Verdict

The modern teaching reads “under the law” as a lifestyle and “not under the law” as permission to leave the commandments. Scripture reads it as a position in the courtroom. Every person is brought in, found guilty, and sentenced. Messiah carries the sentence. The believer walks out pardoned, and lives inside the law as a free man.

The passages that rest on this foundation are answered one at a time in their own testimonies. All of them can be read with the same question: is the verse speaking of the commandment, or of the sentence?

> Do we then make void the law through faith? God forbid: yea, we establish the law.
> 
> — **Romans 3:31**

The pardon establishes the law. A pardon only means something because the law it pardons is real.
