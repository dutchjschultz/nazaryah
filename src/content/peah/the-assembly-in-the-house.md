Paul writes from prison to Philemon, "our dearly beloved, and fellowlabourer," to Apphia and Archippus, and to "the church in thy house" (Philemon 1-2). The word is ekklesia, a called-out assembly. It is a gathering of people, and here it fits inside one family's home. Today "church" usually means a building, or a new body that took Israel's place. Paul's word means neither.

That was the normal pattern. Paul greets "the church that is in their house" at Rome (Romans 16:5), and "the church which is in his house" at Laodicea (Colossians 4:15). The assembly was the people, not the walls around them.

Nor was the assembly a new invention. Stephen used the same word for Israel at Sinai: "the church in the wilderness" (Acts 7:38). That assembly received "the lively oracles" (Acts 7:38), the words of Yahuah given on the mount. The assembly at Philemon's house had the same word for its name and the same oracles to live by.

Paul tells Gentile believers the same thing in plain terms. They are "no more strangers and foreigners, but fellowcitizens with the saints" (Ephesians 2:19). They are wild branches "graffed in among them" (Romans 11:17), grafted into Israel's tree, not planted as a new tree beside it.

So the believers meeting in Philemon's house did not replace Israel. They had been called into the same assembly Yahuah called out of Egypt. The city at the end of the story agrees. Its twelve gates carry "the names of the twelve tribes of the children of Israel" (Revelation 21:12).
