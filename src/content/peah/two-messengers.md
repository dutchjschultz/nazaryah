Most readers pass over the end of Nahum 2: "the voice of thy messengers shall no more be heard" (Nahum 2:13). These were Assyria's envoys, and Judah had heard them before.

One of them stood at Jerusalem's wall and asked who could stop the king, "that Yahuah should deliver Jerusalem out of mine hand?" (2 Kings 18:35). Yahuah answered through Isaiah: "By thy messengers thou hast reproached the Lord" (2 Kings 19:23). That night "the angel of Yahuah went out, and smote in the camp of the Assyrians an hundred fourscore and five thousand" (2 Kings 19:35). The word for angel is the word for messenger. Assyria's messengers mocked, and Yahuah's messenger answered.

Nahum sets the two side by side. Assyria's messengers are silenced (Nahum 2:13). Yahuah's messenger stands "upon the mountains" with "good tidings" of peace (Nahum 1:15).

The prophets explain why only one voice lasts: "the word of our God shall stand for ever" (Isaiah 40:8). Yahushua said, "Heaven and earth shall pass away, but my words shall not pass away" (Matthew 24:35).

Nahum is often read as old news about Assyria with nothing for today. Peter takes Isaiah's line and applies it to his readers: "the word of the Lord endureth for ever. And this is the word which by the gospel is preached unto you" (1 Peter 1:25). The contest between the two voices was not only for Nahum's day.

The loud voices that mock Yahuah's word come to an end. The word of peace keeps going. Revelation shows a messenger "having the everlasting gospel," and right behind him another who cries, "Babylon is fallen" (Revelation 14:6, 8).
