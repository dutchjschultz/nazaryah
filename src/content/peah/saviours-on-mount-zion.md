Obadiah ends with Jacob's house as "a fire" and Esau's as "stubble," and Israel's captives possessing the land again (Obadiah 1:18-20). Then the last line: "And saviours shall come up on mount Zion to judge the mount of Esau; and the kingdom shall be Yahuah's" (Obadiah 1:21). The word is plural. Yahuah raises up saviours, and the kingdom still belongs to Him. The verse is a quiet answer to the claim that whoever saves must be God.

Yahuah alone is Savior: "beside me there is no saviour" (Isaiah 43:11). Yet He sends men to save for Him. "Thou gavest them saviours, who saved them out of the hand of their enemies" (Nehemiah 9:27). Each one was sent by Yahuah. None of them shared His throne.

Yahushua spoke of Himself as one sent: "I came down from heaven, not to do mine own will, but the will of him that sent me" (John 6:38).

Peter said who gave Him that place: "God hath made that same Yahushua, whom ye have crucified, both Master and Messiah" (Acts 2:36). One who is made Master by God is not that God.

The usual escape is that the Son's kingdom proves He is God. Paul answers it. At the end the Son "shall have delivered up the kingdom to God, even the Father," and "the Son also himself" shall be subject, "that God may be all in all" (1 Corinthians 15:24, 28). That is exactly what Obadiah says: the kingdom shall be Yahuah's.

The conclusion is plain. Yahuah saves, and He sends the one who brings the deliverance. The Son provided the way, and the kingdom returns to the Father. Heaven announces it in the same order: "the kingdoms of our Lord, and of his Messiah" (Revelation 11:15).
