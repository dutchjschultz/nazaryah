Acts 15 opens with one question. Certain men came down from Judaea and taught the brethren, "Except ye be circumcised after the manner of Moses, ye cannot be saved" (Acts 15:1). The dispute was not whether Gentile believers should obey Yahuah. The dispute was what a man must do to be saved.

Peter answered that question directly. "Why tempt ye God, to put a yoke upon the neck of the disciples, which neither our fathers nor we were able to bear?" (Acts 15:10). In the next breath he named the other way: "we believe that through the grace of the Lord Yahushua Messiah we shall be saved, even as they" (Acts 15:11). Peter set the yoke against the favor of Messiah as two answers to the same question — how a man is saved.

The yoke, then, is the Law made into the door: Torah-keeping set as the price of entry. No man has ever carried that, because no man enters by his own record. The modern pulpit reads the yoke as the commandments themselves and teaches that the Gentiles were set free from them. The council closed the same chapter by pointing the Gentiles to Moses, "read in the synagogues every sabbath day" (Acts 15:21). Peter removed the Law as the way in. He did not remove the Law from the walk that follows.
