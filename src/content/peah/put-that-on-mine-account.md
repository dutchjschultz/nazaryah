Paul makes Philemon an offer: "If he hath wronged thee, or oweth thee ought, put that on mine account" (Philemon 18). It is a picture of how a debt is actually settled. It also speaks to the common teaching that because sin's debt was dealt with at the tree, the Law that measured the debt was done away.

Three things follow. First, the wrong was real; Paul does not say it never mattered. Second, the debt was not torn up; it was moved to another account. Paul signs for it in the next verse: "I will repay it" (Philemon 19). Third, the standard that measured the debt still stood, or there would be nothing to repay.

Torah had already set that standard. The one who wronged another "shall even restore it in the principal, and shall add the fifth part more thereto" (Leviticus 6:5). Paul did not cancel that rule. He honored it by paying.

At the tree, it is Yahuah who redeems, and the price is His Son. Believers were "not redeemed with corruptible things, as silver and gold," but "with the precious blood of Messiah, as of a lamb without blemish and without spot" (1 Peter 1:18-19). The price was the highest ever paid, and it was paid because the debt was real.

Paying a debt honors the standard; it does not abolish it. Paul asks the obvious question himself: "Do we then make void the law through faith? God forbid: yea, we establish the law" (Romans 3:31). The debt was cancelled. The Law was not.
