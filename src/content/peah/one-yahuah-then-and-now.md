The book is "The burden of Nineveh" (Nahum 1:1), and it opens with a strong word: "God is jealous, and Yahuah revengeth" (Nahum 1:2). He "reserveth wrath for his enemies." It can be a hard verse to read. Many have been taught that this was the God of the Old Testament, and that the New Testament shows a softer God of love. Scripture shows one God all the way through.

The Torah said it first. Yahuah told Moses, "To me belongeth vengeance, and recompence" (Deuteronomy 32:35). And He gave the reason He does not drift from it: "I am Yahuah, I change not" (Malachi 3:6).

Yahushua did not show a different Father. He warned His own disciples to "fear him which is able to destroy both soul and body in hell" (Matthew 10:28). That is the same God Nahum describes.

Paul did not retire this God either. Writing to believers in Rome, he quoted the very same Torah line: "Vengeance is mine; I will repay" (Romans 12:19). Hebrews adds, "our God is a consuming fire" (Hebrews 12:29).

Someone may point out that the New Testament says "God is love" (1 John 4:8). It does. But Nahum says it too, in the same chapter. Yahuah is "slow to anger" (Nahum 1:3), and "Yahuah is good, a strong hold in the day of trouble" (Nahum 1:7). Love and vengeance were never split between two testaments. They sit side by side in one short chapter.

There are not two Gods, one angry and one kind. There is one Yahuah, patient with His people and just toward His enemies, from Nahum to the last page. Revelation praises Him for the same thing Nahum does: He "hath avenged the blood of his servants" (Revelation 19:2).
