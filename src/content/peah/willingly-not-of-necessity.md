Paul could have ordered Philemon to do what was right (Philemon 8). Instead he wanted his kindness "not be as it were of necessity, but willingly" (Philemon 14). Many read this as the New Testament's religion of the heart, set against a Law of forced religion. It is actually Torah's own pattern.

When Yahuah called for the materials of His dwelling, He said to take it "of every man that giveth it willingly with his heart" (Exodus 25:2). Moses repeated it: "whosoever is of a willing heart, let him bring it" (Exodus 35:5).

The Law itself was meant to live in the heart. Moses said of the commandment, "the word is very nigh unto thee, in thy mouth, and in thy heart, that thou mayest do it" (Deuteronomy 30:14). David sang, "I delight to do thy will, O my God: yea, thy law is within my heart" (Psalm 40:8).

The willing heart was in the Law from the start. Paul did not replace Torah with willingness. He asked for willingness the way Torah always had.

The Law was never meant to be kept grudgingly. It was meant to be kept by a heart that wants it. The closing invitation of Scripture is made the same way: "whosoever will, let him take the water of life freely" (Revelation 22:17).
