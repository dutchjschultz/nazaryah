The King James Version reads, "God was manifest in the flesh" (1 Timothy 3:16). Many pulpits treat it as a plain statement that God Himself became a man. The whole claim rests on one small word, and the oldest Greek copies do not agree with the King James on what that word is.

## One Stroke of the Pen

The oldest manuscripts were written in Greek capital letters, and sacred names were shortened. "Who" was written ΟΣ, *hos*. "God" was shortened to ΘΣ, *theos*, with a line drawn over the top. The difference between the two is a short bar inside the first letter and a line above it.

## What the Oldest Copies Read

Codex Sinaiticus, from the 300s, reads "who," and a corrector changed it centuries later. Codex Alexandrinus and Codex Ephraemi, both from the 400s, read "who" as well, and later hands altered both to "God." The old Latin Bible read "which," pointing back to the mystery. The Syriac, Coptic, Gothic, and Ethiopian translations read "who" or "which." The Greek copies that read "God" come later, mostly from the 800s onward, and that later majority is what the King James translators had in hand.

## Who the Verse Is About

Read with "who," the verse names six things about one person: manifest in the flesh, justified in the Spirit, seen of angels, preached unto the Gentiles, believed on in the world, received up into glory. Every line fits the Son. Two of them cannot fit Yahuah. No one justifies Yahuah, and Yahuah is not "received up into glory." He is the one who receives.

The same letter calls the Son "the man" who stands as mediator between God and men (1 Timothy 2:5), and says Yahuah is the one "whom no man hath seen, nor can see" (1 Timothy 6:16). Paul does not say the opposite in chapter 3 of what he says in chapters 2 and 6.

Moses foretold the same shape long before. Yahuah promised to raise up a Prophet "from among their brethren, like unto thee," and to put His words in that Prophet’s mouth (Deuteronomy 18:18). A man raised up and shown openly is the mystery Paul is speaking of.

## A Title With a History

"God manifest" was not a new phrase in Paul’s world. *Theos Epiphanes*, God Manifest, was the title Antiochus IV stamped on his coins, and the same kind of praise was given to Augustus. The older reading keeps that title out of the verse.

The one "received up into glory" is seen again in Revelation as the Lamb who comes and takes the book "out of the right hand of him that sat upon the throne" (Revelation 5:7). Two are standing in that scene, not one.
