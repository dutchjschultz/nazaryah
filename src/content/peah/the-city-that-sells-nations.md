Nahum calls Nineveh "the mistress of witchcrafts, that selleth nations through her whoredoms, and families through her witchcrafts" (Nahum 3:4). A city that sells people is an ugly picture, and it did not stay in Nahum's day. Many have been taught that Revelation's Babylon is one city still to be rebuilt in the desert. The prophets show the same harlot in more than one city.

The prophets saw the same figure in other cities. Babylon was "The lady of kingdoms" (Isaiah 47:5), condemned "for the multitude of thy sorceries" (Isaiah 47:9). Tyre, a trading city, would "sing as an harlot" (Isaiah 23:15). Nineveh, Babylon, and Tyre were three different cities with the same character.

Revelation describes that character again. "By thy sorceries were all nations deceived" (Revelation 18:23). Her cargo ends with "slaves, and souls of men" (Revelation 18:13). Nahum's harlot sold nations and families, and Revelation's harlot sells souls. It is the same trade.

Some say Revelation's Babylon must be a literal city because it is named. But Revelation itself writes on her forehead, "MYSTERY, BABYLON THE GREAT" (Revelation 17:5). A mystery is not a point on a map. The apostles also said the work was already going on in their own day: "the mystery of iniquity doth already work" (2 Thessalonians 2:7), and "even now are there many antichrists" (1 John 2:18).

The harlot is a pattern: a power that trades in people through deception. Each empire that deals in souls carries it again. The call has been the same in every age, and Revelation gives it plainly: "Come out of her, my people, that ye be not partakers of her sins" (Revelation 18:4).
