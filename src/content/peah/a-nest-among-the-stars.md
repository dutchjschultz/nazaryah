Yahuah tells Edom, "I have made thee small among the heathen," and "The pride of thine heart hath deceived thee." Edom had asked, "Who shall bring me down to the ground?" (Obadiah 1:2-3). Yahuah answers, "though thou set thy nest among the stars, thence will I bring thee down" (Obadiah 1:4). Edom never left the rocks of Seir. The stars were the height of its pride. The same words in Isaiah 14 are often read as the fall of an angel named Lucifer, but the prophets use them for nations of men.

Jeremiah repeats the charge against Edom: "though thou shouldest make thy nest as high as the eagle, I will bring thee down from thence" (Jeremiah 49:16).

Isaiah 14 says who it is about. It is a "proverb against the king of Babylon" (Isaiah 14:4). The one who said, "I will exalt my throne above the stars of God" (Isaiah 14:13) is later called "the man that made the earth to tremble" (Isaiah 14:16). A man, not an angel. Ezekiel tells the prince of Tyre the same: "yet thou art a man, and not God" (Ezekiel 28:2).

Yahushua used this language too. "Thou, Capernaum, which art exalted unto heaven, shalt be brought down to hell" (Matthew 11:23).

The usual escape is "son of the morning" (Isaiah 14:12), said to prove a heavenly being. It is a stolen title, a king dressing himself in borrowed light.

The conclusion is plain. Rising to the stars and falling from heaven is the language of human pride brought low. There is no fallen-angel Lucifer in Isaiah 14. The true title belongs to the Son alone: "the bright and morning star" (Revelation 22:16).
