Paul recalls his life "in time past in the Jews' religion," when he was "more exceedingly zealous of the traditions of my fathers" (Galatians 1:13-14). The word is Ioudaismos, the religion of the Jews as it stood in his day, and Paul names its heart: the traditions of the fathers. Many were taught that Paul left the Law when he met Messiah. He says he left the traditions.

Isaiah described that kind of religion: "their fear toward me is taught by the precept of men" (Isaiah 29:13).

Yahushua quoted Isaiah and drew the line: "laying aside the commandment of God, ye hold the tradition of men" (Mark 7:8). The commandment and the tradition are two different things.

Long after his calling, Paul told Festus, "Neither against the law of the Jews, neither against the temple ... have I offended any thing at all" (Acts 25:8). James sent him to the temple so that all would know "thou thyself also walkest orderly, and keepest the law" (Acts 21:24).

The usual escape is that Paul counted his old life "but loss" (Philippians 3:7). That list was his rank and his zeal as a Pharisee. Of the Law itself he wrote, "the law is holy, and the commandment holy, and just, and good" (Romans 7:12).

The conclusion is plain. Paul turned from the traditions of the fathers, not from the commandments of Yahuah. Revelation names the saints the same way, those "which keep the commandments of God" (Revelation 12:17).
