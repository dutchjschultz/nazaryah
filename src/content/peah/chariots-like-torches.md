Nahum sees "The chariots shall rage in the streets, they shall justle one against another in the broad ways: they shall seem like torches, they shall run like the lightnings" (Nahum 2:4). This verse is often read as a picture of cars on a modern highway. Nahum says where the chariots are. The shields are "made red," the "valiant men are in scarlet" (Nahum 2:3), and they rush "to the wall thereof" (Nahum 2:5). It is the battle for Nineveh's walls.

The prophet names his subject at the start: "The burden of Nineveh" (Nahum 1:1). Later he names it again: "Nineveh is laid waste" (Nahum 3:7). The chariots belong to that day.

Assyria had boasted of this very thing: "With the multitude of my chariots I am come up" (2 Kings 19:23). David had already answered it: "Some trust in chariots, and some in horses: but we will remember the name of Yahuah our God" (Psalm 20:7). The chariots Assyria trusted filled its own streets in panic.

Yahushua opened prophecy from Scripture itself. "Beginning at Moses and all the prophets, he expounded unto them in all the scriptures the things concerning himself" (Luke 24:27).

Peter gives the rule: "no prophecy of the scripture is of any private interpretation" (2 Peter 1:20). A prophecy means what its own words and setting say, not what a reader's day suggests.

The conclusion is plain. Nahum 2:4 is the fall of Nineveh, foretold and fulfilled. Prophecy is read from the page, not from the headlines. Revelation opens with the same care: "Blessed is he that readeth" (Revelation 1:3).
