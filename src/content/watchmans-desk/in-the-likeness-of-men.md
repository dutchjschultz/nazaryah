## The Crowd at Lystra

At Lystra, in the hill country of Asia Minor, Paul healed a man who had been crippled from birth. The crowd did not shout that a prophet had come. They shouted in their own tongue that "the gods are come down to us in the likeness of men" (Acts 14:11). The Greek phrase is *homoiōthentes anthrōpois*, made like to men. They named Barnabas Jupiter and Paul Mercurius, the Roman names for Zeus and Hermes, and the priest of Jupiter brought oxen and garlands to the gate to sacrifice to them (Acts 14:12-13).

Paul and Barnabas tore their clothes and ran in among the people, crying out, "We also are men of like passions with you" (Acts 14:15). They pointed the crowd away from the two men standing in front of them and toward the living God who made heaven and earth.

The crowd was not reaching for a strange new idea. It was reaching for the oldest idea in its world. This study lays out that record: what the nations believed about gods in human form, long before Bethlehem and in the centuries after it.

## Two Old Roads

The ancient record runs on two roads. On the first, a god comes down and takes a human form, often to father a child or to walk among men in disguise. On the second, a man is lifted up and declared a god, usually a king. The two roads end at the same place: a god the people can see, standing in a body, close enough to touch.

## Egypt: The God in the King’s Body

Egypt taught that the living Pharaoh was the god Horus on earth, and that at death he joined Osiris. The king was not merely blessed by a god. He was the god in the flesh.

Queen Hatshepsut, who ruled about 1479 to 1458 BC, carved the clearest example on the walls of her temple at Deir el-Bahri. The reliefs show the god Amun taking the form of her father, King Thutmose I, and coming to her mother, Queen Ahmose, in the palace. The inscription says the god made his form like the majesty of her husband. The child born of that visit, the reliefs claim, was the daughter of a god. Amenhotep III carved the same divine-birth scene at Luxor a few generations later.

This is the world many place Moses in. Israel came out of a land where a god was said to live inside the king’s body, and that same Pharaoh asked, "Who is Yahuah, that I should obey his voice?" (Exodus 5:2).

## Mesopotamia: The King Who Wrote God Before His Name

Around 2250 BC, Naram-Sin of Akkad had the sign for "god" written before his own name and had himself carved wearing the horned crown that belonged to the gods. A few centuries later, Shulgi of Ur did the same. The old Babylonian hero Gilgamesh was described as two-thirds god and one-third man.

Scripture shows the habit still alive in Daniel’s day. The officials under Darius had a decree signed that no man could ask a petition of any god or man for thirty days, except of the king (Daniel 6:7). The king was set in the place of every god.

## Greece: Gods in Disguise

Homer’s *Odyssey*, sung centuries before the time of Messiah, warns that the gods take on the look of strangers from far countries and wander the cities, watching who does right and who does wrong. A Greek host never knew whether the beggar at the door was a god.

The myths are full of it. Zeus took the form of a man named Amphitryon and came to his wife, Alcmene, to father Heracles. That is the same pattern carved at Deir el-Bahri: a god wearing a husband’s body. In Euripides’ play *The Bacchae*, staged in 405 BC, the god Dionysus walks on stage and tells the audience he has traded his divine form for a mortal one to walk the streets of Thebes.

One story sits closest to Lystra. The Roman poet Ovid, writing around AD 8, tells of Zeus and Hermes walking through Phrygia in human form, turned away at every door until an old couple named Baucis and Philemon took them in. Phrygia bordered Lycaonia, where Lystra stood, and Zeus and Hermes are the very two gods the Lystra crowd named. When the people saw a lame man walk, the story they already knew told them exactly who was standing in front of them.

## The Present God: Kings Made Gods

After Alexander the Great visited the oracle at Siwa in 331 BC, he was hailed as the son of Zeus-Ammon. His successors carried the idea further. When Demetrius the Besieger entered Athens around 291 BC, the city sang a hymn to him. Its words, preserved by the historian Duris, told Demetrius that the other gods were far away, or had no ears, or did not exist, or paid no attention, but that he was present, not made of wood or stone, but real. The people wanted a god they could see.

The Syrian king Antiochus IV, who defiled the temple in Jerusalem in 167 BC, stamped his coins with the title *Theos Epiphanes*, "God Manifest." A man called himself god made visible, and set his altar in the house of Yahuah.

## Rome: Son of God, Savior, and Good News

Rome declared Julius Caesar a god in 42 BC. His adopted son Octavian then took the title *divi filius*, son of the deified one, and put it on his coins. As Augustus, he was honored across Asia Minor in a decree of about 9 BC, found at Priene, which called him a savior sent by providence and named his birthday the beginning of the *euangelion*, the good news, for the world.

Later emperors pressed harder. The historian Suetonius records that Caligula appeared in public dressed as the gods and demanded worship, and that Domitian wished to be addressed as "Master and God." Every one of these titles, son of God, savior, good news, god manifest, was already on Roman coins and stones before and during the years the apostles preached.

## Four Times in One Book

The book of Acts records the nations reaching for the same idea again and again. In Samaria, the people said of Simon the sorcerer, "This man is the great power of God" (Acts 8:10). At Caesarea, the crowd cried that Herod’s speech was "the voice of a god, and not of a man," and he was struck down "because he gave not God the glory" (Acts 12:22-23). At Lystra they called Paul and Barnabas gods in the likeness of men (Acts 14:11). On Malta, when the viper did not kill Paul, the islanders "said that he was a god" (Acts 28:6).

Four times the nations offered a man the place of a god. Four times the apostles and the record refused it.

## What About Mithras?

Mithras is often named as the great pagan copy of the Messiah. The popular claims say he was born of a virgin on December 25, had twelve disciples, was crucified, and rose again. None of these claims has an ancient source behind it.

In old Persian religion, Mithra was a heavenly power of oaths and light, never a man. The Roman mysteries of Mithras, kept by soldiers and merchants in underground halls, appear from the late first century AD through the fourth. Their carvings show Mithras springing full-grown out of a rock, already wearing his cap. A third-century writer, Commodian, mocked the idea of a god born from a rock. Mithras had no human mother and no human life on earth. The December 25 feast in the Roman calendar of AD 354 belongs to Sol Invictus, the Unconquered Sun, not to Mithras.

Mithras is not the evidence. The evidence is Egypt, Greece, the Hellenistic kings, Rome, and the book of Acts, and it is far stronger than the Mithras story. A watchman does not need a weak witness when strong ones are standing in the record.

## The Church Walks Into the Pattern

Around AD 150, Justin Martyr wrote a defense of the believers to the emperor Antoninus Pius. In it he said that when believers teach the Word was born without a human father, crucified, raised, and taken up to heaven, they propose "nothing different" from what Rome already believed about the sons of Jupiter (First Apology 21). He named them: Hermes, Asclepius, Dionysus, Heracles, Perseus. In the next chapter he told the emperor that if believers say the Son was born of a virgin, Rome could accept that in common with what it believed about Perseus (First Apology 22). Justin also claimed the demons had copied the true story in advance, but his sales pitch to Rome was plain: this will sound familiar to you.

In AD 325, the council at Nicaea wrote that the Son "came down, was made flesh, was made man." The Greek verbs are *katelthonta*, *sarkōthenta*, *enanthrōpēsanta*. The crowd at Lystra would have understood every word. In AD 451, the council at Chalcedon declared the Son fully God and fully man in one person.

This is not only a Catholic inheritance. Protestant churches sing it every December in Charles Wesley’s 1739 hymn, which tells the congregation to see the Godhead veiled in flesh and to hail the incarnate Deity. "God came down as a man" is the shared confession of nearly every pulpit, Protestant, Pentecostal, Catholic, and Orthodox alike. It is also, word for word, the expectation of the pagan world.

## What Scripture Says About Yahuah

Scripture runs the other way at every step. Balaam, a foreign prophet, was made to say it plainly: "God is not a man" (Numbers 23:19). The Hebrew is *lo ish El*, El is not a man. Yahuah said it Himself through Hosea: "I am God, and not man" (Hosea 11:9).

At Horeb, Israel heard His voice but "saw no similitude" (Deuteronomy 4:12). The Hebrew word is *temunah*, form or shape. Moses told them why: so that they would not corrupt themselves by making "the likeness of male or female" (Deuteronomy 4:15-16). The nations made their gods in the shape of men. Israel was forbidden to.

Paul named the same sin. The nations "changed the glory of the uncorruptible God into an image made like to corruptible man" (Romans 1:23). He told the Athenians that the Godhead is not like anything shaped by man’s art or device (Acts 17:29). He wrote that Yahuah dwells in light no man can approach, "whom no man hath seen, nor can see" (1 Timothy 6:16). John wrote, "No man hath seen God at any time" (John 1:18), and he wrote it after thousands of people had seen Yahushua with their own eyes.

## What Scripture Says About the Son

Gabriel told Mary why the child would be called the Son of God: because the Holy Spirit would come upon her and the power of the Highest would overshadow her (Luke 1:35). The Son is called Son because of that conception, not because a god put on a body.

Peter called Him "a man approved of God" (Acts 2:22). Paul wrote that there is "one God, and one mediator between God and men, the man" Messiah Yahushua (1 Timothy 2:5). Yahushua described Himself as "a man that hath told you the truth, which I have heard of God" (John 8:40).

Scripture does show Yahuah at work in the Son, but in a way no pagan myth ever told. "The Father that dwelleth in me, he doeth the works," Yahushua said (John 14:10), and Paul wrote that "God was in" Messiah, reconciling the world to Himself (2 Corinthians 5:19). In the myths, a god takes over a man’s shape. In Scripture, the Father dwells in His Son and works through Him, and the Son remains a man who prays to his God (John 20:17).

## A Verse Worth Checking

The King James Version reads, "God was manifest in the flesh" (1 Timothy 3:16). The words "god manifest" are the very title Antiochus IV stamped on his coins and the very praise given to Augustus. Several of the oldest Greek manuscripts do not read *theos*, God, in that verse. They read *hos*, "who was manifest in the flesh," pointing back to the Messiah named in the verses before. The later reading put the pagan title into the text. The older reading speaks of a man who was shown openly, justified by the Spirit, and received up into glory.

## The Pattern and the Promise

The record is consistent from the Nile to Rome. The nations wanted a god who would come down and stand among them in a body they could see. Egypt carved it, Greece sang it, Athens hymned it, Rome minted it, and Lystra shouted it. The apostles tore their clothes at it.

Scripture’s promise runs the other direction. Yahuah does not shrink into the shape of a man. He raises His people up. At the end of the book, "the tabernacle of God is with men" (Revelation 21:3), the throne belongs to "God and of the Lamb" (Revelation 22:3), and His servants "shall see his face" (Revelation 22:4). The nations wanted their god brought down to them. Yahuah will bring His people up to Him.
