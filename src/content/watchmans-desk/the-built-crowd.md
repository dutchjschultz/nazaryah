<div class="wl-epigraph">
<p>"Thou shalt not follow a multitude to do evil; neither shalt thou speak in a cause to decline after many to wrest judgment."</p>
<p class="wl-epigraph-ref">Exodus 23:2</p>
</div>

Yahuah wrote this into His Law because He knew the crowd would be the test. The verse has two halves. The first forbids following the many. The second forbids speaking up in a cause just to lean with the many. This week showed both halves at work.

Four things came through the week, from voices that agree on almost nothing.

First, the watchdog group the Midas Project laid out some 1,400 paid campaigns, about 10,000 posts carrying tracked links, made to look like ordinary people speaking their minds. The posters were speaking in a cause, and the cause had a sponsor.

Second, an advocacy group paid for a poll of 250 people per state, and Axios ran a headline telling readers the crowd already agreed.

Third, the host sitting in on the Candace Owens program said a past election was won in part by memes. That is a crowd made by content, not conviction.

Fourth, independent journalist Steve Baker, who stood inside the Capitol on January 6, described otherwise good people doing stupid things because they were caught up in the moment, on both sides of the police line.

A bought post, a built poll, a meme, a mob. Each is the same thing: a multitude gathered first, with the reasons supplied afterward.

The standard cuts both ways. The voices exposing the bought crowds this week are gathering crowds of their own. One said plainly he no longer believes anything anyone says. That is not freedom from the multitude. It is a second multitude marching the other direction. The Law does not say follow the smaller crowd. It says the number of people is never the reason.

Mainstream Christianity carries the same weakness. Doctrine is defended by counting heads: most churches teach it, the councils settled it. The Trinity creed was voted into place at Nicaea in 325 and Constantinople in 381. A vote is a multitude. The Word never asks how many agree.

The pattern is old. The same city that cried "Hosanna" (Matthew 21:9) stood before Pilate days later, and Pilate, "willing to content the people" (Mark 15:15), gave them the cross. That crowd was built in a week.

The last crowd in Scripture is the largest: "all the world wondered after the beast" (Revelation 13:3). The question for this week is not which crowd is right. It is whether the reader can stand still when every crowd is moving.
