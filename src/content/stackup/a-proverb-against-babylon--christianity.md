## The Old Path

Isaiah tells Israel to "take up this proverb against the king of Babylon" (Isaiah 14:4). It is a taunt song over a dead king, whose pomp is "brought down to the grave" with worms for his bed (Isaiah 14:11). He had boasted, "I will exalt my throne above the stars of God… I will be like the most High" (Isaiah 14:13-14), and those who see his body ask, "Is this the man that made the earth to tremble?" (Isaiah 14:16). His title *heylel*, shining one, son of the dawn (Isaiah 14:12), was stolen, for the morning star belongs to the Son (Revelation 22:16; 2 Peter 1:19). Ezekiel's prince of Tyrus heard the same answer: "thou art a man, and not God" (Ezekiel 28:2). Scripture names the enemy by what he does, never by a title of light. *Satan* means adversary, a word even used of Yahuah's own messenger who stood against Balaam (Numbers 22:22), and the serpent is named by his craft, "more subtil than any beast of the field" (Genesis 3:1).

## What Christianity Teaches

The church teaches that Satan was once a great angel named Lucifer, the most beautiful of all created beings, who led worship in heaven. Pride rose in his heart, he tried to take God's throne, and he was cast down with a third of the angels. The church draws this story from Isaiah 14:12-15, "How art thou fallen from heaven, O Lucifer, son of the morning," and from Ezekiel 28:12-17, the "anointed cherub" in Eden.

The story grew in steps. The Greek Septuagint put *heylel* into Greek as *heōsphoros*, the dawn-bringer, the Greeks' name for the morning star. Early church writers such as Origen read Isaiah 14 as the devil's fall. Jerome's Latin Vulgate, around AD 400, rendered the word *lucifer*, the Latin name for the morning star. The King James translators in 1611 printed "Lucifer" with a capital letter as a proper name. John Milton's *Paradise Lost* in 1667 gave the fallen angel his speeches and his war in heaven, the story most Christians carry today.

## What Christianity Kept

The church kept the truth that there is an adversary at work against Yahuah's people, a liar and a murderer from the beginning (John 8:44). It kept the lesson that pride goes before a fall (Proverbs 16:18). It kept the right names in its Bible: the serpent, the devil, Satan, the deceiver (Revelation 12:9). It simply laid on top of them a name Scripture never gave.

## Where Christianity Falls

**It read past the name Isaiah wrote.** Isaiah names his subject in the first line: "the king of Babylon" (Isaiah 14:4). He never leaves it. The one he mocks has a grave, worms for a bed, and a corpse that men stare at and say, "Is this the man?" (Isaiah 14:11, 16). He is "cast out of thy grave like an abominable branch" and denied burial with other kings (Isaiah 14:19-20). Angels are not buried. Ezekiel likewise names a king, a city, and its trade, "the multitude of thy merchandise" (Ezekiel 28:16), and tells him, "thou art a man" (Ezekiel 28:2).

**It handed the Son's title to the enemy.** In the same Latin Bible that named the devil *lucifer*, Jerome used that very word in 2 Peter 1:19 for the day star that rises in the believer's heart. The Son says, "I Yahushua… am the root and the offspring of David, and the bright and morning star" (Revelation 22:16). The church took the title the Son wears and pinned it on the adversary. The enemy disguises himself "into an angel of light" (2 Corinthians 11:14). The church took the disguise and called it his true name.

**It confused the bearer with the source.** Yahuah is the light, and the Son carries that light as the morning star carries the dawn. He is "the brightness of his glory" (Hebrews 1:3), a shining that comes from another. The Lucifer story pictures two shining rivals in heaven and teaches readers that the light-bearer's title could belong to a creature. That muddle helps the Trinity. The light belongs to Yahuah, the Son bears it, and the enemy has none of his own.

## The Verdict

The church made a dead king of Babylon into an angel and gave him the Son's own title. The name Lucifer comes from a Greek star god, a Latin translation, and an English poem. Isaiah 14 is a proverb against a man.

Revelation settles who owns the title. The Son says, "I am the root and the offspring of David, and the bright and morning star," and the call goes out, "let him that is athirst come" (Revelation 22:16-17). The morning star was never the name of the one who fell.
