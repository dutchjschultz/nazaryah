## The Old Path

Before Sinai, before circumcision, before the knife on Moriah, Abram "believed in Yahuah; and he counted it to him for righteousness" (Genesis 15:6). To believe is *aman*, to lean your whole weight on something; it is the root of "amen." To count is *chashab*, a bookkeeper's word: righteousness was credited to an account that had not earned it. Abram had no written commandments, no covenant sign, no priesthood, and no tabernacle. He had a promise of a seed, and he leaned on it. Every pronoun in the chapter is singular: one God speaks, one man trusts, one God credits. The same word runs through the prophets: "Believe in Yahuah your God, so shall ye be established" (2 Chronicles 20:20), and "the just shall live by his faith" (Habakkuk 2:4). Hebrews lays "faith toward God" among the first stones (Hebrews 6:1), and Genesis had already set it in place.

## What Hinduism Teaches

Hinduism teaches several paths to release: the path of duty and ritual, the path of knowledge, and the path of devotion, called *bhakti*. Bhakti is loving, personal trust in one god. Most Hindus today walk this path. A family or a person chooses a god to serve above the others, often Krishna, Rama, Shiva, or a goddess, and that one is called the chosen god, the *ishta-devata*.

The Bhagavad Gita is the great book of bhakti. Near its end, Krishna tells the warrior Arjuna to set aside all other duties and take refuge in him alone, and he will free him from all sins (Bhagavad Gita 18:66). The devotion movement spread across India in the Middle Ages through poet-singers who taught that love for the god matters more than caste or ritual. Devotion is shown by singing, chanting the god's names, offerings before his image, and pilgrimage to his temples.

## What Hinduism Kept

Bhakti kept the truth that belief is personal. It is not a list of facts agreed to. It is trust and love given to someone, and that is closer to *aman* than a creed recited in church. Moses said the first command was to "love Yahuah thy God with all thine heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy might" (Deuteronomy 6:5).

It kept the idea of taking refuge. David wrote, "Yahuah is my rock, and my fortress… my God, my strength, in whom I will trust" (Psalm 18:2). Bhakti also kept the sense that devotion counts for more than birth or rank, which is true of Abraham's family (Galatians 3:7).

## Where Hinduism Falls

**Abram did not choose his God; his God chose him.** In bhakti a man picks the god he will love. Abram was serving other gods in his father's house (Joshua 24:2) when Yahuah spoke first: "Get thee out of thy country… unto a land that I will shew thee" (Genesis 12:1). Belief in Scripture is an answer to the one God who called, not a choice among many. Jeremiah mocked the other way: "according to the number of thy cities are thy gods, O Judah" (Jeremiah 2:28).

**Trust needs a word spoken.** Abram leaned his weight on a promise Yahuah made out loud. "Faith cometh by hearing, and hearing by the word of God" (Romans 10:17). Bhakti is love with no covenant promise to lean on. A man can love an image with all his heart, but the image has never promised him anything.

**Devotion is given to an idol.** The god of bhakti sits in a temple as an image, washed, dressed, and fed. Yahuah forbade it: "Thou shalt not make unto thee any graven image" (Exodus 20:4). The Psalm says of such gods, "They have mouths, but they speak not" (Psalm 115:5). Leaning on what cannot speak is not *aman*. It is leaning on air.

**It credits the devotee, not Yahuah.** In Genesis 15 the one doing the counting is Yahuah. In Hinduism the debt of karma is worked off by duty, knowledge, or devotion. There is no ledger kept by a Judge who credits righteousness to the one who did not earn it.

## The Verdict

Hinduism's path of devotion kept something real: belief is love and trust given to a person, not a creed recited. But bhakti gives that trust to a god the worshipper chose, sitting in an image, who never made a covenant promise. Abram leaned on the one God who called him and spoke to him. Love for the wrong god, however warm, is not the belief that was counted for righteousness.

Revelation shows John himself bowing to the wrong one, and the answer he received: "See thou do it not: I am thy fellowservant… worship God" (Revelation 22:9). Devotion is right. The only question is whom it is given to.
