## The Old Path

Before Aaron and his sons wore a single holy garment, Moses brought them to the door of the tabernacle "and wash them with water" (Exodus 29:4); only then were they clothed and anointed (Exodus 29:5-7). Nobody served until he was washed. On Yom Kippur the order held: "therefore shall he wash his flesh in water, and so put them on" (Leviticus 16:4). Torah is full of washings: the priests at the laver (Exodus 30:20), the leper (Leviticus 14:8-9), the one who touched the dead (Numbers 19:19), the Levites (Numbers 8:7). So Hebrews names "the doctrine of baptisms," plural, among the foundations (Hebrews 6:2), and calls the old rites "divers washings" by the same Greek word (Hebrews 9:10). One God commanded every washing in His own singular voice, and none of them named three. In Acts the believer was immersed "in the name of Yahushua Messiah for the remission of sins" (Acts 2:38), and then wore the garment: "as many of you as have been baptized into Messiah have put on Messiah" (Galatians 3:27).

## What Buddhism Teaches

Buddhism has no baptism and no washing that brings a person in. A person becomes a lay Buddhist by reciting the Three Refuges and the Five Precepts, with no water at all. But water appears in several rites, especially in Theravada lands such as Thailand.

After making merit, such as feeding the monks, laypeople often perform a water-pouring. While the monks chant, the giver slowly pours water from a small vessel into a bowl, dedicating the merit just made to dead relatives and to all beings. The water is then poured out on the ground, often at the foot of a tree, with the earth goddess called as witness. Monks also chant blessing verses over a bowl of water and sprinkle it on people, homes, and new vehicles for protection. At the Thai New Year, Songkran, people pour scented water over images of the Buddha and over the hands of their elders to show respect and receive a blessing.

## What Buddhism Kept

Buddhism kept very little of the doctrine of baptisms. It kept the old sense that water carries cleansing and blessing, and that a thing should be made ready before it is set apart. It kept respect poured out on elders, which Torah honors: "Thou shalt rise up before the hoary head, and honour the face of the old man" (Leviticus 19:32). Beyond that, it kept nothing of the washing that prepares a man to serve Yahuah, because it has no Yahuah to serve.

## Where Buddhism Falls

**The water is poured out, not washed in.** In Torah the priest himself went into the water. He was the one made clean, and then he was the one clothed. In the Buddhist rite the water is poured away from the giver, onto the ground, as a carrier of merit. Nobody is washed, nobody is clothed, and nobody is made ready to serve.

**It sends the water to the dead.** The merit-pouring is meant to help dead relatives. Scripture says, "the dead know not any thing, neither have they any more a reward" (Ecclesiastes 9:5). The garment of Exodus 29 was for living priests in this life. Water poured for the dead is a hope with nothing under it.

**It calls the earth to witness instead of its Maker.** The earth goddess is a creature, not a judge. Moses called heaven and earth to witness, but he called them against Israel for Yahuah, not in His place (Deuteronomy 30:19). The washings of Torah were witnessed by one God who commanded them, and every one was commanded in His name.

**Merit cannot be carried by water.** The whole rite rests on merit made by giving, then moved to others. Scripture knows no merit that a man can bank and send. Righteousness is credited by Yahuah to the one who believes (Genesis 15:6), and cleansing comes by "the blood of Yahushua Messiah his Son" (1 John 1:7), not by a bowl poured at the foot of a tree.

## The Verdict

Buddhism has water, but it has no baptism. Its water is poured outward to carry merit to the dead, sprinkled for protection, or poured over images. The water of Torah washed a living man so he could be clothed and serve one God. Water poured for the dead, under the eyes of an earth goddess, is the opposite of water before the garment.

Revelation shows who gives the water that matters, and how: "I will give unto him that is athirst of the fountain of the water of life freely" (Revelation 21:6). It is not poured out to earn merit. It is given freely by the One on the throne, to the living who come thirsty.
