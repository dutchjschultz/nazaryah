## The Old Path

Man was formed "of the dust of the ground" and became "a living soul" (Genesis 2:7), and to dust he returns (Genesis 3:19). The One who formed him from the dust can call him out of it. In the Song of Moses, before Israel ever entered the land, Yahuah said, "I kill, and I make alive," and in the same verse, "there is no god with me" (Deuteronomy 32:39). Hannah sang that He "bringeth down to the grave, and bringeth up" (1 Samuel 2:6). Job knew that "in my flesh shall I see God" (Job 19:26). Isaiah wrote that "the earth shall cast out the dead" (Isaiah 26:19), and Daniel saw "many of them that sleep in the dust of the earth" awake (Daniel 12:2). Until that day "the dead know not any thing" (Ecclesiastes 9:5). The resurrection of the dead is no late borrowing from Persia. It stands in the Law, and Hebrews names it among the first principles (Hebrews 6:1-2).

## What Hinduism Teaches

Hinduism teaches that every person is an *atman*, an eternal self that was never born and never dies. The Bhagavad Gita says the self is not killed when the body is killed (Gita 2:20), and it compares death to a man taking off worn-out clothes and putting on new ones: the self leaves a worn body and enters a new one (Gita 2:22). This wheel of birth, death, and rebirth is called *samsara*, and karma decides what body comes next, whether human, animal, or god.

The dead are usually cremated, and the ashes are often given to a river, the Ganges above all. Many Hindus believe that dying in the holy city of Varanasi brings release. That release, *moksha*, is the goal: to get off the wheel for good, whether by merging with the divine or by living forever in the presence of a chosen god.

## What Hinduism Kept

Hinduism kept very little here. It holds that death is not the end and that what a man does in this life bears on what comes after it. Scripture says the same. But the thing Hinduism says continues is the wrong thing, and the way it continues is the opposite of a resurrection.

## Where Hinduism Falls

**It makes man immortal, when only Yahuah is.** Paul wrote of the "only Potentate… Who only hath immortality" (1 Timothy 6:15-16). Man is dust that was given breath (Genesis 2:7), and "the soul that sinneth, it shall die" (Ezekiel 18:4). Life without end is not something man already has inside him. It is handed to him: "the gift of God is eternal life" (Romans 6:23). An eternal self that cannot die needs no gift and no Giver.

**It gives man many lives; Scripture gives him one.** "It is appointed unto men once to die, but after this the judgment" (Hebrews 9:27). Once, then judgment, not again and again. Job asked the question plainly: "If a man die, shall he live again?" His answer was not a new body next year but a long wait in the grave: "all the days of my appointed time will I wait, till my change come" (Job 14:14). Until then the dead "shall not awake, nor be raised out of their sleep" (Job 14:12).

**It seeks escape from the body; Scripture hopes for the body raised.** Moksha is freedom from ever having a body again. Job hoped to see God "in my flesh" (Job 19:26). Isaiah wrote, "together with my dead body shall they arise. Awake and sing, ye that dwell in dust" (Isaiah 26:19). Paul waited for "the redemption of our body" (Romans 8:23), when "this mortal must put on immortality" (1 Corinthians 15:53). The hope of Scripture is not getting out of the body. It is getting the body back, made new, by the hand of Yahuah.

## The Verdict

Hinduism replaced the resurrection with its opposite. In place of one life, one death, and one rising from the dust, it offers endless bodies and a hope of never having one again. It gives man the immortality that belongs to Yahuah alone and leaves no place for the God who says, "I kill, and I make alive." Rebirth is not resurrection. It is the wheel that Scripture never turns.

Revelation shows the end of every wheel and every grave in one line: "there shall be no more death" (Revelation 21:4). Not many deaths and many births, but death itself finished, and the dead raised to stay.
