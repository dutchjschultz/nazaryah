## The Old Path

Yahuah has only ever had one people. At the first Pesach the Law said, "One law shall be to him that is homeborn, and unto the stranger" (Exodus 12:49). The stranger who joins himself to Yahuah and keeps His Sabbaths is brought to His holy mountain, "for mine house shall be called an house of prayer for all people" (Isaiah 56:6-7). Paul pictures that one people as one olive tree. The wild branch is "graffed in" and partakes "of the root and fatness of the olive tree," and he is warned, "thou bearest not the root, but the root thee" (Romans 11:17-18). Gentiles in Messiah are no longer "aliens from the commonwealth of Israel" but "fellowcitizens with the saints" (Ephesians 2:12, 19). The door into that tree is the Seed, "which is Messiah," and "if ye be Messiah's, then are ye Abraham's seed" (Galatians 3:16, 29). One tree, one root, one law, entered by belief and not by birth.

## What Buddhism Teaches

Buddhism has no God and no chosen people. Its community is the sangha. In its first meaning the sangha is the order of monks and nuns who have left home, shaved their heads, and taken vows. Around them stand the lay men and women who support them. In Theravada Buddhism, the form found in Thailand, Sri Lanka, Burma, Laos, and Cambodia, a fully ordained monk keeps 227 rules. Laypeople keep five basic precepts, give food to the monks each morning, and gain merit by giving.

The path to nirvana is taught as open to all, but in practice the monk is the one walking it in earnest, and the layman hopes for a better rebirth. In Thailand many young men ordain for a short time, a few weeks or a rainy season, to make merit for their parents.

## What Buddhism Kept

Buddhism kept something true about birth. The Buddha received men and women into the order from every caste, and he taught that a man is not made noble by his birth but by his conduct. That stood against the caste system of his day, and on that point it agrees with the Word. Peter said, "God is no respecter of persons" (Acts 10:34).

## Where Buddhism Falls

**It splits the people in two.** Yahuah told all of Israel, not a class within it, "ye shall be unto me a kingdom of priests, and an holy nation" (Exodus 19:6). Peter said the same to every believer: "ye are a chosen generation, a royal priesthood" (1 Peter 2:9). Buddhism keeps one class in robes and another class feeding it. The monk walks the path and the layman pays for a share of it. Scripture has one people, all walking the same road under the same Law.

**It makes holiness a leaving of the world.** The monk leaves wife, children, and work behind. Scripture gave the Law to farmers, shepherds, husbands, and mothers, and it set the home at the center of teaching: "thou shalt teach them diligently unto thy children" (Deuteronomy 6:7). Paul warned of a teaching that would come "forbidding to marry, and commanding to abstain from meats" (1 Timothy 4:1-3). Holiness in Scripture is lived in the household, not escaped from it.

**It has no root at all.** A grafted branch lives because "the root" bears it (Romans 11:18). Buddhism has no God to be the root and no Seed to be the door. The Buddha is honored as a teacher who found a way, not as one sent by Yahuah. A community with no root is not a tree. It is a gathering of cut branches, each drawing on its own effort.

## The Verdict

Buddhism was right to refuse caste and wrong to build a new rank in its place. It sets the robed few above the many, makes holiness a matter of leaving home, and has no root for anyone to be grafted into. Yahuah's people are one household with one root, and every member of it is a priest.

Revelation gives that priesthood to every one the Lamb has washed. He "hath made us kings and priests unto God" (Revelation 1:6). Not a monk above a layman, not robes above the rest, but every one of the redeemed, "out of every kindred, and tongue, and people, and nation" (Revelation 5:9-10).
