## The Old Path

On the fourth day Yahuah set lights in the sky "for signs, and for seasons, and for days, and years" (Genesis 1:14). The seasons are *moadim*, appointed meetings, and Yahuah calls them "my feasts" (Leviticus 23:2). "He appointed the moon for seasons" (Psalm 104:19). The sun turns the year, and its first month is Abib (Deuteronomy 16:1; Exodus 12:2). The moon numbers the month from the new moon (Psalm 81:3). The Sabbaths fall on the eighth, fifteenth, twenty-second, and twenty-ninth of each month, and the day runs from dawn to dusk. The appointments still stand, kept "from one new moon to another, and from one sabbath to another" (Isaiah 66:23).

## What Judaism Teaches

The Jewish calendar used today is a calculated one. Tradition credits Hillel II with publishing its rules around AD 359, and its fixed form was settled over the centuries that followed. The month begins at a computed average moment of the new moon, called the *molad*. The year is kept close to the sun by a fixed cycle of nineteen years, with a thirteenth month added in seven of them.

The calculation also carries postponement rules that move days on purpose. Yom Kippur is never allowed to fall on a Friday or a Sunday, and the start of the seventh month is shifted to keep certain feasts off certain days of the week.

The Sabbath is kept every seventh day on the unbroken weekly cycle, from Friday evening to Saturday night, with every day counted from sundown. The first day of the seventh month is called Rosh Hashanah, the new year. Shavuot is counted fifty days from the second day of Passover, and it always lands on the sixth day of the third month. The month names in use, such as Nisan, Tishrei, and Tammuz, were taken up from the Babylonian calendar during the exile.

## What Judaism Kept

Judaism kept more of the calendar than any other religion. It still keeps the feasts by their Hebrew names and in their order: Pesach, Hag HaMatzot, Shavuot, Yom Teruah, Yom Kippur, Sukkot. It still counts months from the new moon and still adds a month so Pesach stays in spring. It never dropped the sun or the moon. The Jewish people carried those dates through two thousand years when the church threw them away.

## Where Judaism Falls

**It traded the sky for arithmetic.** Yahuah hung the clock in the heavens for every man to read (Genesis 1:14). A calculation fixed by men is a calendar of men. Torah never gave anyone the power to move a feast so it would not land on an awkward day. "These are the feasts of Yahuah… which ye shall proclaim in their seasons" (Leviticus 23:4). Their seasons, not a rabbinic convenience.

**It fixed the year by rule, not by the sun.** The nineteen-year cycle adds its month on schedule, whether or not the sun has reached its mark. Yahuah tied the first month to the sun's own witness and to the season of green ears (Deuteronomy 16:1). The Torah also says plainly that Aviv opens the year: "the first month of the year to you" (Exodus 12:2). Calling the seventh month the new year turns the Torah's count around.

**It kept the Sabbath on the Roman week.** Judaism keeps the seventh day on a cycle that runs without a break from month to month. That cycle lines up with Saturday, Saturn's day, and it counts the day from sundown, a reckoning learned in Babylon. Scripture ties the Sabbath to the moon, on the eighth, fifteenth, twenty-second, and twenty-ninth. The day is the light itself: "God called the light Day, and the darkness he called Night" (Genesis 1:5). The day opens at dawn and closes at dusk.

**It put Shavuot in the wrong month.** The count is seven Sabbaths and then fifty days (Leviticus 23:15-16). Counted rightly from the wave sheaf, Shavuot lands in the fourth month, in summer. A count that always stops on the sixth of the third month has dropped the seven Sabbaths and kept only the fifty days.

## The Verdict

Judaism kept the names and the feasts but lost the sky. Its dates come from a book of rules, its Sabbath from the Roman week, and its new year from Babylon. It is the closest of the five, and it still keeps the appointments on days Yahuah did not set.

Revelation shows the woman, the faithful people of Yahuah, "clothed with the sun, and the moon under her feet, and upon her head a crown of twelve stars" (Revelation 12:1). She needs no calculation to tell the time when the sky Yahuah made is wrapped around her.
