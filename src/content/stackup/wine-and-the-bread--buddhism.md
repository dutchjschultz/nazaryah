## The Old Path

Melchizedek met Abram with "bread and wine" (Genesis 14:18). The Law never separated the two: every burnt offering carried a meal offering and a drink offering beside it (Numbers 15:4-5). The night before He died, Yahushua gave His disciples both, the bread, "my body which is given for you," and the cup, "the new testament in my blood" (Luke 22:19-20). The cup is the blood and the entry, for "it is the blood that maketh an atonement for the soul" (Leviticus 17:11). The bread is the body and the walk, living "by every word that proceedeth out of the mouth of God" (Matthew 4:4). Paul joined them: "justified by his blood… saved by his life" (Romans 5:9-10). The cup starts a man. The bread finishes him.

## What Buddhism Teaches

Buddhism teaches that life is suffering, that suffering comes from craving, that craving can be ended, and that the way to end it is the Eightfold Path: right view, right intention, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, and right concentration. The goal is *nirvana*, a word meaning blown out, like a flame. When craving is blown out, rebirth stops.

There is no God to forgive in Buddhism, and no one to atone. The Dhammapada, one of its best-loved scriptures, says that by oneself evil is done and by oneself one is made pure, and that no one can purify another (Dhammapada 165). Lay Buddhists gather merit by giving food to monks, keeping precepts, and meditating, and the fifth precept forbids strong drink.

To be fair, not every school stands on effort alone. The Pure Land schools of East Asia teach trust in the vow of a Buddha named Amitabha to bring the faithful into his Pure Land. But even there, there is no blood and no atonement for sin.

## What Buddhism Kept

Buddhism saw the root of the trouble clearly. It calls the root craving, and Scripture agrees: "every man is tempted, when he is drawn away of his own lust, and enticed. Then when lust hath conceived, it bringeth forth sin: and sin, when it is finished, bringeth forth death" (James 1:14-15). Buddhism also kept a walk. Its precepts against killing, stealing, lying, adultery, and drunkenness follow much of the second tablet of the Law. In the language of the old pattern, Buddhism kept some of the bread.

## Where Buddhism Falls

**It says no one can purify another.** That is the one sentence the altar was built to answer. David prayed, "Purge me with hyssop, and I shall be clean: wash me, and I shall be whiter than snow" (Psalm 51:7). Hyssop was the branch dipped in the blood of the lamb and struck on the doorposts (Exodus 12:22). David did not purify himself. He asked Yahuah to do it with blood. "It is the blood that maketh an atonement for the soul" (Leviticus 17:11). Buddhism takes away the One who purifies and leaves a man to scrub his own heart.

**It walks a path with no door.** The Eightfold Path is a road a man walks by his own effort, start to finish. Scripture's road begins at a door: "I am the door: by me if any man enter in, he shall be saved" (John 10:9). The walk of the commandments is real, but it is walked after the entry by the blood, not as the means of getting in. Buddhism has some of the bread and none of the cup.

**Its goal is to be put out, not to live.** Nirvana is the flame blown out. The bread is life: "we shall be saved by his life" (Romans 5:10). The tree of life, the bread of the presence, the manna, and the meal offering all speak of a life fed and kept, not a self erased. Buddhism offers an end to suffering by an end to the sufferer. Yahuah offers life.

## The Verdict

Buddhism saw the disease and refused the Physician. It knows craving brings death, and it tells a man to cure himself by walking harder. With no Maker there is no blood, and with no blood there is no door. Its path, at its very best, is a walk that never had an entry and an end that is no life at all.

Revelation calls the reader to a table, not to an extinction: "Blessed are they which are called unto the marriage supper of the Lamb" (Revelation 19:9). A supper is bread and wine set by someone else, for guests who could not provide it themselves. No one there purified himself. They were called, and they came in.
