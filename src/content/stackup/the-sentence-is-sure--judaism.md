## The Old Path

When Yahuah told Abraham about Sodom, He said, "I will go down now, and see" (Genesis 18:21). Judgment in Scripture is a court case. The Hebrew *shaphat* means to judge, to decide a case, to pass sentence after a hearing. Abraham pleaded, "Shall not the Judge of all the earth do right?" (Genesis 18:25). The two men went on toward Sodom while Abraham "stood yet before Yahuah" (Genesis 18:22), and those two are named "two angels" (Genesis 19:1): one Judge, not three. Then the smoke of the plain went up "as the smoke of a furnace" (Genesis 19:28). Sodom was the demonstration, turned "into ashes" as "an ensample unto those that after should live ungodly" (2 Peter 2:6). Solomon wrote that "God shall bring every work into judgment" (Ecclesiastes 12:14), and Daniel saw the dead rise, "some to everlasting life, and some to shame and everlasting contempt" (Daniel 12:2). Hebrews names eternal judgment among the first principles (Hebrews 6:2). The sentence is sure, the day is set, and the Judge is one.

## What Judaism Teaches

Judaism places judgment at the center of its fall holy days. Yom Teruah, which Judaism calls Rosh Hashanah, opens a ten-day season known as the Days of Awe. Judaism teaches that on that day the books are opened and each person's verdict for the coming year is written, and that on Yom Kippur the verdict is sealed. Jews greet one another in that season with the wish to be written and sealed for a good year.

Since the Temple fell in 70 AD, there has been no sacrifice. Yom Kippur is kept with fasting, prayer, confession, and charity in place of blood. Traditional Judaism also holds a final judgment at the end of days, but the living practice of judgment is the yearly season.

## What Judaism Kept

Judaism kept the books. Daniel saw that "the judgment was set, and the books were opened" (Daniel 7:10), and Malachi wrote that "a book of remembrance was written before him" (Malachi 3:16). It kept Yahuah as Judge, and it kept the appointed times He gave, among them "a memorial of blowing of trumpets" (Leviticus 23:24) and the day of atonement (Leviticus 16). It takes judgment seriously and keeps it in front of the people every year, which is more than the church does.

## Where Judaism Falls

**It turns the final sentence into a yearly one.** Scripture speaks of one judgment, not one each autumn: "it is appointed unto men once to die, but after this the judgment" (Hebrews 9:27). Yahuah "hath appointed a day" (Acts 17:31), and Solomon wrote that "God shall judge the righteous and the wicked" (Ecclesiastes 3:17). A verdict written again every year is a review, not a sentence. Sodom was not given a new hearing the next autumn.

**It keeps the day of atonement without blood.** Leviticus 16 is a day of blood. The high priest carried blood inside the veil and sprinkled it "upon the mercy seat" (Leviticus 16:15), "for it is the blood that maketh an atonement for the soul" (Leviticus 17:11). "Without shedding of blood is no remission" (Hebrews 9:22). Prayer, fasting, and charity are good, but they were never the atonement. The Temple's fall did not cancel the need for blood. It showed that the blood had already been brought: Yahushua entered "by his own blood… once into the holy place, having obtained eternal redemption" (Hebrews 9:12).

**It refuses the Judge's appointed man.** Daniel saw "one like the Son of man" come before the Ancient of days, and "there was given him dominion" (Daniel 7:13-14). Yahushua said the same: the Father "hath given him authority to execute judgment also, because he is the Son of man" (John 5:27). The authority is given by Yahuah, who alone is God, to the man He ordained (Acts 17:31). Judaism reads Daniel 7 every year and does not see the man standing in it.

## The Verdict

Judaism kept the books, the Judge, and the holy days, and lost the blood and the man. It made the eternal judgment a yearly cycle and the day of atonement a day without atonement. The sentence is not rewritten each autumn. It is passed once, on the day Yahuah has appointed, by the man He raised from the dead, and the only covering in that court is the blood already carried in.

Revelation shows that one day of books: "the dead were judged out of those things which were written in the books, according to their works" (Revelation 20:12). One opening of the books, one sentence, and no second hearing.
