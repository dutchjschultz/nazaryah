## The Old Path

Man was formed "of the dust of the ground" and became "a living soul" (Genesis 2:7), and to dust he returns (Genesis 3:19). The One who formed him from the dust can call him out of it. In the Song of Moses, before Israel ever entered the land, Yahuah said, "I kill, and I make alive," and in the same verse, "there is no god with me" (Deuteronomy 32:39). Hannah sang that He "bringeth down to the grave, and bringeth up" (1 Samuel 2:6). Job knew that "in my flesh shall I see God" (Job 19:26). Isaiah wrote that "the earth shall cast out the dead" (Isaiah 26:19), and Daniel saw "many of them that sleep in the dust of the earth" awake (Daniel 12:2). Until that day "the dead know not any thing" (Ecclesiastes 9:5). The resurrection of the dead is no late borrowing from Persia. It stands in the Law, and Hebrews names it among the first principles (Hebrews 6:1-2).

## What Judaism Teaches

Traditional Judaism holds the resurrection of the dead as a fixed article of belief. The central prayer of the synagogue, said several times a day, praises God as the One who gives life to the dead. Many Jews seek burial on the Mount of Olives, facing the Temple Mount, in the expectation that the dead will rise there when the messiah comes. The resurrection is placed at the end of days, tied to a messianic age still waiting to arrive.

Alongside this, most Jewish teaching holds that the soul lives on apart from the body after death and receives reward or purging before the resurrection. Not all of Judaism kept the hope. In 1885 the Reform movement in America formally rejected the bodily resurrection, and its prayer books changed the wording of the blessing.

## What Judaism Kept

Judaism kept the hope that came straight out of the Law and the Prophets. The Pharisees held it against the Sadducees, "for the Sadducees say that there is no resurrection" (Acts 23:8). Paul stood on that same ground before the council: "of the hope and resurrection of the dead I am called in question" (Acts 23:6). Before Felix he said he believed "all things which are written in the law and in the prophets," and had hope "that there shall be a resurrection of the dead, both of the just and unjust" (Acts 24:14-15). On the resurrection itself, Paul and the synagogue started from the same verses.

## Where Judaism Falls

**It waits for a resurrection that has already begun.** David wrote, "neither wilt thou suffer thine Holy One to see corruption" (Psalm 16:10). Peter pointed out that David "is both dead and buried, and his sepulchre is with us unto this day," so David spoke of another: "This Yahushua hath God raised up, whereof we all are witnesses" (Acts 2:29, 32). Isaiah said the servant would be "cut off out of the land of the living," and then that "he shall prolong his days" (Isaiah 53:8, 10). A man cut off who then lives long has been raised. The firstfruits sheaf was waved before the harvest (Leviticus 23:10-11). Judaism looks for the harvest and refuses the sheaf.

**It keeps a soul alive beside the grave.** The Tanakh does not send the dead anywhere but the dust. "In death there is no remembrance of thee" (Psalm 6:5), and "there is no work, nor device, nor knowledge, nor wisdom, in the grave" (Ecclesiastes 9:10). Daniel's dead are asleep. A soul already enjoying its reward has little need of a resurrection. That teaching is Greek thought sitting in a Hebrew house.

**Part of it has let the hope go.** When the Reform movement dropped the bodily resurrection, it took the Sadducees' side of an old argument. Paul asked a Jewish king a question that still stands: "Why should it be thought a thing incredible with you, that God should raise the dead?" (Acts 26:8). The same Yahuah who formed Adam from the ground can call him out of it.

## The Verdict

Judaism kept the hope of the resurrection more faithfully than most of the world, and it kept it from its own Scriptures. But it holds the promise while refusing the proof. The resurrection did not wait for the end of days to begin. It began outside Jerusalem when Yahuah raised His Son, the firstfruits, and every later rising follows that one.

Revelation names Him in the opening greeting: "Yahushua Messiah, who is the faithful witness, and the first begotten of the dead" (Revelation 1:5). The firstborn from the dust has already come out. The rest of Israel's dead wait to follow Him.
