## A People of the Written Word {#written-word}

Scripture does not present Israel as a people who learned to write late. Writing appears at the very start of the nation. Before Sinai, at the battle with Amalek, Moses is told to put the record in writing.

> *“Write this for a memorial in a book”* — Exodus 17:14

At Sinai the tablets themselves were written. The text says plainly whose hand did the writing.

> *“And the tables were the work of Elohim, and the writing was the writing of Elohim, graven upon the tables.”* — Exodus 32:16

When Israel crossed into the land, the Law was to be written on plastered stones, and written so that anyone could read it.

> *“And thou shalt write upon the stones all the words of this law very plainly.”* — Deuteronomy 27:8

*Very plainly.* The Law was never meant to be locked in a code that only priests could read. It was written in common letters for a common people. That raises a fair question. What did those letters look like? The letters printed in a Hebrew Bible today are not the letters Moses wrote. The record of the ground, the stones, and the pottery tells how the script changed, and Scripture itself leaves footprints of that change.

## The First Alphabet: Proto-Sinaitic {#sinai}

Before the alphabet, the great nations wrote with hundreds of signs. Egypt used hieroglyphs. Babylon used wedge marks pressed into clay. Only trained scribes could master them. Then something new appeared: a short set of letters, each standing for one sound. Anyone could learn it.

The oldest examples are carved in the turquoise mining country of Serabit el-Khadim in the Sinai Peninsula, and on two rock faces at Wadi el-Hol in Egypt. They are called Proto-Sinaitic. Most scholars date them between about 1850 and 1550 BC. The Wadi el-Hol carvings point to the earlier end of that range, around 1850 BC, which places the beginning a little earlier than the seventeenth or eighteenth century often given.

These letters were written by Semitic-speaking workers living and laboring in Egypt and the Sinai. That is the same ground Israel lived on and walked across. History cannot prove who carved each mark, but the setting fits the Scripture account far better than the old claim that writing came late to the Hebrews.

Each letter was a simple picture of an everyday thing, and the letter took its sound from the first sound of that thing’s name. The ox head was *aleph*. The house was *bet*. Water was *mem*. The eye was *ayin*. The head was *resh*. The open hand was *kaph*. These pictures are well supported, and they explain why each Hebrew letter still carries a name instead of a bare sound.

## Proto-Canaanite: The Letters in the Land {#canaan}

The same picture-letters show up in the land of Canaan in short inscriptions on daggers, pottery, and small plaques. This form is called Proto-Canaanite. It is very close to Proto-Sinaitic, so close that many scholars treat the two as one early script known simply as Early Alphabetic.

The Canaanite examples begin earlier than the twelfth century. The first ones date to around the seventeenth century BC, and they continue down to roughly 1100 BC. During these centuries the letters were still settling. Some were written right to left, some left to right, some even top to bottom.

One small witness comes from the days of the judges. At Izbet Sartah, a village near Aphek in Israel’s hill country, a potsherd from around 1200 to 1100 BC carries an alphabet practice line, the kind of thing a student scratches out while learning letters. Ordinary Israelites were learning to write.

## Phoenician and Paleo-Hebrew: The Script of the Kings and Prophets {#kings}

By about 1050 BC the letters had settled into a fixed set of twenty-two, written right to left, made of lines instead of full pictures. Along the coast this form is called Phoenician. In the hill country of Israel and Judah it became what is now called Paleo-Hebrew, meaning old Hebrew. Phoenician, Paleo-Hebrew, Moabite, and early Aramaic were regional dialects of one shared alphabet, which is why the old Hebrew, Canaanite, and Phoenician writings look so much alike.

Paleo-Hebrew appears clearly by the tenth century BC in both kingdoms, Israel in the north with its capital at Samaria, and Judah in the south. This is the script of David’s and Solomon’s era, of Isaiah and Jeremiah, of the kings of Israel and Judah. The witnesses in the ground are many:

- *The Gezer Calendar* (tenth century BC), a farmer’s list of the months by their harvest work.

- *The Khirbet Qeiyafa ostracon* (around 1000 BC), an early inscription from a fortress overlooking the Valley of Elah, where David met Goliath. Its exact language is still debated.

- *The Tel Zayit stone* (tenth century BC), a full alphabet carved into a boulder in Judah.

- *The Mesha Stele* (around 840 BC), written by Mesha king of Moab, the same Mesha of 2 Kings 3:4–5. It names Omri king of Israel and boasts of taking the vessels of Yahuah. Its letters are nearly identical to Paleo-Hebrew.

- *The Samaria ostraca* (eighth century BC), shipping receipts for oil and wine from the northern capital.

- *The Siloam inscription* (around 700 BC), carved inside the water tunnel Hezekiah cut under Jerusalem (2 Kings 20:20). It records the moment the two digging crews broke through and met.

- *The Ketef Hinnom silver scrolls* (around 600 BC), two tiny rolled amulets found in a tomb outside Jerusalem. They carry the priestly blessing of Numbers 6:24–26, “Yahuah bless thee, and keep thee,” with the Name written out. They are the oldest known copy of any Scripture text.

- *The Lachish letters* (around 588 BC), notes from an army officer as Babylon closed in. They match Jeremiah 34:7, which names Lachish and Azekah as the last cities of Judah still standing.

These are the letters the prophets wrote with. Job longed for his words to be “graven with an iron pen and lead in the rock for ever” (Job 19:24). The Siloam tunnel, the Mesha Stele, and the silver scrolls are exactly that kind of writing, and they still speak.

One letter deserves special attention. In Paleo-Hebrew the last letter, *tav*, was written as a simple cross mark, like an X or a plus sign. When Ezekiel saw the man with the writer’s inkhorn, he was told to “set a mark upon the foreheads of the men that sigh and that cry for all the abominations” (Ezekiel 9:4). The Hebrew word for that mark is *tav* (H8420), the name of the letter itself. In Ezekiel’s day the mark on the forehead was the last letter of the alphabet, the letter of finishing and sealing.

## Babylon and the Square Letters {#babylon}

The letters in a modern Hebrew Bible are called square script. They are not Paleo-Hebrew. They are Aramaic letters borrowed and adapted to write Hebrew, and the change came through the captivity.

Aramaic was the language of the Arameans of Syria, which is why the King James Version calls it “Syrian.” Assyria, then Babylon, then Persia used it as the common language of government and trade across the empire. Even before the captivity, Hezekiah’s officials knew it as the language of diplomats, while the common people of Jerusalem did not.

> *“Speak, I pray thee, to thy servants in the Syrian language; for we understand it: and talk not with us in the Jews’ language in the ears of the people that are on the wall.”* — 2 Kings 18:26

After seventy years in Babylon, the returning exiles came home speaking the empire’s language. Scripture shows the shift in several places. Letters to and from the Persian court were written in Aramaic (Ezra 4:7). Whole sections of Scripture were written in Aramaic: Ezra 4:8 through 6:18 and 7:12–26, Daniel 2:4 through 7:28, and Jeremiah 10:11. When Ezra read the Law to the people, it had to be explained so they could follow it.

> *“So they read in the book in the law of Elohim distinctly, and gave the sense, and caused them to understand the reading.”* — Nehemiah 8:8

By Nehemiah’s time some children of mixed marriages “could not speak in the Jews’ language” at all (Nehemiah 13:24). The language had slipped, and the letters followed. Through the Persian period, from about 539 to 332 BC, scribes in Judah increasingly wrote Hebrew with Aramaic letters. By the third and second centuries BC that hand had grown into the distinct square script that is still used today. The change was gradual, not a single decree, and it came out of the exile, as the source notes rightly state.

Yahushua’s own words show which script was standard in His day.

> *“For verily I say unto you, Till heaven and earth pass, one jot or one tittle shall in no wise pass from the law, till all be fulfilled.”* — Matthew 5:18

The jot is the *yod*. In the square script it is the smallest letter, hardly more than a stroke. In Paleo-Hebrew the yod is a full-sized letter. The saying only works if the Law was being read in square letters. Yet Yahushua’s point is that not even the smallest stroke of the Law would fall away, whatever letters it was copied in. The Law did not change when the alphabet did.

## The Square Letters Are Not Kabbalah {#not-kabbalah}

Many readers assume the square letters are a rabbinic invention, a mystical alphabet that belongs to Kabbalah. The record says otherwise. The letters came first. The mysticism came long after and was laid on top of them.

The Dead Sea Scrolls settle the matter. They were copied between about 250 BC and AD 68, and most of them are written in square letters. The Great Isaiah Scroll, copied around 125 BC, is written in a square script that a modern Hebrew reader can still follow. These scrolls were in use while Yahushua walked the land. The jot He spoke of belongs to this script.

The books of Kabbalah were written centuries later, roughly between AD 200 and 1300. Kabbalah did not create the letters. It took letters already in use for hundreds of years and claimed hidden powers and secret meanings for them.

Two things are often mistaken for the Hebrew alphabet and should be kept apart from it. The first is the set of small crowns some scribes draw on certain letters in synagogue Torah scrolls. Mystical meanings attach to these crowns, but they are decoration, not letters. The second is Rashi script, a rounder style of letters used mainly to print rabbinic commentaries. Neither one is the square script of the scrolls.

The line is drawn between the letters and what men later laid on them. The square letters carried the Law in Yahushua’s day, and He counted their smallest stroke as part of that Law. The mysticism is the foreign addition. It can be set aside without setting aside the Hebrew text.

## The Samaritan Script: A Cousin, Not the Original {#samaritan}

The Samaritan script is often presented as the oldest biblical writing. The record does not support that claim. Samaritan letters are a descendant of Paleo-Hebrew, not the parent of it. They kept the old shapes while Judah moved to square letters, which is why Samaritan looks so close to the ancient script.

As a distinct style, Samaritan writing took shape late, most likely in the last centuries before Messiah or after, as the Samaritans worked to set themselves apart from Jerusalem. It was not in use when the Second Temple was rebuilt around 516 BC. The Samaritan Pentateuch is the Samaritan copy of the five books of Moses. Its text form goes back to the Second Temple period, but the oldest surviving Samaritan manuscripts date only from about the eleventh and twelfth centuries AD.

Scripture also explains who the Samaritans were. After Assyria carried off the northern kingdom, foreign peoples were settled in Samaria, and they “feared Yahuah, and served their own gods” (2 Kings 17:33). Their Torah was edited in places to make Mount Gerizim the chosen place of worship. Yahushua’s verdict on their worship was direct.

> *“Ye worship ye know not what: we know what we worship: for salvation is of the Jews.”* — John 4:22

The Samaritan script is valuable because it preserves the family likeness of the old letters. It is a cousin of the script Moses and the prophets used, and it should be read as a witness, not as the original.

## The Old Letters Did Not Die, and Neither Did the Name {#scrolls}

Even after square letters became normal, Paleo-Hebrew was kept for things counted holy. Among the Dead Sea Scrolls, a small number, around a dozen, were written entirely in the old letters, mostly books of the Torah, including a well-known Leviticus scroll. In many scrolls written in square letters, the scribe stopped and wrote the Name of Yahuah alone in Paleo-Hebrew, setting it apart from every other word on the page.

The same holds in the Greek. Some of the earliest Greek copies of the Hebrew Scriptures, made before Messiah, did not replace the Name with the Greek word for “Lord.” They wrote the four Hebrew letters of the Name right into the Greek text. The habit of hiding the Name behind a title came later. The modern English practice of printing LORD in capital letters is the end of that long road, not the beginning of it.

Paleo-Hebrew letters also appeared on the coins of Judah into the first and second centuries AD, during the two revolts against Rome. After the second revolt was crushed in AD 135, the old script passed out of Jewish use. Only the Samaritans kept their form of it.

## Letters Without Vowels {#masoretes}

Every stage traced so far wrote consonants only. Moses, Isaiah, Jeremiah, and the scribes of the scrolls wrote no vowel marks. The reader supplied the vowels from knowing the spoken language.

The dots and dashes now seen above and below Hebrew letters were added by scribes known as the Masoretes, roughly between AD 600 and 1000, many centuries after Yahushua. By then the Name was no longer spoken aloud, and the vowel marks placed on it were borrowed from the title *Adonai* to remind the reader to say “Lord” instead. European readers later pronounced those borrowed vowels with the consonants of the Name and produced “Jehovah,” a word no prophet ever spoke. The form many churches defend as the Name is a hybrid of the Name and the title that replaced it.

The Masoretes did not create the letters. The square letters they copied had already been in that form for about a thousand years, as the Dead Sea Scrolls show. What the Masoretes added were the vowel marks and the reading accents, nothing more.

## Where the Numbers Came From {#gematria}

Hebrew letters are also used as numbers. Aleph is 1, bet is 2, and the count runs up to tav, which is 400. Adding up the values of a word’s letters to find a hidden meaning is called gematria.

This number system is not as old as the alphabet. The older Israelite records, such as the Lachish and Arad letters, wrote numbers with Egyptian-style number signs, not with letters. Using letters as numbers was a Greek habit, since the Greeks counted with their own letters. Hebrew took up the same practice in the Greek era after Alexander. Hebrew letter-numbers appear on Jewish coins from the revolts against Rome, where they mark the year of the revolt.

The word gematria itself most likely comes from the Greek word *geometria*. The practice of adding up word values to draw out secret messages grew up in rabbinic and Kabbalah writing. Scripture never commands it and never uses it to teach. Revelation 13:18 calls the reader to count the number of the beast, but that is a single call to wisdom about one name, not a method for reading every word of Scripture. The meaning of a word is found in how Scripture uses it, not in the sum of its letters.

## From Aleph to A {#aleph-to-a}

The alphabet did not stay in the East. Around the eighth century BC the Greeks borrowed the Phoenician letters. *Aleph* became *alpha*. *Bet* became *beta*. The word “alphabet” itself is simply aleph-bet. The Romans took the letters from the Greek world, and English took them from the Romans.

Turn a capital A upside down and the ox head with its two horns is still there. Every reader of English is using letters whose family tree runs back through Greece and Phoenicia to the picture-letters of the Sinai. The same alphabet that carried the Law to Israel now carries the Scriptures to the nations.

## A Word on Letter Pictures {#letter-pictures}

The pictures behind the letter names are sound history and a fine teaching aid. They help a reader remember the letters and see the world the alphabet came from. The meaning of a Hebrew word, however, is settled by how Scripture uses that word from Genesis forward, not by stacking the pictures of its letters into a new sentence. The pictures open the door. Scripture decides what is in the room.

## The Line of the Letters in Brief {#timeline}

- *About 1850 to 1550 BC* — Proto-Sinaitic, the first alphabet, carved in Egypt and the Sinai.

- *About 1700 to 1100 BC* — Proto-Canaanite, the same early letters in the land of Canaan.

- *About 1050 BC* — The letters settle into twenty-two, written right to left. Phoenician on the coast, Paleo-Hebrew in Israel and Judah.

- *Tenth to sixth century BC* — Paleo-Hebrew is the script of the kingdoms and the prophets.

- *586 BC and after* — The captivity. Aramaic, the language of the empire, takes hold among the exiles.

- *About 539 to 200 BC* — Hebrew is written more and more with Aramaic letters, growing into the square script.

- *About 250 BC to AD 68* — The Dead Sea Scrolls are copied, most of them in square letters.

- *Days of Yahushua* — Square script is standard. Paleo-Hebrew survives for the Name, some Torah scrolls, and coins.

- *Last centuries BC onward* — Samaritan script takes shape as an offshoot of Paleo-Hebrew.

- *AD 135* — Paleo-Hebrew passes out of Jewish use.

- *About AD 200 to 1300* — The books of Kabbalah are written, laying mystical meanings over letters long in use.

- *About AD 600 to 1000* — The Masoretes add vowel marks to the consonant text.

## The Name Written on the Forehead {#forehead}

The letters changed shape many times. The Law written in them did not, and the Name of Yahuah was guarded through every change, even when men stopped speaking it. Ezekiel saw the *tav* written on the foreheads of the faithful in Jerusalem. John saw the same picture at the end of the story. The servants of Elohim are sealed in their foreheads (Revelation 7:3), and the company standing with the Lamb on Mount Sion bear “his Father’s name written in their foreheads” (Revelation 14:1). The first letters were carved in stone. The last writing in Scripture is the Father’s Name, written on His people.
